📊 Phase Diagrams — The Foundation of Metallurgy

A phase diagram maps the stable phases of a material at each temperature and composition. Every heat treatment decision starts here.

Fe-C (Iron-Carbon) Phase Diagram
The single most important diagram in metallurgy. Maps the relationship between temperature (up to ~1600°C) and carbon content (0-6.67%) for steel and cast iron.

Key phases:
• Ferrite (α) — BCC, soft, magnetic, max 0.022%C at 727°C
• Austenite (γ) — FCC, non-magnetic, max 2.14%C at 1147°C
• Cementite (Fe₃C) — 6.67%C, hard and brittle
• Pearlite — lamellar mix of ferrite + cementite (0.76%C)

Critical temperatures:
• A1 = 727°C — eutectoid (pearlite ↔ austenite)
• A3 = 727-912°C — ferrite disappears (depends on %C)
• Acm = austenite + cementite boundary
• 1147°C — eutectic (liquid → austenite + cementite)
▶ YouTube
Steel vs Cast Iron — The 2.14% Line
<2.14%C = Steel — forgeable, weldable, heat-treatable
>2.14%C = Cast Iron — castable, brittle (gray) or ductile (SG)

Hypo-eutectoid (<0.76%C): ferrite + pearlite. Softer, more ductile. Most structural steels (1018, 4130).
Eutectoid (0.76%C): 100% pearlite. Balanced strength/hardness.
Hyper-eutectoid (>0.76%C): pearlite + cementite network. Harder but brittle. Tool steels, bearings (52100).

Rule of thumb: more carbon → harder but less ductile and harder to weld.
Al-Cu Phase Diagram (Aluminum Alloys)
Explains precipitation hardening (aging) in 2xxx and 7xxx aluminum alloys.

Solvus line: the solubility limit of Cu in Al drops sharply with temperature — this is the key to age hardening:
1. Solution treat (~500°C) — dissolve all Cu into solid solution
2. Quench — trap Cu atoms in supersaturated solution
3. Age (natural or artificial at 120-190°C) — Cu precipitates form, blocking dislocations → strength increases

T6 temper: solution treated + artificially aged = peak strength
Over-aging: precipitates coarsen → strength decreases

Stainless Steel Phase Relationships
Schaeffler/DeLong diagram: predicts microstructure from Cr/Ni equivalents.

• Austenitic (304, 316): FCC, non-magnetic, cannot be hardened by heat treatment
• Ferritic (430): BCC, magnetic, no phase transformation
• Martensitic (410, 440C): hardenable, magnetic
• Duplex (2205): 50/50 austenite + ferrite — best of both worlds
• PH (17-4PH): martensitic + precipitation hardening

Sensitization (danger!): 450-850°C → Cr carbides form at grain boundaries → Cr-depleted zone → intergranular corrosion. Solution anneal at 1050°C+ to fix.

💎 Crystal Structure — Why Metals Behave Differently

The atomic arrangement determines everything: ductility, strength, slip systems, and why some metals are easy to form while others crack.

BCC — Body-Centered Cubic
Ferrite (α-Fe) Cr Mo W V

Atoms per unit cell: 2
Packing factor: 68%
Slip systems: 48 (but only 12 active at room temp)

Behavior: strong, less ductile than FCC. Has a ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) — a critical concern for cold-weather structures (ships, bridges, pipelines).

Key fact: ferritic steels become brittle below DBTT. This caused the Liberty Ships fractures in WWII.
▶ YouTube
FCC — Face-Centered Cubic
Austenite (γ-Fe) Al Cu Ni Au 304 SS

Atoms per unit cell: 4
Packing factor: 74%
Slip systems: 12 (all active at room temp)

Behavior: excellent ductility and formability. No DBTT — stays ductile even at cryogenic temperatures.

Why austenitic stainless steel is used for cryogenic: FCC structure doesn't become brittle at -196°C (LN₂). Ferritic/martensitic steels would shatter.
HCP — Hexagonal Close-Packed
Ti (α) Mg Zn Zr Co

Atoms per unit cell: 6
Packing factor: 74% (same as FCC)
Slip systems: 3 basal (very few!)

Behavior: limited ductility, strong texture dependence (anisotropic). Harder to form than FCC or BCC metals.

Why Mg is hard to form at room temp: only 3 slip systems. Needs 200-300°C to activate pyramidal slip → warm forming required. Same issue (less severe) for titanium.
Allotropic Transformations
Some metals change crystal structure with temperature. This is the entire basis of steel heat treatment:

Iron:
• Room temp → 912°C: BCC (ferrite)
• 912°C → 1394°C: FCC (austenite)
• 1394°C → 1538°C: BCC (delta ferrite)

Titanium:
• <882°C: HCP (alpha)
• >882°C: BCC (beta)

Why this matters: when austenite (FCC) is quenched fast enough, it can't transform back to ferrite — instead it forms martensite (BCT), which is the hardest phase in steel.
🔍 Grain Size — The Hall-Petch Relationship

Smaller grains = higher strength AND higher toughness. One of the few strengthening mechanisms that improves both simultaneously.

ASTM E112 — Grain Size Number
Formula: N = 2(G-1) grains per square inch at 100×

ASTM GAvg. diameter (μm)Typical use
1-3200-100As-cast, annealed slowly
4-690-45Normalized steel, general
7-932-16Q&T steel, fine-grained
10-1211-6Micro-alloyed, HSLA
13-144-3SPD, nanocrystalline
Higher G = finer grain = stronger. Defense/aerospace specs often require G ≥ 5.
▶ YouTube
Hall-Petch Equation
σ_y = σ₀ + k / √d
Where:
• σ_y = yield strength
• σ₀ = friction stress (lattice resistance)
• k = Hall-Petch slope (material constant)
• d = average grain diameter

Implication: halving the grain diameter increases yield strength by ~41% (√2 factor). This is why normalizing (air cool from austenite) produces finer grain and higher strength than slow furnace cooling (annealing).
Grain Refinement Methods
1. Micro-alloying: small additions of Nb, V, Ti pin grain boundaries → prevent grain growth during hot rolling. This is how HSLA steels achieve high strength without high carbon.

2. Controlled rolling: finish rolling below recrystallization temperature → pancaked austenite → more nucleation sites → finer ferrite grain.

3. Normalizing: heat to 30-50°C above A3, air cool → finer and more uniform grain than as-rolled.

4. Severe plastic deformation (SPD): ECAP, HPT → nanocrystalline grain (G > 14). Research/specialty.
Grain Growth — The Enemy
Problem: holding too long at high temperature → grains grow → strength drops.

When it happens:
• Welding HAZ (heat-affected zone) — the #1 concern
• Over-soaking during heat treatment
• Hot forging at excessive temperature

Prevention: micro-alloying (Nb, V, Ti), controlled soaking time, correct austenitizing temperature (not higher than needed).

⚠️ This is why welding thick sections of fine-grained steel (like HSLA) requires careful heat input control — you can undo the grain refinement in the HAZ.
📈 TTT / CCT Diagrams — Transformation Kinetics

Phase diagrams tell you what phases are stable. TTT/CCT diagrams tell you what phases you actually get at a given cooling rate.

TTT — Time-Temperature-Transformation
Also called "isothermal transformation diagram" or "C-curve" (because of its shape).

How to read it: start from austenite temperature, drop vertically to a holding temperature, read the time to start/finish transformation.

Regions (from top to bottom):
• Pearlite nose: ~540°C — fastest diffusion-based transformation
• Bainite bay: 250-540°C — needle-like, strong
• Ms line: ~300°C (for 0.4%C) — martensite start
• Mf line: ~200°C — martensite finish

To get martensite: must cool fast enough to miss the pearlite nose entirely.
▶ YouTube
CCT — Continuous Cooling Transformation
More practical than TTT because real cooling is continuous, not isothermal.

Reading CCT: overlay a cooling curve on the diagram. Where it crosses the transformation boundaries → that's the microstructure you get.

Slow cool (furnace): crosses pearlite region → soft, annealed
Medium cool (air/oil): may produce pearlite + bainite mix
Fast cool (water): misses nose → martensite

Critical cooling rate: the minimum cooling rate to get 100% martensite — depends heavily on alloy content. Plain carbon steel needs very fast water quench; 4340 can be oil-quenched (alloys shift the nose right).
Martensite — The Hardest Phase
What it is: a supersaturated solid solution of carbon in BCT (body-centered tetragonal) iron. Forms by diffusionless (shear) transformation from austenite.

Properties:
• Extremely hard (up to 65+ HRC for high-carbon steel)
• Very brittle in as-quenched state
• Must be tempered to gain usable toughness

Ms temperature depends on: carbon content and alloying. More C/alloy → lower Ms → risk of retained austenite (untransformed austenite trapped at room temperature).

💡 This is exactly why high-carbon tool steels (D2, M2) need sub-zero treatment (-80°C) — their Mf is below room temperature, so some austenite is retained after normal quenching.
▶ YouTube
Bainite — The Underappreciated Phase
Forms between pearlite and martensite temperature ranges.

Upper bainite (350-540°C): feathery, moderate strength, limited toughness
Lower bainite (250-350°C): acicular, high strength + good toughness — often better than tempered martensite!

Austempering: quench to bainite range and hold → all austenite transforms to bainite. Advantages: no quench cracks, less distortion, good strength+toughness without tempering.

ADI (Austempered Ductile Iron): same principle applied to ductile cast iron → transforms to "ausferrite" → exceptional properties for castings.
🌡️ Solidification — From Liquid to Solid

How metal freezes determines casting quality: porosity, segregation, dendrite arm spacing, and grain structure.

Dendritic Solidification
Most metals solidify as dendrites (tree-like crystals) growing into the liquid.

SDAS (Secondary Dendrite Arm Spacing): the key quality metric.
• Smaller SDAS → faster cooling → better mechanical properties
• Typical: sand cast 50-200μm, permanent mold 20-50μm, die cast 5-15μm

Between the dendrite arms: last liquid to solidify → enriched in solute → micro-segregation → potential porosity, hot tearing, and property variation.
▶ YouTube
Casting Defects — Metallurgical Root Causes
Shrinkage porosity: liquid metal contracts ~3-6% on solidifying. If feed metal can't reach the last-to-solidify zone → cavity. Solution: risers, directional solidification.

Gas porosity: dissolved gas (H₂ in Al, N₂ in steel) comes out of solution during solidification → round pores. Solution: degassing, vacuum casting.

Hot tearing: semi-solid metal pulled apart by thermal contraction before fully solid. Solution: avoid sharp corners, add fillets, adjust alloy composition.

Segregation: macro (center of ingot enriched) and micro (between dendrites). Homogenization anneal can fix micro, not macro.
🔎 Fractography — Reading the Fracture Surface

A fracture surface tells the story of how a part failed — the loading type, the material condition, and where the crack started. This is the detective work of metallurgy.

Ductile Fracture
Desirable in most cases

Visual clues:
• Gray, fibrous, "torn" appearance
• Significant plastic deformation (necking) before fracture
• Cup-and-cone fracture in tension specimens
• 45° shear lips at edges

Under SEM: dimples (micro-void coalescence) — the classic sign. Dimple shape indicates loading: equiaxed = tension, elongated = shear.

Meaning: the material absorbed energy before failing. The part was not operating far above its design capacity — it gave warning before breaking.
▶ YouTube
Brittle Fracture
Dangerous — no warning!

Visual clues:
• Bright, shiny, crystalline, flat surface
• Chevron marks (V-patterns) pointing back to origin
• Little or no plastic deformation
• No necking, no shear lips

Under SEM: cleavage facets (flat, smooth, with river marks) — trans-granular brittle fracture. Or smooth grain-boundary facets — inter-granular brittle fracture (corrosion, temper embrittlement, hydrogen).

Meaning: catastrophic. The material had no capacity to absorb energy. Causes: wrong material, operating below DBTT, hydrogen embrittlement, temper embrittlement.
Fatigue Fracture
The #1 cause of in-service failure

Visual clues:
• Beach marks (clamshell marks) — concentric arcs radiating from origin. Each mark represents a period of crack growth.
• Smooth initiation zone + rough final fracture zone
• Origin almost always at a stress concentrator: fillet, keyway, corrosion pit, machining mark

Under SEM: striations — microscopic lines, each one = one load cycle.

Key insight: beach marks tell you the crack was growing for a long time before final failure. NDT inspection at the right interval could have caught it.
Creep & Stress Rupture
High temperature service

Visual clues:
• Intergranular (along grain boundaries) cracks and voids
• Grain boundary sliding visible under microscope
• Elongated, necked appearance in creep rupture
• Oxide scale on fracture surface (exposed at high temp for a long time)

Meaning: the part was operating above ~0.4×Tm (absolute melting point) under sustained stress. Turbine blades, boiler tubes, furnace components.
⚠️ Failure Analysis — Systematic Investigation

When a part fails in service, the metallurgical investigation follows a structured process to find the root cause and prevent recurrence.

The 8-Step Failure Analysis Process
1. Preserve evidence — DO NOT clean or touch the fracture surface! Match fracture halves, photograph everything in-situ.
2. Collect history — service conditions, loading, time in service, maintenance records, similar failures.
3. Visual/macro examination — fracture origin, crack path, deformation, corrosion products.
4. Non-destructive testing — MPI/PT for other cracks, UT for internal defects.
5. Fractography (SEM) — ductile vs brittle, fatigue striations, intergranular/transgranular.
6. Metallography — cross-section, microstructure, grain size, inclusions, decarburization.
7. Chemical analysis — OES/XRF verify correct alloy. EDS on fracture surface for contaminants (Cl, S, P).
8. Mechanical testing — hardness profile, tensile test from adjacent material if available.

Hydrogen Embrittlement — A Special Danger
Delayed fracture — can happen hours/days after load!

Susceptible materials: high-strength steel (>1000 MPa / >32 HRC), especially martensitic.

Sources of hydrogen:
• Electroplating (especially Cd, Zn, hard Cr) — MOST COMMON
• Welding (moisture in electrode coating)
• Pickling / acid cleaning
• Service environment (sour gas / H₂S)

Prevention: bake out within 4 hours after plating (190°C, 8-24h per ASTM B849). Pre-plate stress relief. Material substitution below 32 HRC for critical parts.
Metallographic Examination — What to Look For
Etchant: 2-5% Nital (for carbon/alloy steel), Kalling's (for stainless), Keller's (for aluminum).

What the microstructure tells you:
• Pearlite spacing: fine = faster cooling (normalized), coarse = slow (annealed)
• Prior austenite grain size: reveals heat treatment temperature
• Decarburization: ferrite layer at surface = furnace atmosphere problem
• Banding: alternating layers of ferrite/pearlite = segregation from casting
• Inclusions: MnS (elongated, gray), Al₂O₃ (angular, dark) — stress risers for fatigue

Standard: ASTM E3 (preparation), ASTM E407 (etching), ASTM E112 (grain size).
