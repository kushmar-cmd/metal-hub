Ductile Iron

Cast iron with spheroidal (nodular) graphite

Typical values for the temper in the title

Ductile iron is formed by adding a small amount of magnesium to the molten iron, which makes graphite crystallize as spheres rather than flakes. Spheres do not act as "notches" like flakes do, so strength and ductility jump: it can elongate up to about 18% in soft grades, and exceed 800 MPa in strong grades.

It combines the castability of iron with properties close to steel, so it replaced cast and forged steel in many parts: crankshafts, suspension and agricultural machinery parts, water pipes and valves, gears and bearing housings.

Grades and conditions

In cast iron, the grade is chosen by minimum strength, tested on a cast test bar. In a thick section properties are lower than the test bar, because it cools more slowly.

Applications

  • Crankshafts and gears
  • Suspension and agricultural machinery parts
  • Water pipes and valves
  • Bearing housings and brackets
  • Pressurized pump bodies

Fabrication

Machining

Good, slightly less than gray iron.

Welding

Difficult: the high carbon forms hard, brittle zones near the weld. Repairs use nickel electrodes, preheat and slow cooling, in short runs.

Grades

Grade names are coded: in A536, Rm-yield-elongation in ksi and percent; in EN, Rm in MPa and elongation.

Common mistakes

Ordering "ductile iron" without a grade

Grades differ greatly in strength and ductility.

Confusing 80-55-06 with GJS-500-7

80-55-06 is closer to GJS-600-3 (Rm 552). Some sources pair it with 500-7.

Welding without a procedure

Hard, brittle zones form.
