Gray Cast Iron

Typical values for the temper in the title

Gray cast iron contains about 3–3.5% carbon and about 2% silicon. Most of the carbon is present as graphite flakes, which give the fracture its gray color, hence the name. The flakes weaken it in tension (it is brittle, with under 1% elongation), but give special advantages: excellent vibration damping, good thermal conductivity, easy machining, and low-cost casting of complex shapes.

In compression it is much stronger than in tension (roughly 3–4 times). So it is the classic material for machine-tool bases, engine blocks, brake discs and drums, pump bodies and covers.

Grades and conditions

In cast iron, the grade is chosen by minimum strength, tested on a cast test bar. In a thick section properties are lower than the test bar, because it cools more slowly.

Applications

  • Machine-tool bases and beds
  • Engine blocks and cylinder heads
  • Brake discs and drums
  • Pump and valve bodies
  • Covers and gearbox housings

Fabrication

Machining

Very good: graphite breaks the chip and acts as a lubricant. Usually machined dry. The black dust needs extraction.

Welding

Difficult: the high carbon forms hard, brittle zones near the weld. Repairs use nickel electrodes, preheat and slow cooling, in short runs.

Damping

Among the highest of engineering metals, so it suits precision machine bases.

Common mistakes

Using gray iron in a part under impact or tensile load

It is brittle. For such a part, ductile iron or steel.

Designing to a "yield strength"

Gray iron has no defined yield strength: it breaks with almost no plastic deformation. Design to tensile strength with a suitable safety factor.

Using test-bar properties in a thick section

Strength is lower in a thick section.
