0. The Drawing as a Contract

On the table lies a drawing of a single part: several views, dozens of dimensions, symbols, notes and a title block. Most people see it as a picture of the part. Someone who reads drawings at a high level sees something completely different: a contract.

In this course we will learn to read that contract all the way through: what it requires, what it allows, and what it does not say at all. Every drawing has at least three parties who read it. The designer writes in it what the part must do.

The manufacturer reads in it what he is allowed to make. And the inspector reads in it the basis for accepting or rejecting the part. If all three read the same drawing and understand the same thing, the drawing has done its job. If each one understands something different, the argument will

come in the end, usually when the parts are already finished. A part that meets every requirement on the drawing is a conforming part, even if it does not work in the assembly. And a part that exceeds a single requirement, even by a hundredth

of a millimeter, is a rejected part, even if it works perfectly. That sounds harsh, but it is the principle that lets a manufacturer on one side of the world make a part that will be assembled on the other. So when a conforming part does not work, the problem is

in the drawing, and the drawing is what gets corrected. And when a rejected part still has to be used, that is a formal decision by whoever is responsible for the design, not a decision of the shop floor. One of the basic rules in ASME Y14.5 is that the drawing

defines the part, without dictating how to make it. The drawing says that a hole must be ten millimeters in diameter, with a certain tolerance and at a certain location. It does not say whether to drill it, mill it or ream it.

The decision of how to make it belongs to the manufacturer. That is what lets him choose the cheapest and fastest process that still meets the requirements. There are exceptions: when the process itself determines the properties of the part, such as heat treatment, coating or welding, the drawing refers to a specification that defines it.

A good drawing reader asks about every requirement: does it define the part, or does it dictate a method to me? And a method is binding only when it is written on the drawing explicitly. One of the things that sets an advanced reader apart is that he also reads what is not written.

A dimension with no tolerance next to it is not a dimension without a tolerance. It takes the general tolerance from the title block or from a general note. All dimensions on the drawing apply at a temperature of twenty degrees Celsius, even if that is not written anywhere.

They apply to the part in its free state, without clamping, unless stated otherwise. And the standard named in the title block defines dozens of rules that are not drawn, and those rules differ from standard to standard. That is why exactly the same drawing can be interpreted differently under

ASME and under ISO, and we will see this in detail later in the course. Another basic rule: you do not measure dimensions from the picture itself. Even when the drawing looks accurately to scale, a dimension that is not written on it is not defined, and you must not fill it in with a ruler.

And if a dimension needed for manufacturing is missing, that is not an invitation to guess. It is a question for the designer. A good drawing defines every characteristic of the part once, in one way, and without contradictions. A dangerous drawing is not necessarily a drawing with an obvious mistake.

Often it is a drawing that can be read in two ways. A duplicated dimension, an undefined surface, a note that contradicts a dimension, or a tolerance that cannot be measured: each one is an opening for an argument. 1. Projection Systems

A mechanical part is three-dimensional, and the sheet is flat. Every drawing starts with one question: how do you transfer the part onto the sheet without losing any information? The answer is orthographic projection, and two different systems for arranging the views.

In this chapter we will understand both of them in depth, and see how confusing them produces a part that is the mirror image of what was designed. In orthographic projection, the lines of sight are parallel to each other and perpendicular to the projection plane.

So every view shows only two dimensions: width and height, or width and depth, and never all three. A surface parallel to the projection plane appears in its true size and shape. A surface perpendicular to the plane appears only as a line. And an inclined surface appears foreshortened: it looks smaller than it really is.

That is another reason not to measure from the picture: a length that appears on the sheet is not necessarily the true length. Imagine the part inside a glass box, with one view projected onto each of its six sides. When the box is unfolded onto the sheet, there are two ways to arrange the views,

and that is the whole difference between the two systems. In third-angle projection, common in the United States and in ASME standards, the projection plane is between the eye and the part. So each view is drawn on the side it is viewed from:

the top view above the front view, and the right- side view to its right. In first-angle projection, common in Europe and in ISO standards, the part is between the eye and the projection plane. Then each view is drawn on the opposite side:

the top view below the front view, and the right- side view to its left. systems. Only their position on the sheet is different. And someone who knows only one system will read the other one backwards, without noticing. To remove any doubt, the title block includes a symbol that defines the system.

The symbol is a truncated cone in two views: a side view, which looks like a trapezoid, and an end view, which looks like two circles. The two circles are the view from the narrow end of the cone. In third angle, the circles are drawn next to the narrow end, and the trapezoid points toward them.

the wide end, and the trapezoid points away from them. That is how you identify the system correctly, even if the symbol was drawn mirrored or in an unexpected place. Sometimes, instead of a symbol, the system is written out in words.

Suppose a first-angle drawing arrives, and it is read by someone used to third angle. The part is a plate with a tall wall along the back edge, and a hole near the front right corner. Sometimes the system is defined there, or in the standard the drawing refers to. Then check whether a 3D model is attached to the drawing, and compare against it. But do not rely on the views looking illogical. As we saw, the reversed interpretation is completely consistent.

And if there is still doubt, ask the designer. On a non-symmetrical part, a guess is a bet on the entire batch. The front view does not have to be the front of the part. It is the view that describes the shape best. It is chosen so that it has the fewest hidden lines,

2. Advanced Sections

A regular view shows the part from the outside. Everything inside appears as hidden lines. In a complex part, the hidden lines pile up on top of each other, and they cannot be read. The section view solves this: the part is cut by an imaginary plane, the near portion is removed, and we look inside.

In this chapter we will get to know the types of sections, their conventions, and what each section reveals and what it hides. Every section starts with the cutting-plane line, drawn in the adjacent view. It shows where the cutting plane passes, and the arrows at its ends show the viewing direction.

The letters at the ends identify the section, for example A, and the section itself is called section A-A. line. Without it, you do not know what you are looking at. The direction of the arrows determines what remains in the view:

the portion the eye sees, not the portion that was removed. A cut surface is marked with hatching: thin parallel lines, usually at 45 degrees. What lies behind the cutting plane is drawn with regular lines, and what is hidden is usually not drawn at all.

A single part has the same hatching in every section on the drawing. In an assembly, adjacent parts get hatching at a different angle or a different spacing, so it is clear where one part ends and the next begins. The hatch pattern does not define the material.

The material is defined in the title block or in the parts list. And a very thin part, such as a gasket or sheet metal, is sometimes shown solid filled instead of hatched. In a full section, the plane passes through the entire part, usually along the axis of symmetry.

On a symmetrical part you can save effort: a half section removes only a quarter of the part, and the view shows half cut and half from the outside. That way one picture shows both the external shape and the internal one. The boundary between the two halves is a center line,

not a solid line, because there is no real edge there. And on the uncut half, hidden lines are not drawn, because the cut half already shows them. Sometimes the important features do not lie in the same plane. In an offset section, the cutting-plane line changes direction

at right angles and passes through all the features. In the section itself everything is drawn as if it were cut in a single plane, and the bends in the cutting-plane line are not shown. So someone who reads the section without checking the

cutting-plane line may think all the features lie in one row. On a round part, such as a flange, the holes usually sit on a bolt circle, not opposite each other. In an aligned section, the cutting-plane line passes through the holes at an angle, and the inclined portion is then rotated into the plane.

That way the holes appear in the section at their true distance from the center. And this is an important point: such a section is not a true projection. It deliberately departs from the rules of projection to show what matters. A flange with three holes, 120 degrees apart,

will look in the section as if it had holes on both sides of the center. The number of holes and their angular position are read in the front view, not in the section. Some things are not hatched even when the plane passes through them. A rib or a thin web cut lengthwise is not hatched,

because hatching would give the impression of a solid, heavy part. nuts, pins, keys and balls are not sectioned lengthwise. They are drawn whole, even when the plane passes exactly through their axis. Someone who does not know this convention may think the

bolt was not cut at all, or that the rib does not exist. And on a part with ribs or spokes that are not on the cutting-plane line, they too are rotated into the plane, as in an aligned section. When only a small internal area needs to be shown, a broken-out section is used.

A small portion of the view is removed and bounded by a freehand break line. A revolved section shows the cross-sectional shape of a long feature, such as a spoke or an arm. It is rotated ninety degrees and drawn on the view itself. A removed section is the same idea, but it is drawn outside the view,

identified with letters, and sometimes at a larger scale. A section reveals the inside, but only where the plane passed. A hole outside the plane will not appear in the section. And since hidden lines are not drawn, it will not appear as a hidden line either.

So a section is never a complete picture. It is read together with the other views. 3. Conventions and Simplifications

A drawing does not try to be an accurate picture of the part. Accurately drawing every thread form, every gear tooth or every coil of a spring would take hours, and would add no information. So the drawing uses conventions: a shortened,

agreed representation that describes the feature without drawing it. In this chapter we will get to know the important conventions, and the principle behind all of them: when the picture is simplified, the information is in the callout. On an external thread, according to ISO, the major diameter is

drawn with a thick line and the root diameter with a thin line. In the end view, the root diameter is drawn as three quarters of a thin circle, so it will not be confused with a hole or a chamfer. A thick line marks the end of the full thread.

In ASME standards, in the simplified representation, the root diameter is sometimes shown as a hidden line, so you need to know which standard the drawing follows. In all cases, the picture does not define the thread. The callout defines it: type, diameter, pitch and tolerance class.

On an internal thread the picture is reversed: the tap drill diameter, which is the minor diameter, is drawn with a thick line, and the major diameter with a thin line. In a section, the hatching goes up to the thick line, that is, up to the wall of the hole. In a blind hole, both the thread depth and the drill

depth are drawn, and the drill depth is always deeper. And the drill point is drawn as a cone at 120 degrees, even if the actual drill angle is 118. The depth of the hole is measured to the end of the full diameter, not to the tip of the cone. assembly is drawn in section, the two threads meet.

the bolt is drawn in full, and it hides the internal thread. The internal thread appears only in the part of the hole where there is no bolt, and there the hatching again goes up to the thick line. Someone who does not know this may think the hole is not threaded to its full depth.

In the conventional representation, a gear is drawn without teeth: the addendum circle with a thick line, and the pitch circle with a thin center line. Sometimes one or two teeth are drawn, when the position of a tooth relative to another feature, such as a keyway or a hole, must be shown.

And in drawings produced from CAD systems, very often all the teeth are drawn. But even then, the tooth shape in the picture is only a picture. You do not measure from it, and it does not define the tooth. What defines the teeth is in a data table on the drawing: module,

Rolling bearings on an assembly drawing are sometimes drawn as a rectangle with a cross inside, according to ISO 8826. The bearing itself is defined by its catalog number in the parts list, not by the picture. A spring is also drawn simplified: a few coils at the ends, and the rest shown with center lines.

The spring is defined in a table: wire diameter, mean diameter, number of coils, free length, and sometimes a force at a given length. On a spring, the force at a given length is often more important than any geometric dimension. When the same feature repeats several times, it is drawn and defined once.

A callout such as 6X @10 means there are six identical holes. At the other locations only center lines are drawn, and sometimes not even the holes themselves. Knurling is drawn on only a small portion of the surface, and the type and pitch are defined in the callout. A small intersection between two features, such as a small hole entering a shaft, is sometimes drawn with straight lines instead of the exact curve.

The callout, the table and the note define it exactly. So when there is a gap between the picture and the callout, the callout usually governs, but such a gap is always a reason to ask. And someone who does not know a convention will see it as an

4. Dimensioning Philosophy

A dimension on a drawing is not just a number. It is an engineering decision. distance, from which point, and with which tolerance. Exactly the same part can receive two different dimensioning schemes, and end up as two parts that function differently.

In this chapter we will learn to read the intent behind the dimensions, not just the numbers. A functional dimension directly defines what matters for the function of the part. If two holes must match two pins on another part, the important distance is the distance between them.

So it is dimensioned directly, from hole to hole, and not each hole separately from the edge. A dimension chosen for manufacturing convenience can harm the function, even when all the numbers are within tolerance. The standard is aimed at functional dimensioning.

The manufacturing method is chosen by the manufacturer later. There are two main ways to arrange dimensions: as a chain, or from a single baseline. In chain dimensioning, each dimension starts where the previous one ended. With four chained dimensions, each with a tolerance of one tenth,

the position of the last feature can vary by up to four tenths. In baseline dimensioning, every feature is dimensioned from the same edge, and the tolerance does not accumulate. But there is a price: the distance between two adjacent features is

no longer controlled directly, and it can vary by up to two tenths. There is no single right way. The choice tells the reader which distance the designer wanted to keep tight. On drawings for CNC manufacturing you often see ordinate dimensioning:

one zero point, and each feature gets one number on each axis. This is really baseline dimensioning, with the same properties: no accumulation from the baseline, but the distance between two features is not controlled directly. a hole table, with a letter or a number for each hole.

and the tolerances apply to it like any other dimension. A redundant dimension is one that can be calculated from other dimensions on the drawing. A closed chain is the classic example: three partial dimensions, plus an overall dimension that covers all three.

A part can meet three of them and exceed the fourth, and then it is not clear which dimension governs. So a correct chain leaves one segment undimensioned, or marks one of the dimensions as a reference dimension.

And the segment left without a dimension is usually the least important one. That too is a statement by the designer. A reference dimension appears in parentheses, and sometimes with the note REF. It is for information only: it has no tolerance, and it is not inspected for acceptance of the part.

It is there to save the reader a calculation, for example an overall length. A common mistake is to inspect a reference dimension and reject a part because of it. The part is judged by the dimensions that define it. A basic dimension is enclosed in a frame.

It is a theoretically exact dimension: it has no tolerance of its own, and the general tolerance does not apply to it. The tolerance of the feature comes from its geometric feature control frame, for example a position tolerance.

Someone who applies the general tolerance from the title block to a basic dimension is inspecting the part against a requirement that does not exist. And a basic dimension with no geometric tolerance referring to it is an error on the drawing, because then the feature has no tolerance at all.

Instead of a dimension and a tolerance, both limits can be written directly, for example twenty and twenty point zero five. This is convenient for manufacturing and inspection, because no calculation is needed. And some dimensions have only one limit, marked MIN or MAX,

for example a maximum corner radius or a minimum thread depth. determined by the function or by other dimensions. ASME has a symbol called the dimension origin symbol: a small circle at the end of the dimension line, instead of an arrowhead.

It says which feature measurement starts from, and that changes the result when the surfaces are not perfectly parallel. Measuring from the wrong side can accept a part that should have been rejected, or reject a good part.

When a dimension is changed on the drawing without updating the picture, it is marked with a line under the number. The line says: the picture here does not match the number, and the number governs. And that is another reminder of the rule from chapter zero: do not measure from the picture.

The dimensioning scheme is where the designer tells you what matters to him. A direct dimension between two features says: this distance is critical. Dimensions from a single baseline say: this edge is the reference point of the part. And a dimension with a particularly tight tolerance says:

here the function is sensitive, and so is the cost. Someone who reads the drawing this way can plan manufacturing and inspection that protect what really matters. Let's take an example: a plate with a central hole for a shaft, and two holes for bolts.

The designer dimensioned the bolt holes from the central hole, because they must match a flange that sits on the shaft. The manufacturer would prefer dimensions from the edge, because that is where it is convenient for him to fixture and measure.

If the manufacturer converts the dimensions to the edge on his own, the tolerances accumulate, and the distance to the central hole can be exceeded even when all of his dimensions are within tolerance. And if he still wants to work from the edge, he must tighten his

tolerances so that the designer's original dimensions are maintained. That is the principle: you may manufacture any way you like, but inspection is done to the dimensions on the drawing. A dimension is an engineering decision, not just a number.

A chain accumulates tolerances, a baseline holds each feature relative to the edge, and a direct dimension holds the distance between two features. A redundant dimension creates a contradiction, a reference dimension is not inspected, and a basic dimension gets its tolerance only from the feature control frame.

5. Default Tolerances

Most dimensions on a drawing are written without a tolerance next to them. That does not mean they have no tolerance. It means the tolerance is written somewhere else. Default tolerances govern most of the part's geometry, and most readers never look at them at all.

A dimension with its own tolerance, a reference dimension and a basic dimension do not receive the general tolerance. And if the drawing has no general tolerance at all, dimensions without a tolerance are undefined, and that is a question for the designer.

On drawings to ISO, the general tolerance is usually defined by reference to ISO 2768. Part one of the standard sets tolerances for linear and angular dimensions, in four classes: fine, medium, coarse and very coarse, marked f, m, c and v. The tolerance is not fixed. It grows with the dimension.

In the medium class, a dimension of 20 mm gets +0.2, and a dimension of 200 mm gets +0.5. without checking which range it falls in on the table. the shorter leg, not according to the size of the angle. ISO 2768 also sets tolerances for external radii and chamfer heights:

Part two of ISO 2768 set general geometric tolerances: straightness, flatness, perpendicularity and symmetry, in three classes: H, K and L.

A callout such as ISO 2768-mK means: medium class for dimensions, and class K for geometry. In 2021 this second part was replaced by a new standard, ISO 22081, so on drawings you can meet both callouts. Someone who receives a drawing with mK needs to know that the perpendicularity and

flatness of the part are also limited, even though there is no symbol on them. ISO 22081 works differently from part two of ISO 2768. Instead of a table by tolerance type, it usually defines a general profile of a surface tolerance, relative to a datum reference frame that appears in the title block.

So when you see a reference to ISO 22081, look in the title block for both the tolerance value and the datums it refers to. On drawings to ASME, and especially on inch drawings, there is a different convention. The title block defines the tolerance by the number of decimal places.

For example, one place: +0.1. Two places: +0.01. And three places: +0.005. So the same number, written two ways, gets two different tolerances: 1.5 versus 1.500 are not the same requirement. These values are not part of the standard itself. Each company sets them in

its own title block, so always read the title block of the specific drawing. And someone who adds or removes a trailing zero on a dimension changes its tolerance. A typical ASME title block also includes a tolerance for angles, for example +0.5°, and sometimes a note about breaking sharp edges.

And there is a modern trap: a drawing produced from a CAD system, with three decimal places on every dimension. If the title block sets the tolerance by the number of decimal places, every such dimension suddenly gets the tightest tolerance.

That is usually not the designer's intent, but as far as the drawing is concerned it is the requirement, so you ask. General tolerances also apply to what is not dimensioned at all. A right angle drawn without a dimension gets the general angular tolerance.

And holes drawn on the same center line, or features that look symmetrical, have that relationship only within the general tolerances, not exactly. In other words: what looks aligned or perpendicular on the drawing is aligned and perpendicular only within the general tolerance.

There is a principle in ISO 2768 worth knowing. According to the standard, a part that exceeds a general tolerance is not automatically rejected if its function is not impaired. But that is not a decision for whoever is measuring. As we saw in chapter zero,

the decision on a nonconforming part belongs to whoever is responsible for the design. ASME Y14.5 has no similar clause. A dimension out of tolerance is out of tolerance. The general tolerance is chosen according to the normal accuracy of the shop, so that most dimensions will be achieved without special effort.

A general tolerance that is too tight makes the whole part more expensive, because every dimension becomes a requirement. And a general tolerance that is too loose hides a risk: an important dimension that was left without its own tolerance gets a tolerance that does not suit it.

So when you read a drawing, ask about every important dimension: is the general tolerance really enough for it? A dimension without a tolerance takes the general tolerance from the title block. In ISO 2768 the tolerance depends on the size of the dimension,

and the callout mK also limits the geometry. On inch drawings, the number of decimal places is part of the requirement. And what looks perpendicular or aligned on the drawing is limited only by the general tolerance. In the next chapter we reach one of the deepest differences between ASME and ISO:

6. Envelope versus Independency

Suppose a shaft is dimensioned with a diameter of twenty, plus or minus five hundredths. The shaft is inspected, and every measurement is between twenty minus five hundredths and twenty plus five hundredths. But the shaft is slightly bent. Is it acceptable? The answer depends on the standard in the title block.

Under ASME it may be rejected, and under ISO it may be accepted. In this chapter we will understand why, and what it changes in inspection and in assembly. ASME Y14.5 has a rule called Rule #1: the envelope principle. The rule says that the size limits of a feature govern

9. The Datum Reference Frame

In the third part of the course we will read geometric tolerances within the drawing, not each symbol on its own. And we start from the datum reference frame, because every location or orientation tolerance is measured relative to it.

The datum reference frame is the instruction for how to hold the part during inspection, and it is also the designer's statement of how the part sits in the assembly. In this chapter we will learn to read it: what each letter says, why the order matters, and what it reveals about the function.

A part floating in space can move in six ways: three translations along the axes, and three rotations about them. These are the six degrees of freedom of the part. To measure the location of a hole, the part must first be locked,

so that none of these degrees changes during measurement. The datum reference frame does exactly that: it defines which features of the part lock it, and in which order. Without a datum reference frame, a position tolerance is a number with no starting point.

So a feature control frame with no datum letters limits only form, not location. The first letter in the frame is the primary datum. The part rests on it, touching at least three points. This locks three degrees of freedom: one translation and two rotations.

The second letter is the secondary datum. The part is pushed against it at a minimum of two points, and two more degrees are locked. The third letter is the tertiary datum. One point is enough, and it locks the last degree. Three, two, one: this is the principle on which every datum reference frame is built.

In inspection it looks like this: the part is placed on the surface plate, pushed against an angle plate, and pressed against a stop. And the measurement itself is taken from the coordinate system that was created, not from the edges of the part.

The order of the letters is not a matter of convenience. If the primary datum is A, the part sits fully on A, and only then is pushed against B. If the primary datum is B, the part sits on B, and surface A aligns only as far as it can. When the angle between the two surfaces is not exactly ninety degrees,

the two orders give different coordinate systems, and therefore different results. A hole can pass inspection in one order, and fail in the other. So the letters are read from left to right, exactly in the order they are written, and the order is not changed.

There are three concepts worth separating. The datum feature is the real surface on the part, with all its irregularities. The triangle with the letter is attached to it. The datum is a theoretically perfect plane, or a perfect axis, from which measurement is taken.

And between them stands the datum feature simulator: the surface plate, the angle plate or the precision pin, which actually contact the part. Measurement is taken from the simulator, because it is what the part really meets, both in inspection and in assembly.

So a rough or unstable surface chosen as the primary datum will give results that change from one inspection to the next. A good datum reference frame reflects the assembly. The primary datum is usually the surface the part sits

on in the assembly, the largest and most stable surface. The secondary and tertiary datums are usually the features that locate the part: a central bore, a pin, or a wall it is pushed against. When you read a drawing, ask: are the datum features that

were chosen the features that really contact the other parts? If so, inspection will simulate the assembly. If not, a part that passes inspection may not fit in the assembly. And when the datum reference frame does not look functional,

that is a reason to ask the designer, not to change it. On a cast or forged part, the raw surfaces are not true enough to serve as a full datum surface. So datum targets are used: points, lines or small areas defined on the surface. They are shown in a circle divided in two: the size of the area on top,

and the letter and number below, for example A1. Three targets define the primary datum, two the secondary, and one the tertiary, exactly as before. That way both the first raw part and the machined part are inspected from the same targets, and the transition between the processes is preserved.

A datum feature can also be a feature of size, such as a hole or a shaft. Then the simulator is a pin or a ring, and the datum is their axis. When there is no modifier next to the letter in the frame, the simulator adjusts itself to the actual size of the hole, and there is no play.

When the letter is followed by M in a circle, the simulator is a pin of fixed size, at the maximum material boundary. A hole larger than that boundary leaves play, and the part can move slightly on the pin. This is called datum shift, and it is allowed.

That is exactly what happens in an assembly with a pin, so it also makes manufacturing easier without harming the function. When you read a feature control frame, you can reconstruct the entire inspection from it. The letters say what the part is placed on and in which order,

and the modifiers say whether there is play. A common trap is to inspect all the dimensions from the same frame, when different feature control frames on the drawing refer to different datum reference frames. Another trap is to fixture the part for manufacturing

on other features, and then be surprised at inspection. You may manufacture in any fixture, but someone who fixtures the part according to the drawing's datum reference frame reduces the risk from the start. The datum reference frame locks the six degrees of freedom, by the three-two-one method.

The order of the letters determines the result, and it is read exactly as written. Measurement is taken from the simulator, so a primary datum must be stable and functional. 10. Geometric Tolerances and Dimensions Together

In the previous chapter we read the datum reference frame. Now we will read how geometric tolerances and dimensions work together, on the same feature. location also controls orientation and form, and orientation also controls form. The welding symbol is one of the densest symbols on a drawing. One small line encodes the weld type, size, length, side and process. It is also one of the easiest symbols to read backwards, because there are two main standards, and they do not read the side the same way.

ISO system B works with a single line, like the American standard. So when you see a double line, it is an ISO system A drawing, and you read by the solid line, not by above and below. Someone who reads such a drawing with American eyes will weld on the wrong side.

In the American standard, the number to the left of the triangle is the leg size of the fillet weld. In ISO a letter must appear before the number: a for throat thickness, or z for leg length. Someone who welds a five-millimeter leg where a5 is written produces a weld about thirty percent weaker than designed. And a number without a letter on an ISO drawing is a question for the designer, not a guess. The weld length is written to the right of the symbol.

With no length, the weld runs the full length of the joint. For intermittent welds, in the American standard, a length and a pitch are written: 50-150, meaning segments fifty millimeters long, every hundred and fifty, center to center. In ISO the notation is different: the number of segments,

13. Material, Heat Treatment and Coating

Two parts can be identical in dimensions, and completely different in strength, hardness and durability. The difference is written on the drawing, but not in the picture: in the title block and in the notes. The material, its condition, the heat treatment and the coating

are part of the definition of the part, just like the dimensions. In this chapter we will learn to read all these definitions, and in which order they apply. The material usually appears in the title block, in the material field. It is written to a particular standard: for example 1.4404

under the European standard, or 316L under the American system. Often a product standard also appears, which defines the form of the material and its properties, for example plate, bar or tube. In many alloys, the temper is part of the material. Aluminum 6061 in the T6

temper is very different from the same aluminum in the annealed condition. And T651 says something more: the material was stretched to relieve stress, and that affects distortion during machining. Someone who substitutes a material that looks equivalent to him, without approval,

changes the part. An equivalent between standards is close, not identical. And a material certificate is usually required too, for example to EN 10204 type 3.1, proving that the actual material is the material on the drawing. Heat treatment appears in a note, and defines what the part must

When only part of the surface is hardened, the area is marked on the drawing with a thick chain line, according to ISO 15787. And the location where the hardness is tested is usually marked as well, because on a large part the hardness is not uniform.

A hardness requirement with no test location is incomplete, because every point on the part can give a different result. There are several hardness scales, and each is measured by a different method. Rockwell C for hard steels, Brinell for softer materials and castings,

and Vickers for thin layers and laboratory measurements. There are conversion tables between the scales, but the conversion is approximate. So when the drawing requires a particular scale, you measure on that scale. enough for acceptance, unless otherwise approved.

The coating too is defined by a specification, not just by a name. Anodizing, for example, can be decorative or hard, dyed or clear, and each one has a different thickness and different properties. So the note usually includes the standard, the type, the class and the thickness.

For zinc plating to ISO 2081, for example, the definition includes the coating thickness and the type of post-treatment. And on stainless steels, a passivation note refers to a standard such as ASTM A967. This is not a coating, but a chemical treatment of the natural passive layer.

So it is important to know whether the dimensions on the drawing apply before or after coating. This is usually written in a note, and when it is not written, you ask. Areas that must not be coated, such as threads or fit surfaces, are marked on the drawing as areas to be masked during coating.

A coated thread without prior allowance may not go together, and a hole with a tight fit may not accept the shaft. There is one trap that is not just a question of size. In high-strength steels, electroplating can introduce hydrogen into the material.

The hydrogen causes embrittlement, and the part can break without warning, hours or days after assembly. So the specification usually requires hydrogen embrittlement relief baking, shortly after plating. When the drawing specifies a hard steel and electroplating,

and there is no baking requirement, that is an urgent question for the designer. The notes on the drawing also define, explicitly or implicitly, a sequence of operations. Usually: material in a given condition, rough machining, heat treatment, finish machining, and coating only at the end.

Heat treatment is defined by its result: a hardness range, a case depth and a test location. A coating is defined by a specification, changes dimensions, and requires knowing whether the dimensions are before or after coating. 17. Assembly Drawings

A part drawing says what one part looks like. An assembly drawing says how the parts come together. It does not redefine the geometry of each part. That is the job of the part drawings. But it defines what is assembled, how many of each, in which order,

with what force, and what must be achieved at the end. So misreading an assembly drawing can reject a whole assembly, even when every part on its own is conforming. In this chapter we will read the parts list, the balloons,

the assembly notes, and the dimensions that appear only in the assembly. An assembly drawing usually includes a view or a section showing all the parts in place. In an assembly section, as we saw in chapter two, shafts, bolts and pins are not hatched, and adjacent parts get hatching at different angles.

That way you can tell the boundary between two parts, even when they are made of the same material. There are few dimensions on an assembly drawing, and they belong to the assembly only: overall dimensions, locations, or dimensions to be achieved after assembly.

The parts list is the table that defines what goes into the assembly. It is defined in standards such as ISO 7573 and ASME Y14.34, and it usually has an item number, a part number, a description, a quantity, and a material or standard. The item number is the number that appears in the balloon,

and the part number is the number of the drawing or of the purchased part. Standard parts are described by their standard, for example an ISO 4762 screw, M8x25, property class 12.9. And for consumables, such as adhesive, sealant or shims,

the quantity is sometimes written as AR, as required. Someone who replaces a standard screw with one that looks identical but has a different property class changes the assembly. A balloon is a circle with an item number, and a leader line pointing to the part in the picture.

When the leader ends in a dot, it points to the surface of the part. When it ends in an arrowhead, it points to its outline. Every item in the list must appear in a balloon at least once. Identical parts that repeat several times get one balloon, and the quantity is written in the list.

And for fastener sets, such as a screw, a washer and a nut, a stack of balloons on a single leader is sometimes used. When there is a balloon with no row in the list, or a row with no balloon, that is an error on the drawing, and you need to ask.

Most of the assembly information is in the notes. A tightening torque, for example twenty-five newton meters, appears next to the screw or in a general note. And it must always come with a condition: whether the thread is dry or lubricated.

The same torque on a lubricated thread produces a much higher clamping force. In a joint with several screws, a tightening sequence sometimes appears too, for example in a cross pattern, and in several steps. Other notes define a threadlocker, lubrication, or a sealant, to a specification.

Someone who tightens without reading the condition may tighten less than required, or stretch the screw until it fails. The assembly drawing often shows the fits between the parts, for example H7/g6 next to a shaft in a bushing.

For an interference fit, the note also defines the assembly method: pressing, heating the outer part, or cooling the shaft. And when the assembly includes an adjustment, such as shims that close a gap, the drawing defines the final dimension to be achieved, not the shim thickness.

That is exactly the solution from the tolerance stack-up in chapter eight: measure at assembly, and select the right shim. Sometimes a part is not fully machined before assembly. A hole for a dowel pin, for example, is sometimes drilled through two parts together,

loses the match that drilling them together was meant to give. Each level has its own drawing and parts list, and a subassembly appears in the top-level assembly as a single item. So when you look for a particular part, you sometimes have to go down several levels. An exploded view, in which the parts are drawn separated along the assembly line,

helps you understand the order, but it does not replace the parts list. And for parts that come in pairs, right-hand and left-hand, the drawing sometimes shows only one of them, and states that the other is its mirror image. A summary of the traps in an assembly drawing.

19. Critical Characteristics and Inspection

A drawing has dozens of dimensions, and they are not all equally important. Some dimensions, if exceeded, still leave a working part. And some, if exceeded, make the part fail, and sometimes put people at risk. The drawing marks these dimensions, and that marking determines how

they are manufactured, how they are inspected, and what is documented. In this chapter we will learn to read critical characteristics, and understand what first article inspection and measurement on a coordinate measuring machine require. A key characteristic, a KC, is a dimension or requirement whose

variation has a strong effect on function, safety or assembly. diamond, a hexagon, a letter in a box, or another symbol. So the meaning of the symbol is always defined in the notes on the drawing, or in the customer's specification.

Many organizations also have a classification of defects: critical, major and minor. A critical defect can cause a hazard, a major defect impairs the function, and a minor defect does not affect use. Someone who does not know the symbol reads a critical characteristic like any other dimension.

Cpk adds the location of the process: the distance from the mean to the nearer limit, divided by three standard deviations. A process can be narrow enough, but too close to a limit, and then Cp is good and Cpk is low. A common requirement is a Cpk of at least 1.33, and sometimes more for critical characteristics.

So someone who aims for the edge of the tolerance to leave material for rework lowers the Cpk, even when every part is within tolerance. First article inspection, FAI, proves that the process is capable of producing a part that meets every requirement of the drawing.

In aerospace it is defined in AS9102. The standard defines three forms: the first identifies the part and the drawing, the second documents materials, special processes and functional tests, and the third documents every single characteristic.

In the third form, every dimension, every tolerance and every note on the drawing gets a row, with the measured result. And this inspection is repeated after a design change, a process change, a move to another site, or a long break in production.

Someone who changes the manufacturing process and does not repeat the inspection is supplying a part that has not been proven to meet the requirements. For first article inspection to be complete, the drawing is ballooned. Every requirement gets a balloon with a number: a dimension,

20. Model-Based Definition · MBD

In every chapter so far, the definition of the part has been a sheet: views, dimensions and notes. In many organizations this is changing. The definition moves into the 3D model itself, and the 2D drawing disappears or becomes secondary. This is called model-based definition, MBD.

In this chapter we will learn to read such a model, and what changes in the rules we have learned, when the model is the contract. be on the drawing is attached to the geometry itself. Dimensions, geometric tolerances, datums, surface texture and notes

are together called PMI: product and manufacturing information. There are two kinds of PMI. Graphical PMI looks like text on the screen, but the computer does not know which surface it belongs to. Semantic PMI is linked to the surfaces themselves, and the computer knows

which tolerance applies to which surface, relative to which datums. Only semantic PMI lets CAM software or a measuring machine read the requirements automatically. Y14.41, and on the international side in ISO 16792.

The rules for such a model are defined in ASME Y14.41, and on the international side in ISO 16792. The symbols themselves have not changed: the same GD&T symbols, the same frames and the same datums, only in 3D space. To transfer a model with PMI between different systems, the STEP AP242 format is used.

And for inspection results and measurement plans there is the QIF format, which links the requirement in the model to the measured result. An MBD model usually includes saved views, which correspond to the views on a drawing. Each saved view shows a particular viewing direction, and only the PMI

that belongs to it, so that not everything appears on the screen at once. When you select a tolerance, the system highlights the surface it applies to and its datum features. So in a good model there is no question which surface a note belongs to, as there sometimes is on a crowded drawing.

Someone who reads a model only through a picture of it loses all this information. Here there is a fundamental change compared to a drawing. In chapter zero we learned not to measure from the picture. In an MBD model, the geometry is the definition, and it is exact.

A dimension that is not written explicitly is queried from the model, as a basic dimension. And its tolerance comes from a general note, usually a profile of a surface tolerance on all surfaces, relative to a datum reference frame. For example: all unmarked surfaces, a profile of five tenths relative to A, B and C.

In other words, in an MBD model every surface is defined, even if there is no number next to it. When the model is the contract, its version matters exactly as much as a drawing revision. The file must be identified with a number and a version, and often with an integrity check of the file as well.

Many organizations add a reduced drawing: a 2D sheet with the important dimensions, the material and the notes, for convenience on the shop floor. But the reduced drawing is usually for reference only, and the model governs. So it is important to check what is stated about the relationship between them,

and which one governs when they contradict each other. Not every 3D file carries the same information. A native CAD file carries everything, but requires the same system. STEP AP242 carries exact geometry and semantic PMI as well.

Older versions of STEP, such as AP203 or AP214, usually carry only geometry, without the requirements. And triangle mesh files, such as STL, are an approximation of the geometry, not the geometry itself.

Someone who receives a model in a format that does not carry PMI receives a shape without tolerances, and that is not enough for manufacturing. The great advantage of MBD is that the same model can be used all the way through. The CAM software builds toolpaths directly from the geometry.

The measuring machine can read the semantic PMI and build a measurement program from it. And first article inspection can be built directly from the list of requirements in the model, instead of from manual ballooning of a drawing. But this requires a tool at every station that can display the model and its PMI.

A worker who has only a picture or a reduced drawing does not see some of the requirements. A summary of the traps in an MBD model. Measuring a surface from the model when its tolerance is defined elsewhere, without checking the general note.

Working to an old version of the model, because the file was saved on a local computer. Saving to a format that does not carry PMI, and losing all the tolerances. A reduced drawing that was not updated after a change to the model. And units: a model built in inches and opened as if it were in millimeters.

Start by identifying the model: number, version, and the status of the reduced drawing. Then the general notes: units, standard, and the general profile tolerance. Then the saved views, one by one, with the PMI of each. And only then queries on the geometry, for dimensions that are not written explicitly.

Someone who starts by measuring the model, without reading the notes, does not know which tolerance applies to what he measured. In MBD, the 3D model is the definition, and the PMI is linked to the geometry. requirements, and STEP AP242 transfers it between systems.

Dimensions that are not written are taken from the model, and their tolerance comes from a general note. And the model version matters like a revision, and the model governs over the reduced drawing. In the next chapter we will deal with change management:

21. Change Management

mistakes are discovered, and requirements change. And with every change, the important question is not only what changed, but whether everyone is working to the same definition. Change management is the system that ensures this: who requests, who approves,

from when the change applies, and what is done with what has already been made. In this chapter we will learn to read a drawing that has changed, and to tell a revision, a deviation and a waiver apart. A drawing that has changed has a revision block, usually in the upper corner.

Each row has a revision letter or number, a short description of the change, a date, and who approved it. The rules for this are defined in ASME Y14.35. Where something changed in the picture, a small triangle with

the revision letter sometimes appears, so it is easy to find. And around the border of the sheet there is a grid of letters and numbers, like on a map. The revision block states which zone each change is in. Someone who reads only the latest revision in the block,

and does not look for the markers in the picture, may miss a change that looks small. Not every change gets a new revision. Sometimes it gets a new part number. The rule is interchangeability: can an old part and a new part replace each other everywhere? the parts are interchangeable, and a revision is enough.

If the change affects one of them, for example a hole that moved and will not fit an old assembly, the new part gets a new part number. Otherwise, two different parts with the same number will sit in the stores, and someone will assemble the wrong one.

A change starts with an engineering change request, an ECR, which explains what the problem is and what is proposed. After approval, an engineering change order, an ECO, is issued, which defines exactly what changes and in which documents.

The order also defines effectivity: from when the change applies. For example from a certain serial number, from a date, or from a production lot. And it defines the disposition of what already exists: parts in stock, parts in work, and parts at the customer.

The options are usually: use as is, rework, or scrap. Someone who receives a drawing at a new revision without checking the effectivity may stop production that is still allowed, or continue production that is no longer allowed. There are situations in which the drawing is not changed, and yet it is not met.

A deviation is approval in advance to manufacture differently from the drawing, for a limited quantity or a limited time. For example, a different material because the material on the drawing is unavailable, for one hundred parts only.

A waiver is approval after the fact to accept a part that has already been made and does not meet the requirement. In some standards it is also called a concession. In both cases, the drawing does not change.

The approval applies only to the parts or the period defined in it. Someone who keeps manufacturing under a deviation after the quantity has run out is producing nonconforming parts. When a part does not meet a requirement, a material review board decides what to do with it.

Rework brings the part into full conformance with the drawing. After rework, the part conforms. Repair leaves the part different from the drawing, but usable. So repair requires engineering approval, and sometimes customer approval as well. Someone who calls a repair rework is hiding the fact that the part does not meet the drawing.

When working with a customer, there are changes the customer must approve. A change that affects form, fit, function, safety or cost is considered a Class | change, and requires customer approval. A minor change, such as correcting a spelling mistake,

is considered a Class II change, and sometimes a notification is enough. And a process change too, without a change to the drawing, can require approval, as we Saw with first article inspection in chapter nineteen. On the shop floor, the important question is which copy is in your hands.

An old printed copy is one of the most common sources of mistakes. So printed copies are sometimes marked as uncontrolled copies, or collected when a new revision is issued. A handwritten correction on a drawing, without a change order, is not a change. It is only a note. against the system, not just against the sheet in your hands.

A summary of the traps in change management. Working from an old copy, because it was the one available next to the machine. A handwritten correction treated as an approved change. A deviation used after its quantity or its period has run out.

A new revision, where a new part number was needed. Old stock that was not dealt with, because nobody read the disposition. And a process change, without repeating first article inspection. When you receive a drawing that has changed,

start from the revision block: what changed, and in which zone. Then look for the revision markers in the picture, and compare with the previous revision. Then read the change order: the effectivity, and the disposition of what already exists. And finally check that the related documents were updated too:

work instructions, inspection plans and machining programs. Someone who updates only the drawing, and keeps the old measurement program, will measure a new part to old requirements. The revision block, the markers in the picture and the

zones around the border show what changed and where. A change that breaks interchangeability gets a new part number, not just a revision. A deviation is approval in advance, a waiver is approval after the fact, and both apply only to what is defined in them.

Rework brings the part to the drawing, and repair leaves it different and requires approval. In the next chapter we move to the last part of the course, and read a drawing through the manufacturer's eyes. See you in chapter twenty-two.

22. Reading Through the Manufacturer's Eyes

Until now we have read drawings to understand what the designer requires. The manufacturer reads the same drawing with a different question: how do we make this, how much will it cost, and what can go wrong. This kind of reading is done before the quote and before work starts,

not after the part is already on the machine. In this chapter we will go through the drawing in the order an experienced manufacturer reads it, and see what to look for at each stage. The first pass is the title block: material, condition, quantity, revision and standard.

The material and the overall dimensions determine the stock: which bar diameter, which plate thickness, and how much machining allowance. A material that is not available in the right size changes the price and the lead time before work even starts.

coating, marking, and documents the drawing refers to. Every such reference is additional work, and sometimes an outside supplier and waiting time. The next question is how many setups will be needed. Every side of the part that has machined features is

usually another setup, and every setup has setup time. The datum reference frame says what to locate on: the primary datum feature is usually machined first, and serves as the basis for everything else. Features with a tight tolerance between them are best machined

in the same setup, so as not to introduce a transfer error. When the drawing requires a tight relationship between two sides of the part, you need to plan how the datum is transferred between setups. After that, look for what determines the process and the cost.

The tightest tolerance on the drawing sometimes determines the entire process: grinding, reaming, or a separate finishing operation. A low roughness, or a very small geometric tolerance, also adds an operation. A sharp internal corner in a pocket is a well-known trap.

An end mill always leaves a radius, and a truly sharp corner requires EDM. So when there is no internal radius on the drawing, ask which radius is allowed, before quoting. A deep, narrow hole, with a depth of more than ten times the diameter, requires a special deep-hole drill, with through-tool coolant, and a long time.

And thin walls distort during machining and clamping, and need to be planned for in advance. A long, slender shaft, with a length of more than four times the diameter, will deflect in turning without support. It requires a tailstock center or a steady rest, and that affects the setup and the time.

A manufacturer who discovers them on the machine loses money. As we saw in chapter thirteen, the notes define a sequence of operations. Heat treatment in the middle of the process requires leaving a machining allowance, and planning finish machining after it.

Coating at the end of the process requires knowing whether the dimensions are before or after coating, and masking the areas that must not be coated. Every such outside step adds lead time, so it is part of the planning and of the price. An experienced manufacturer also checks how he will

measure every dimension, before he makes anything. Does he have an instrument with enough resolution for the tightest tolerance? Are there critical characteristics that require statistical control or inspection of every part? Is first article inspection required, and what does it include?

And when inspection requires equipment he does not have, he needs to decide in advance whether to send it out for inspection, or to ask. From this whole pass, a list of questions for the designer is built. Dimensions that contradict each other, missing tolerances,

an undefined datum feature, or a coating with no thickness. Features that cannot be made as drawn, such as a sharp internal corner. And sometimes suggestions too: a larger radius, a wider tolerance that does not harm the function, or a more available material.

But a suggestion is a question, not a decision. As we Saw in chapter zero, a manufacturer does not change the drawing on his own. And a question asked before manufacturing costs much less than one discovered after the part has been rejected.

In a quote, the drawing is translated into time and material. The setup time for each setup is spread over the whole quantity, so in a small quantity it is a large part of the price. The cycle time per part is determined mainly by the tolerances,

23. Diagnostic Workshop

In this chapter we will not learn anything new. We will use what we have learned. We will look at six drawings, and each of them has one error. After each drawing we will pause for a moment. Try to find the error yourself, before we reveal it. And for every error we will ask three questions: what is wrong,

what will come out of it in production, and what do we ask the designer. Here is a plate with three dimensions: forty, eighty, and one hundred twenty for the overall length. Each of them has a tolerance of plus or minus one tenth. What is wrong? The dimensions close a chain. The overall length is defined twice:

once directly, and once as the sum of the other two dimensions. If the two smaller dimensions are at their upper limit, the sum is one hundred twenty point two, and that is already outside the tolerance of the overall dimension. The manufacturer does not know which dimension to honor.

So we ask the designer which of them is a reference dimension, in parentheses. Here is a position tolerance frame, referring to datums A and B. Only datum A is identified in the picture. What is wrong? Datum B is not defined anywhere on the drawing, so the tolerance is incomplete.

Every manufacturer will choose a different surface, and every measuring machine will build a different reference frame. The results will not be comparable. The question for the designer is simple: which feature is datum B? Here is a hole with a position tolerance of one tenth, relative to A, B and C.

Its location is defined by dimensions of thirty and twenty, with a tolerance of plus or minus five hundredths. What is wrong? With a position tolerance, the dimensions that locate the feature must be basic dimensions, in a frame, with no tolerance.

Here there are two contradicting definitions: a square zone from the dimensions, and a round zone from the geometric tolerance. The manufacturer does not know which to inspect. The question for the designer: are the dimensions supposed to be basic dimensions?

Here is a hole with an H7 fit, and a note: zinc plating, eight microns. There is no note about a masked area, and no note on whether the dimensions are before or after plating. What is wrong? Eight microns of plating on each side reduces the hole by sixteen microns,

and that is almost the entire H7 tolerance width at this diameter. If the part is machined to the drawing and then plated, the hole will come out of tolerance. The question for the designer: should the hole be masked during plating, or does the dimension apply after plating?

Here is an M8 thread, sixteen deep, in a blind hole. The tap drill depth is also defined as sixteen. What is wrong? The tap drill hole must be deeper than the thread, to leave room for the tip of the tap. At equal depths, the thread will not be full to the bottom, or the tap will hit the bottom and break.

The question for the designer: what is the tap drill depth, or what full-thread depth is really required? Here is an ISO fillet weld symbol, with a double reference line, and the number five to the left of the triangle. What is wrong?

In ISO, a letter must appear before the number: a for throat thickness, or z for leg length. Without a letter, five can be a throat thickness of five, meaning a leg of about seven, or a leg of five. The difference is about thirty percent in strength. The question for the designer: a or z? In each of these cases, the drawing looks complete at first glance.

The error was not in the picture, but in the relationship between two details: a dimension and a dimension, a tolerance and a datum, a fit and a coating. but checking that the details agree with each other. In every case, the solution was not to choose an interpretation, but to ask.

A manufacturer who chooses an interpretation on his own takes on the responsibility for the result. A good question for the designer is short and unambiguous. It states the drawing number, the revision, and the location on the sheet, by the zone on the border. It describes the problem in one sentence, and offers one or two options.

For example: on drawing number such-and-such, revision C, zone B3, datum B is not defined. Is the bottom surface intended? A question like that is answered quickly, and leaves no room for interpretation. And a question that was asked and answered in writing is

kept with the drawing, so the next production run knows too. A summary of the traps in the workshop. Reading each detail on its own, and not checking the relationship between them. Choosing a convenient interpretation, instead of asking.

24. A Complete Reading Protocol

This is the last chapter of the course. Throughout the course we learned each part of the drawing separately: views, dimensions, tolerances, datums, materials and documents. In this chapter we will bring it all together into one protocol, ten steps, that suits any drawing.

The goal is not to skip any step, and not to discover a problem only when the part is already on the machine. The first step is to check what is in your hands. The drawing number, the revision, and the number of sheets.

The revision must match the one on the order. And also check the document status and the approvals: a drawing that has not been released is still a draft. The second step is to read the rules the drawing was written by.

The units, the projection symbol, and the tolerancing standard, which determines whether the envelope principle or the independency principle applies. And then the general tolerances and the general notes. They apply to every dimension that has no tolerance of its own.

The third step is the material and its condition, the heat treatment, and the coating. And with them, all the documents the drawing refers to: material specifications, process specifications and inspection standards. From here you can already know what the stock is,

which outside processes will be needed, and in which order. The fourth step is to understand the shape. Go through the views and the sections, and build a 3D picture of the part in your head. And pay attention to conventions and simplifications, such as threads,

symmetrical parts and hidden lines, so you do not read them as ordinary geometry. The fifth step is the datum reference frame. Identify the primary, secondary and tertiary datums, and the order between them. The datum reference frame determines how the part is set

up in manufacturing, and how it is fixtured in inspection. The sixth step is to go through every dimension. For every dimension, check what type it is: a toleranced dimension, a basic dimension, or a reference dimension.

Check the dimension chains, and that no dimension is defined twice. whether it is a clearance, transition or interference fit. The seventh step is to go through every feature control frame. In every frame ask: what does it apply to, what is the shape of the zone,

relative to which datums, and is there an MMC or LMC modifier. And check that every datum that appears in the frame is actually identified on the drawing. The eighth step is everything defined by symbols and references. tap drill holes, welds, edges, undercuts and splines.

This is where many of the errors we saw in the diagnostic workshop are found, because they look small. The ninth step is to identify critical characteristics, and plan the inspection. Which dimensions are marked as critical, and what they require:

control, documentation, or inspection of every part. Is first article inspection required, and is there a suitable measuring instrument for every tolerance. The tenth step is to collect everything that is unclear. Every contradiction, every missing tolerance,

and every feature that cannot be made as drawn, becomes a question. The question includes the drawing number, the revision and the zone, and it is sent to the designer before work starts. And the answers are kept in writing, with the drawing.

On an assembly drawing, add the parts list, the balloons, the assembly notes and the torques to the protocol. In an MBD model, the steps are the same, but they are read from the saved views and the PMI, not from a sheet.

And on a drawing that has changed, start from the revision block, and check what changed and what the effectivity is. You might ask why a protocol is needed, if you know how to read a drawing. The answer is that most of the mistakes we saw in this course did

not come from a lack of knowledge, but from a step that was skipped. A revision that was not checked, a general note that was not read, or a datum that nobody noticed was missing. A fixed protocol turns reading into a routine, and routine is what prevents mistakes.
