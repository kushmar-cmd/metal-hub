0. Map of the World of Materials

Here is a simple bracket. Same shape, same dimensions, same drawing. Now let's make it three times: in steel, in aluminum, and in titanium. The steel bracket weighs one kilogram. The same bracket in aluminum weighs about a third of that, and in titanium a little over half.

But weight is only the beginning. The three brackets will bend differently under the same load, react differently to heat and environment, cost very different amounts, and take a completely different path through the shop. The drawing is identical. The material changes everything. That is what this course is about. All engineering materials fall into four large families: metals, polymers, ceramics, and composites.

In this course we focus on metals, the materials most machined parts are made of. Metals are divided into two groups. Ferrous metals, where iron is the main element: all kinds of steel, and cast iron. And non-ferrous metals: aluminum, copper, titanium, nickel, and more. Steel is by far the most widely used metal in industry.

It is cheap, it is strong, and its properties can be changed over a very wide range with alloying elements and heat treatment. How do we compare materials? By their properties. Let's start with the mechanical properties, the ones that decide how a part behaves under load. Yield strength is the stress at which the material starts to deform permanently. Up to that point, when the load is removed, the part springs back. Beyond it, the part stays bent.

Tensile strength is the maximum stress the material can carry before it breaks. For most parts, yield strength is the more important number, because a part that has deformed no longer works. Ductility is the ability of a material to deform a lot before it breaks. It is measured as the elongation of a tensile specimen. Hardness is the resistance of the surface to indentation and wear.

It is measured by pressing an indenter into the material, using the Brinell, Rockwell, and Vickers methods. And the property people tend to forget: stiffness, measured by the modulus of elasticity. It tells you how much a part bends under load, long before it reaches yield. The modulus of steel is about 200 gigapascals, titanium about 110, and aluminum about 70.

And here is a fact that surprises many people: all steels, from the cheapest to the hardest tool steel, have almost the same stiffness. Heat treatment raises strength, but it does not reduce how much the part bends under load. If a bracket bends too much, replacing the steel with a stronger steel will not help. You need to change the shape, or move to a material with a higher modulus.

Another mechanical property is toughness: the ability to absorb energy and resist fracture, for example under impact. It is measured with the Charpy test. Here lies one of the basic trade-offs of metallurgy: the harder you make a material, the more brittle it usually becomes. A very hard steel resists wear very well, but it can crack under impact. A softer steel will bend, but it will not break. That is why, in the chapters on heat treatment, we will see that the goal is never the highest hardness, but the right combination for the job the part has to do.

Beyond the mechanical properties there are physical properties, which matter just as much when choosing a material. Density: steel is about 7.85 grams per cubic centimeter, titanium about 4.4, and aluminum about 2.7. Divide strength by density and you get specific strength, and here titanium and the strong aluminum alloys win. That is why they dominate aerospace. Thermal expansion: for the same temperature change, aluminum grows about twice as much as steel.

In a precision part, or an assembly that combines the two materials, this can be the difference between an assembly that works and one that jams. Thermal and electrical conductivity: copper and aluminum conduct very well, stainless steel and titanium far less. This affects how heat spreads through the part, and also how much heat stays in the cutting zone during machining. And finally, resistance to the environment: corrosion, high temperatures, and contact with chemicals.

A part that is strong enough on day one but rusts after a year is the wrong material choice. This is where metallurgy comes in. It answers the question: where do the properties of a metal come from? The answer is a chain of three links: the chemical composition, the internal structure, and the properties we measure. Composition decides what is possible. But processing, heat treatment, and cooling decide which internal structure actually forms, and the structure is what decides the properties.

An example: 4140 steel. The same bar, exactly the same composition. In the soft condition its hardness is about 200 Brinell, and it machines easily. After hardening it can reach more than 50 Rockwell C, and then it is very hard to machine with ordinary tools. The composition did not change. The structure inside the grains did.

That is why a material cannot be defined by its name alone. 4140 steel without a heat treatment condition is an incomplete specification, and so is 6061 aluminum without the T that follows it. And now the question every estimator asks: how much does it cost? Price per kilogram is the first part. Carbon steel is the cheapest. Aluminum and stainless steel cost several times more. Titanium and nickel alloys can reach twenty times as much per kilogram, or more.

But for a machined part, price per kilogram does not tell the whole story. First, an aluminum bar weighs a third of a steel bar of the same size, so the difference in material cost is smaller than it looks. Second, and usually the bigger factor: machining time. Aluminum is cut at very high speeds. Titanium and nickel alloys are cut at speeds several times lower, and they wear out tools quickly.

And there are more factors: availability in standard sizes, minimum order quantity, the heat treatment and coating the material needs, and the material certificates the customer requires. A good material choice weighs all of these together, not just the price per kilogram. So how do you actually choose a material? You work like a funnel. Start with the requirements of the part: loads, weight, operating temperature, environment, and the standards the customer requires.

From the requirements comes a short list of candidate materials, all of which can do the job. Then check manufacturing: is the material easy to machine, can it be welded, and what heat treatment and coating does it need. And finally, decide on cost and availability. Very often the best choice is not the strongest material, but the simplest material that meets all the requirements.

And here is the map of the course. We start with the fundamentals of metallurgy: crystal structure, properties and testing, phase diagrams, and the ways to strengthen a metal. Then we go through the different steels and their heat treatments, and cast iron. From there we move on to the non-ferrous metals: aluminum, copper, titanium, and the superalloys.

We will learn how materials fail in service, through corrosion and wear, and how the material affects machining, welding, and the form it is supplied in. And we finish with standards, material certificates, and choosing a material in practice, with examples of real parts. Because in the end, every good part starts with the right material.

1. Crystal Structure and Grains

When you look at a metal part, it seems uniform and solid. But magnify the surface a hundred times, and you see that it is built from thousands of tiny crystals packed tightly together. These are the grains. And if we zoom in much further, into a single grain, we see that the atoms inside are arranged in straight rows, in a pattern that repeats over and over. This order, the crystal lattice, is the foundation of everything we will learn in this course.

It explains why metal can be bent and machined, why heat treatment changes it, and why one steel shatters in the cold while aluminum does not. What holds the atoms together? In metals, the atoms release some of their electrons, and these electrons move freely among all the atoms, like a shared cloud. This is the metallic bond. It explains two properties that set metals apart from other materials. First, the free electrons conduct electricity and heat.

Second, the bond is not tied to one particular neighbor. When a row of atoms slides to a new position, it simply bonds to its new neighbors, and the material does not break. That is why metal can be bent, forged, and drawn, while glass or ceramics crack. Most engineering metals arrange themselves in one of three structures. The first is BCC, a cube with an atom at each corner and one atom in the center. Iron at room temperature, chromium, molybdenum, and tungsten have this structure.

The second is FCC, a cube with an atom at each corner and in the center of each face. Aluminum, copper, nickel, and austenitic stainless steel have this structure. The third is HCP, a close-packed hexagonal structure. Titanium at room temperature, magnesium, and zinc have this structure. And the structure has practical consequences. FCC has many planes the atoms can slide on, so these metals are very ductile, and they stay ductile even at very low temperatures.

In BCC, slip depends more on temperature, and in the cold it becomes difficult. That is why ordinary carbon steels can become brittle at low temperatures. HCP has few slip planes, so magnesium and zinc are hard to form cold, and titanium is also less formable than aluminum. lron has a special property, and the whole theory of steel heat treatment is built on it: it changes its crystal structure with temperature. Up to 912 degrees Celsius, iron is BCC.

Above 912 degrees it changes to FCC, and this structure is called austenite. Above 1,394 degrees it returns to BCC, all the way to melting at 1,538 degrees. This change happens in the solid state, without melting the part. And when the structure changes, so does the amount of carbon the iron can hold inside it.

This is exactly the transformation that hardening relies on, as we will see in chapter 6. Now for the important question: how does a metal deform? The natural assumption is that a whole plane of atoms slides at once over the plane beneath it. But if you calculate the force that would require, you get a strength a hundred times or more higher than what we actually measure.

The reason is defects in the lattice called dislocations. A dislocation is a row of atoms that is missing, or squeezed in, inside the lattice. Think of a heavy rug you want to move. Pulling the whole thing is very hard. But if you make a ruck in it and push the ruck along, the rug moves easily, a little at a time.

That is exactly how a dislocation moves: it shifts only one row of bonds at any moment, so the force needed is much smaller. The plastic deformation of a metal, every bend, every forging, and every chip, is the movement of billions of dislocations. From this comes the central idea of metal strength: to strengthen a metal, you have to make it harder for dislocations to move. One way to do that is deformation itself.

When a metal is deformed cold, the dislocations multiply, run into each other, and tangle, and every new dislocation finds it harder to move. This is work hardening. We all know it: bend a paper clip back and forth, it gets harder at the bend, and eventually it breaks there. This effect explains why stainless steel hardens under the cutting tool, and why bent sheet metal becomes harder along the bend line. And work hardening can be removed by heating, which rebuilds the grains.

Now back to the grains. When molten metal cools, crystals start growing at many points at once. Each crystal grows in its own direction, until it meets its neighbors. Each such crystal is a grain, and the line where two grains meet is a grain boundary. Inside each grain the rows are straight and orderly, but from grain to grain the direction changes.

And here is the point: a dislocation moving inside a grain reaches the boundary, and there the rows suddenly turn in another direction. The dislocation cannot keep going straight, and it stops. In other words, every grain boundary is a barrier. So grain size directly affects strength. The smaller the grains, the more boundaries there are, the more barriers, and the stronger the metal.

And there is a rare bonus here: a fine grain also improves toughness. It is almost the only way to strengthen a metal without paying for it in brittleness. Grain size is measured under the microscope and expressed as a grain size number, according to the standard ASTM E112. Note that the number works in reverse: a higher number means a finer grain. And grain size is not fixed. Long heating at high temperature makes the grains grow.

This happens, for example, in a heat treatment done at too high a temperature, or in the zone next to a weld. When sheet or bar is rolled, the grains and the inclusions in the material are stretched along the rolling direction. As a result, the properties are no longer the same in every direction. Along the rolling direction, ductility and toughness are better, and across it they are lower. So when bending sheet, it is better for the bend line to be perpendicular to the rolling direction.

A bend whose line runs parallel to the rolling direction can crack, especially with a small radius and a hard material. The same principle works in forging. In a forged part, the flow lines of the material follow the shape of the part. In a part machined from bar, the cutting slices through these lines. This is one reason why highly loaded critical parts, like crankshafts, are forged. When the direction matters, it is marked on the drawing.

What does all this mean in the shop? FCC material, such as austenitic stainless steel or aluminum, or a steel that has passed a low- temperature impact test. A part that was overheated in heat treatment can come out with coarse grains and low toughness, even when its hardness is fine. And what drives the cost? A grain direction requirement on the drawing limits how parts are nested on the sheet, so there is more scrap.

A forged part needs a die, and that only pays off in quantity. And a requirement for fine grain or a low-temperature impact test adds testing and certificates. In the next chapter we will meet the tools used to measure all these properties: the tensile, hardness, impact, and fatigue tests.

2. Mechanical Properties and Testing

Every material certificate and every serious drawing carries numbers: minimum yield strength, a hardness range, impact energy at a given temperature. Each one is measured in a defined test, to a standard, on a specimen of a defined size. To read them correctly, and to know what they guarantee and what they do not, you need to understand how each test works. In this chapter we go through the main tests: tensile, hardness, impact, fatigue, and creep.

The tensile test is the most basic of all. You take a round or flat specimen, mark a gauge length on it, and pull it slowly until it breaks, while measuring the force and the elongation. Divide the force by the cross-sectional area of the specimen and you get stress, in megapascals, which is newtons per square millimeter. At first the line is straight: this is the elastic region. Most metals have no sharp point where the line bends, so yield strength is defined by convention.

You draw a line parallel to the straight part, offset by 0.2 percent elongation, and the stress where that line meets the curve is the yield strength. On certificates it is marked Rp0.2. Soft structural steels sometimes have a sharp yield point, marked ReH. The highest point on the curve is the tensile strength, Rm. After it, the specimen starts to neck down in one spot, and shortly afterwards it breaks. You fit the two halves together, measure the gauge length, and get the elongation in percent, A.

You also measure the reduction of area at the fracture, Z. These two numbers describe the ductility. Now let's put a few curves on top of each other. Structural steel S355: a minimum yield of 355 megapascals, which is exactly what the number in its name says. After that it keeps stretching a long way before it breaks. Hardened steel: the curve climbs much higher, sometimes above 1,500 megapascals, but it breaks after very little elongation.

A strong aluminum alloy: medium strength, and a gentler slope because of its lower modulus. And one more important point: the numbers on the certificate are minimum values, and they were measured on a specimen from the same batch. Your part, after machining, welding, or heat treatment, is a different story. A hardness test is quick, barely damages the part, and is done on the part itself.

The principle is simple: you press a hard indenter into the material with a known force, and measure the impression it leaves. In the Brinell method you press a ball, usually ten millimeters in diameter, and measure the diameter of the impression. The impression is large, so the method suits coarse materials like castings and forgings, and gives a good average over many grains. The value is written HB, or HBW. In the Rockwell method you measure not the width of the impression but the depth of penetration, and the machine shows the number directly. It is the most common method on the shop floor.

It has several scales: HRC, with a diamond cone, for hardened steels, and HRB, with a small ball, for softer materials. In the Vickers method you press a diamond pyramid and measure the diagonals of the impression under a microscope. The same method covers a very wide range of hardness, and at low loads it can measure thin layers. The value is written HV. A few practical rules.

First, you can convert between the methods, but only approximately, using conversion tables from a standard such as ASTM E140. The tables are valid for steels, not for every material. A part that works under a load that repeats again and again, like a rotating shaft, a spring, or a bolt in an engine, can break at a stress far below its yield strength.

It does not happen all at once. A tiny crack starts at one point, grows a little with every load cycle, and finally the remaining section can no longer carry the load, and the part breaks. On the fracture surface you see beach marks, curved lines spreading out from the origin, and a final rough zone where the part finally broke. In a fatigue test you count how many cycles each specimen survives at each load, and you get an S-N curve. Steels usually have a fatigue limit, about half the tensile strength for a polished specimen, and below it the part lasts almost indefinitely.

And fatigue requirements bring finer finishes, precise radii, shot peening, and crack inspection. To sum up: tensile for strength and ductility, hardness for a quick check, impact for blows and cold, and fatigue and creep for what happens over time. 3. Alloys and Phase Diagrams

Almost no metal used in industry is pure. Pure iron is very soft, and you can bend a bar of it by hand. But add less than one percent of carbon and you get steel, several times stronger. A metal mixed with other elements to change its properties is called an alloy. In this chapter we will see what happens to the atoms when elements are mixed, and learn to read the map that shows which structure forms at each composition and each temperature: the phase diagram.

In iron at room temperature this limit is tiny: less than three hundredths of a percent of carbon.

What happens to the extra carbon? It forms a new structure, with its own composition and lattice: a compound of iron and carbon called cementite. Cementite is very hard and very brittle. A region of the material with a uniform structure and a uniform composition is called a phase. Most alloys contain more than one phase, and the amount, shape, and distribution of the phases are what decide the properties. To know which phases will form, we use a phase diagram. The horizontal axis is composition, and the vertical axis is temperature.

Each region of the diagram shows which phases are stable at that composition and temperature, when cooling is slow enough. Let's start with the simplest example: copper and nickel, which dissolve in each other in any proportion. At the top everything is liquid, and at the bottom everything is solid. Between the two lines there is a region where liquid and solid exist together. Let's follow one alloy as it cools: it starts to solidify when it crosses the upper line, and finishes only when it crosses the lower line.

In other words, an alloy does not solidify at a single temperature like a pure metal, but over a range of temperatures. For many pairs of elements the diagram looks different. Let's look at aluminum and silicon. When silicon is added to aluminum, the melting temperature drops, down to one point where it is lowest: 12.6 percent silicon, and 577 degrees. This point is called the eutectic point. At this composition the alloy solidifies at a single temperature, like a pure metal, and flows very well.

That is why most aluminum casting alloys are close to it. And the same idea is behind soft solders, which were chosen at the composition that melts at the lowest temperature. An important horizontal line runs at 727 degrees: below it, austenite is no longer stable. And at one point on this line, 0.77 percent carbon, the austenite transforms all at once into a new structure. Finally, the boundary at 2.1 percent carbon divides the diagram in two: steels on the left, and cast iron on the right.

Most engineering steels sit in a small corner on the left side, between zero and one percent carbon. What happens when steel cools slowly from austenite? At 0.77 percent carbon, the austenite breaks down into thin alternating layers: soft ferrite and hard cementite. This structure is called pearlite, because under the microscope it shines like mother of pearl.

In a steel with less carbon, for example a steel with 0.45 percent, part of the austenite first turns into ferrite, and only the rest turns into pearlite. The result is a mixture: roughly half ferrite and half pearlite. The more carbon there is, the more pearlite, and the stronger and harder the steel, and the less ductile. And in a steel with more than 0.77 percent carbon, like bearing steels and some tool steels, the extra carbon forms cementite at the grain boundaries.

But what if we don't give the steel time? If it is cooled from austenite very quickly, for example by quenching in water or oil, the carbon has no time to leave the lattice and form cementite. It stays trapped, and the lattice is forced into a distorted, strained structure. This structure is called martensite, and it is very hard and very brittle. 4. Strengthening Mechanisms

In chapter 1 we saw that a metal deforms when dislocations move through it. From this comes one principle that explains every way of strengthening a metal: put obstacles in the way of the dislocations. There are four main kinds of obstacle. Foreign atoms in solid solution, which we met in chapter 3. Other dislocations that tangle with each other. Grain boundaries. And tiny particles of a second phase. In this chapter we go through each one, and also see what undoes it.

The simplest way to strengthen a metal is to deform it cold. In cold rolling, drawing through a die, or bending, the dislocations multiply and tangle, and the metal becomes stronger and harder. This is work hardening. That is how cold-drawn bars, hard stainless steel sheet, and springs are made. But there is a price: the more cold work, the lower the ductility.

In aluminum, the letter H in the temper designation means work hardening. H14 is half hard, and H18 is full hard. Work hardening has two more sides worth knowing. The first: it can be reversed. Annealing, meaning heating to a suitable temperature, lets new, soft grains form in place of the deformed ones. This is recrystallization, and the metal returns to the soft condition.

The second: cold work leaves residual stresses in the material. When material is removed by machining, the stresses are released unevenly, and the part can warp. That is why a long, slender part machined from cold-drawn bar sometimes needs a stress relief treatment, or machining in stages. The second way is grain refinement. As we saw in chapter 1, every grain boundary stops dislocations, so a finer grain gives higher strength. And unlike the other methods, it improves toughness too.

In modern steels, a fine grain is achieved with tiny additions, hundredths of a percent, of niobium, vanadium, or titanium, together with rolling at a controlled temperature. The result is strong structural steels that are still easy to weld. The third way is precipitation hardening, and it is the secret behind the strong aluminum alloys. The process has three steps. In the first step, solution treatment, the alloy is heated to a high temperature, where the alloying elements dissolve completely in the metal.

In the second step it is cooled quickly, usually in water, so the elements stay trapped in solution. The third step is aging: heating to a moderate temperature for a few hours, and tiny precipitates begin to form. The tiny precipitates are spread throughout the grains, and each one is a barrier to dislocations. Strength rises with aging time, up to a peak.

If heating goes on too long, the precipitates grow, there are fewer of them, and strength falls. This is overaging. In aluminum, the temper T6 means solution treatment followed by artificial aging. T4 means natural aging, at room temperature. The difference is huge: 6061 aluminum in the soft condition, O, starts to yield at around 55 megapascals. In T6 it reaches 276. The same mechanism works in 17-4PH stainless steel, the alloy Inconel 718, and maraging steels.

The fourth way, and the strongest of all, is the martensitic transformation, which we met in chapter 3. Steel is cooled quickly from austenite, and the trapped carbon distorts the lattice so much that dislocations can hardly move. This is how strengths of 2,000 megapascals and more are reached. But fresh martensite is too brittle to use, so it is always tempered: heated again to a moderate temperature, giving up some hardness in exchange for toughness. A familiar example: 4140 prehard steel, which arrives already hardened and tempered, at about 30 Rockwell C, and can be machined directly.

And now the practical question: what undoes each mechanism? Work hardening disappears with annealing, and therefore also with welding. Grain refinement is damaged by long heating at high temperature, which makes the grains grow. Precipitation hardening is damaged by any heating that causes overaging, or dissolves the precipitates again. That is why, when 6061-T6 is welded, the zone next to the weld loses a large part of its strength. And martensite softens with any heating above its tempering temperature.

In other words, every heating a part goes through after heat treatment can change its properties. What does this mean in the shop? Before welding, hot bending, or a coating applied with heat, check how the material got its strength. And what drives the cost? Precipitation hardening, and hardening and tempering treatments, need controlled furnaces and close control of times and temperatures. Distortion after heat treatment requires extra stock for finish machining.

And cold-drawn bars cost more than hot-rolled bars, but they arrive at more precise dimensions with a better finish, so they sometimes save machining. To sum up: four mechanisms, and one principle, stop the dislocations. In the next chapter we start going through the families of materials, beginning with the most important of all: carbon steels.

5. Carbon Steels

Carbon steel is the most basic and most widely used steel: iron, carbon, and a little manganese. The manganese ties up the sulfur and improves strength. It also contains small amounts of phosphorus and sulfur, left over from the production process and limited by the standard. It has no deliberate alloying elements such as chromium, nickel, or molybdenum. That is why it is the cheapest, and its properties are set mainly by the amount of carbon and the heat treatment.

Carbon steels are divided into three families by carbon content. Low carbon: up to about 0.3 percent. Medium carbon: from 0.3 to 0.6 percent. And high carbon: above 0.6 percent, up to about one percent. As we saw in chapter 3, the more carbon there is, the stronger and harder the steel, and the better it hardens, but also the less ductile and the less weldable. Low carbon steel, also called mild steel, is the workhorse of industry.

It is used for sheet, pipe, sections, and beams, and also for simple machined parts. Familiar examples are 1018 and 1020, and the structural steels S235 and S355. It is easy to weld, bend, and machine, but it hardly hardens in an ordinary hardening treatment. When it needs a hard surface, only the surface is hardened, as we will see in chapter 7. Medium carbon steel is the choice for machine parts: pins, shafts, gears, and anchor bolts.

Its best-known representative is 1045, called C45 in the European standard. It hardens well. You can harden and temper the whole part, or induction harden only the area that wears, for example a bearing seat on a shaft. But welding needs care: because of the carbon, the zone next to the weld can harden and crack, so preheating is usually needed. High carbon steel, such as 1060 and 1095, is used for springs, blades, and high-strength wire.

After hardening it is very hard and wear resistant. On the other hand, it is more brittle, harder to machine in the hardened condition, and it is almost never welded. Now for the names. In the American system, AISI and SAE, a steel has a four-digit number. The first two digits tell you the type of steel: 10 is plain carbon steel, 11 and 12 are free-machining steels, with added sulfur.

The last two digits are the carbon content in hundredths of a percent. So 1045 is a plain carbon steel with 0.45 percent carbon. In the European standard, C45 is the same steel, and C45E is the version with controlled sulfur, suited to hardening. In the past, under DIN, it was called Ck45. In Japan it is called S45C. Every steel also has a European material number, and for C45 it is 1.0503.

Structural steels are named differently. In S355J2, the letter S means structural steel, 355 is the minimum yield strength in megapascals, and J2 is the impact requirement we met in chapter 2. Under the old DIN names, structural steels were called St37 and St52, and you still hear these names on the shop floor. There are also carbon steels designed specifically for fast machining, such as 12L14 and 1215. They contain added sulfur, and sometimes lead. The additions break the chip into short pieces and reduce friction, so you can cut faster, with less tool wear and a good finish.

They are very common on automatic lathes. The price is lower strength and ductility, and poor weldability, so they are not suitable for heavily loaded parts. We will come back to this in chapter 19. What does this mean in the shop? Carbon steel rusts, so it almost always needs a coating or paint. The material arrives hot rolled, with an oxide layer, or cold drawn, at precise dimensions.

And what drives the cost? Carbon steel itself is the cheapest. What adds cost is heat treatment, a low- temperature impact requirement with a certificate, and cold-drawn or ground material at a precise size. Sometimes a free-machining steel, slightly more expensive per kilogram, is the one that saves the most machine time. 6. Heat Treatment of Steel

In chapter 3 we saw that the internal structure of steel depends on how it is heated and cooled. Heat treatment is built on exactly that. The four basic heat treatments of steel are annealing, normalizing, hardening, and tempering. All of them have three stages: heating to a certain temperature, holding there, and cooling at a certain rate. The main difference between them is the cooling rate. In annealing, the steel is heated above the line where it turns into austenite, held there, and cooled very slowly, inside the furnace.

The result is the softest condition: coarse pearlite and ferrite. Annealing is used to make machining or cold forming easier, and to remove the hardening left by cold work. In high carbon steels, spheroidize annealing is used. It turns the cementite into small spheres, and that is the easiest condition for machining them. And there is also stress relieving: heating below the transformation temperature. It does not change the structure, it only lowers the internal stresses, for example after welding or heavy machining.

Normalizing is similar to annealing, but the cooling is done in still air, outside the furnace. Cooling is faster, so the pearlite is finer, and the grain is small and uniform. The steel comes out somewhat stronger and harder than after annealing. Normalizing is usually done after forging or hot rolling, to even out the structure before machining or before hardening. In hardening, the steel is heated until it has turned completely into austenite, and then cooled very fast, by quenching. As we saw in chapter 3, rapid cooling produces martensite.

It is quenched in water, oil, polymer, or gas. Water cools fastest, so it gives the highest hardness, but also the highest risk of distortion and cracking. Oil cools more slowly, and is safer for complex parts. Martensite forms only where cooling is fast enough. At the surface, cooling is fastest, and in the middle of a thick section it is slower. In a carbon steel like 1045, in a thick bar, only an outer layer hardens, and the core stays soft.

The ability of a steel to harden in depth is called hardenability, and it is improved by alloying elements, as we will see in chapter 8. Fresh martensite is very hard, but too brittle. So after hardening, the steel is tempered: heated again to a temperature below the transformation line, and held there. The higher the tempering temperature, the lower the hardness, and the higher the toughness. Low tempering, around 200 degrees, keeps the hardness high, and suits tools and bearings.

High tempering, around 550 to 650 degrees, gives good strength and high toughness, and suits shafts and bolts. Hardening followed by high-temperature tempering is called quenching and tempering. And it is important to temper right after hardening, before the part cracks from the stresses. Hardening is the most dangerous treatment for a part. Steel expands when it turns into martensite, and the part can distort or crack. Exposed sections cool first, and that creates uneven stresses. The risk is especially high with uneven sections, sharp corners, and holes close to an edge.

Another problem: when heated in air, the surface loses carbon and stays soft. So extra stock is left for grinding after heat treatment, or the part is heated in a protective atmosphere or a vacuum. What does this mean in the shop? On the drawing you write a hardness range, for example 40 to 45 Rockwell C, not just "hardened". Design uniform sections and corner radii, and leave stock for finish machining after heat treatment.

And what drives the cost? Every heat treatment is another step, with furnace time and transport to the subcontractor. A protective atmosphere or vacuum costs more than an ordinary furnace. And straightening, grinding after distortion, and hardness checks add more. In the next chapter we will meet a way to harden only the surface: carburizing, nitriding, and induction hardening.

7. Surface Hardening

Many parts have two requirements that seem to contradict each other. The surface has to be very hard, to resist wear and contact, for example on gear teeth or a bearing seat. But the part as a whole has to be tough, so it does not break under impact. The solution is to harden only the surface, and keep a tough core.

And there is a bonus: the hardened case stays in compression, and that greatly improves the fatigue life of the part. The most common method is carburizing. In carburizing, a low carbon steel is heated in a carbon-rich atmosphere, at around 900 to 950 degrees. At this temperature the steel is austenite, and carbon slowly diffuses into the surface, up to about 0.8 percent.

The longer it is held, the deeper the case, usually half a millimeter to two millimeters. Then it is quenched and tempered at a low temperature. The result: a surface of about 60 Rockwell C, and a soft, tough core. Steels such as 8620 and 16MnCr5 are used. Carburizing is the choice for gears, pins, and bearing races.

When part of the component must stay soft, for example a thread, the areas that must stay soft are masked, for example with copper plating or a special paste. Because there is a quench, there is also distortion. So stock is left for grinding, but not too much. The hardened case is thin, and if too much of it is removed, it is lost. Nitriding works differently.

Nitrogen diffuses into the surface at a low temperature, around 500 to 570 degrees, and forms hard nitrides with elements such as chromium, aluminum, and molybdenum. There is no quench, so there is almost no distortion, and the part comes out almost at final size. The case is thin, a few tenths of a millimeter, but very hard. The part has to be hardened and tempered beforehand, at a tempering temperature higher than the nitriding temperature.

A white layer forms on the surface, hard and brittle. Sometimes it is wanted, and sometimes it is removed. The drawback: the process is long, sometimes tens of hours. In induction hardening, a coil heats only the surface, within seconds, and immediately afterwards water is sprayed on. It suits medium carbon steels, such as 1045 and 4140, which harden on their own without adding carbon.

You can harden only the area that needs it, for example a bearing seat or gear teeth, and the case depth is set by the frequency of the current. Flame hardening works on the same principle, with a burner, and suits large parts. So which one do you choose? Carburizing gives a deep, strong case in a low carbon steel, but with distortion and grinding.

Nitriding gives a thin, very hard case, with almost no distortion, but it is a long process. 8. Alloy Steels

In chapters 5 to 7 we saw what carbon steel can do: it is cheap, easy to work, and hardens well when it has enough carbon. But it has four limitations. It hardens only to a shallow depth, so in a thick section the core stays soft. To harden it you need very fast cooling, in water, and that increases the risk of distortion and cracking. In the cold it becomes brittle. And at high temperature it softens quickly.

Alloy steels are designed to solve exactly these problems. In this chapter we deal with low alloy steels, where the total of the alloying elements is usually up to about five percent. To understand alloy steels, you have to separate two ideas that are easily confused. Hardness is how hard the martensite is, and as we saw in chapter 3, it is set almost entirely by the carbon. Hardenability is how deep the part hardens, and that is exactly what the alloying elements improve.

Take two thick bars with the same carbon content, one of carbon steel and one of alloy steel, and harden them both. At the surface both will reach roughly the same hardness. But in the carbon steel the hardness drops off a few millimeters in, while in the alloy steel it stays high deep into the section. Hardenability is measured with the Jominy test. You take a bar 25 millimeters in diameter, heat it to austenite, and spray a jet of water on one end only.

That end cools very fast, and the further away you go, the slower the cooling. Then you measure hardness at points along the bar, and you get a curve. In 1045 steel the curve drops after just a few millimeters. In 4140 steel it stays high for much longer. This curve tells the designer what diameter can be hardened right through to the core, and with which quench. Why does this happen? To understand it, we use the continuous cooling diagram, CCT. It shows which structure will form at each cooling rate.

The horizontal axis is time, and the vertical axis is temperature. In the middle of the diagram there is a nose-shaped region: if the cooling curve crosses it, pearlite or other soft structures form. If cooling is fast enough to get around the nose, martensite forms. In a carbon steel the nose is very close to the axis, so very fast cooling is needed. Alloying elements delay the breakdown of austenite and push the nose to the right, to longer times.

So even slower cooling, in oil or even in air, is enough to get martensite, even in the middle of a thick section. Now let's meet the elements, one by one. Manganese improves hardenability, and ties up the sulfur. Chromium improves hardenability, and forms hard, wear-resistant carbides. Above 12 percent it turns the steel into stainless steel, as we will see in chapter 10. Nickel greatly improves toughness, especially in the cold.

Molybdenum greatly improves hardenability, reduces sensitivity to temper embrittlement, and improves strength at high temperature. Vanadium refines the grain and forms very hard carbides. Silicon strengthens the ferrite, and is common in spring steels. And boron, in tiny amounts, thousandths of a percent, greatly improves hardenability, which is why it is used in hardened bolts. In the American system, the first two digits tell you which elements the steel contains.

41 is chromium molybdenum. 43 is nickel chromium molybdenum. 86 is nickel chromium molybdenum in smaller amounts. 51 is chromium. And the last two digits are, as in carbon steel, the carbon content in hundredths of a percent. In the European system, the name starts with the carbon content times a hundred, followed by the element symbols. In the name 42CrMo4, the number 42 means 0.42 percent carbon.

The 4 at the end belongs to the first element, chromium, and is divided by four, meaning one percent chromium. Molybdenum appears in the name, but without a number of its own. The factor for chromium, manganese, and nickel is four, and for molybdenum and vanadium it is ten. The two most common alloy steels in the shop are 4140 and 4340. 4140, called 42CrMo4 in Europe, is the workhorse: pins, shafts, gears, and tool holders.

It also comes prehardened, quenched and tempered to about 30 Rockwell C, as we saw in chapter 4. 4340 adds about 1.8 percent nickel. The nickel gives higher toughness and deeper hardenability. 9. Tool Steels

Tool steels are the steels used to machine, form, and cut every other material: dies, punches, die- casting and plastic molds, drills, and taps. They have to combine three properties that are hard to combine: high hardness, wear resistance, and enough toughness not to break. To achieve that, they contain a lot of carbon, and alloying elements such as chromium, molybdenum, tungsten, and vanadium, which form very hard carbides. Each series of tool steels balances these properties differently, according to its use.

In the American system, each series is marked by a letter. W is water hardening. O is oil hardening. A is air hardening. And D is high carbon, high chromium. These four series are cold work steels. S is for shock resistance. H is for hot work. M and T are high speed steels for cutting tools. And P is for plastic molds. 10. Stainless Steels

Stainless steel is steel with at least ten and a half percent chromium. The chromium reacts with the oxygen in the air, and forms a passive layer of chromium oxide on the surface. The layer is very thin, only a few nanometers, and transparent, but it seals the metal off from the environment. And its great advantage: when it is scratched, the passive layer forms again by itself, as long as there is oxygen.

But it is important to remember: stainless steel is not immune to corrosion. It is resistant, and how resistant depends on its composition and the environment. There are five families of stainless steel, according to their crystal structure. Austenitic, with nickel, in the FCC structure. Ferritic, with chromium only, in the BCC structure. Martensitic, with more carbon, which can be hardened. Duplex, which is half austenite and half ferrite. And precipitation hardening stainless, P H.

Each family has completely different properties, so "stainless" on a drawing is an incomplete specification. The most common family is the austenitic one, the 300 series. 304 contains about eighteen percent chromium and eight percent nickel. It is the basic stainless steel: food equipment, kitchens, and general structures. 316 adds two to three percent molybdenum, which greatly improves resistance to chlorides, such as seawater, salt, and cleaning agents.

The nickel keeps the austenitic structure even at room temperature, so this stainless steel is not attracted to a magnet in the soft condition. It also does not harden by heat treatment, only by cold work, and it keeps excellent toughness even at cryogenic temperatures. It has two important variants. 11. Cast Iron

Cast iron is iron with a lot of carbon, usually two and a half to four percent, plus one to three percent silicon. On the iron carbon diagram we met in chapter 3, it sits to the right of the 2.1 percent boundary, close to the eutectic point. So it melts at around 1,150 to 1,250 degrees, a few hundred degrees lower than steel, and it flows well in the mold. This makes it possible to cast complex parts from it, with internal cavities, cheaply.

Engine blocks, pump housings, machine tables, and brake discs are all usually made of cast iron. The difference between the types of cast iron lies not mainly in the composition, but in the form the carbon takes. In most types, the carbon separates out as graphite. And whether the graphite appears as flakes or as spheres changes the properties completely. In gray iron the graphite is in flakes. In ductile iron it is in spheres. In CGI it takes an intermediate form, like short worms.

And in white iron there is no graphite at all, and the carbon is bound up in cementite. Gray cast iron is the most common. The name comes from the color of its fracture surface. The graphite flakes act like tiny notches inside the material. In tension they concentrate stress, so gray iron is brittle, with an elongation of less than one percent, and a relatively low tensile strength. But in compression it is three to four times stronger. And it has advantages that steel does not.

Gray iron absorbs vibration, which is why machine tables are made from it. It conducts heat well, which makes it suitable for brake discs. And the graphite lubricates the tool and breaks the chip, so it is very easy to machine. If a small amount of magnesium is added to the molten iron, just before casting, the graphite rounds up and forms spheres instead of flakes. A sphere does not concentrate stress the way a flake does, so the material becomes ductile. This is ductile cast iron, also called nodular iron.

Its strength is roughly twice that of gray iron, and its elongation can reach fifteen percent or more. That is why it replaces forged steel in many parts: crankshafts, gears, suspension parts, and water pipes. Ductile iron can be strengthened even further with a special heat treatment called austempering. The result is ADI. ADI goes through austempering, and its strength rises a lot, to the level of quenched and tempered steels.

It is about ten percent lighter than steel, and absorbs vibration better. It is used in gears and in parts for agricultural and heavy machinery. In white cast iron, the carbon does not separate into graphite, but stays bound in cementite. This happens with fast cooling, or when there is little silicon. Its fracture is white and shiny. It is very hard and very wear resistant, but very brittle, and it can hardly be machined with cutting tools, only ground. It is used for parts that wear, such as mill liners and slurry pumps.

If white iron is held at high temperature for a long time, the cementite breaks down into graphite in the form of clumps, and the material becomes ductile. This is malleable iron. It used to be common in pipe fittings, and today ductile iron has almost completely replaced it. CGI sits in the middle, between gray and ductile. It is much stronger than gray iron, and still Keeps good thermal conductivity and vibration damping.

That is why it has become the material of choice for the blocks and heads of heavy diesel engines, where the pressures are high. In the European standard, the name of a cast iron tells you the type of graphite. GJL is gray, from lamellar graphite. GJS is ductile, from spheroidal graphite. GJV is CGI, and GJM is malleable. Then come the numbers. In gray iron, GJL-250 means a minimum tensile strength of 250 megapascals.

Ductile iron has two numbers: GJS-400-15 means 400 megapascals, and an elongation of 15 percent. The American standard uses a similar system, but in different units. Machining cast iron is different from machining steel. The casting skin is hard and abrasive, with sand and oxides, so the first pass should cut beneath it, not skate over it.

Gray iron produces graphite dust instead of chips, so it is usually machined dry, with extraction. In thin walls, fast cooling in the casting sometimes creates areas of white iron, hard spots that break tools. And welding cast iron is difficult: it is brittle, cracks easily, and needs preheating and nickel electrodes. What does this mean in the shop? A cast part arrives with machining allowances and casting deviations, so it should be checked before machining.

And watch for holes and porosity that are exposed during machining. And what drives the cost? The casting itself is cheap per kilogram, but it needs a mold and a pattern, and it only pays off in quantity. A requirement for nondestructive testing, for checking the nodularity of ductile iron, or for a heat treatment like ADI, adds cost. And sometimes, in small quantities, a part machined from steel bar is cheaper than a casting.

To sum up: the form of the graphite decides all the properties of cast iron. In the next chapter we move on to the non-ferrous metals, starting with aluminum.

12. Aluminum

Aluminum weighs about a third as much as steel, conducts heat and electricity well, and does not rust, because a thin, hard oxide layer forms on it by itself. As we Saw in chapter 1, its structure is FCC, so it does not become brittle in the cold. But it also has weaknesses. Its modulus of elasticity is about a third of steel's, so an aluminum part bends more. It melts at 660 degrees, and loses strength at temperatures of just a few hundred degrees. And it has no fatigue limit, as we saw in chapter 2.

Aluminum alloys fall into two families, according to how they are strengthened. The first family is heat treatable. It is strengthened by precipitation hardening, as we saw in chapter 4: solution treatment, quenching, and aging. These are the 2000, 6000, and 7000 series. The second family does not respond to heat treatment, and is strengthened only by cold work. These are the 1000, 3000, and 5000 series.

This difference decides everything: the temper, the behavior in welding, and what happens to the part when it is heated. Every alloy has a four-digit number, and the first digit tells you the main alloying element. The 1000 series is pure aluminum, over ninety-nine percent: an excellent conductor and corrosion resistant, but soft. The 2000 series is with copper: strong, fatigue resistant, but sensitive to corrosion.

The 3000 series is with manganese, for sheet and heat exchangers. The 4000 series is with silicon, mainly for welding wire. The 5000 series is with magnesium: very corrosion resistant, and easy to weld. The 6000 series is with magnesium and silicon, the most common series for extrusions and machining. And the 7000 series is with zinc, the strongest of them all. The most common alloy in the shop is 6061 in the T6 condition.

It machines well, resists corrosion, can be welded and anodized, and reaches a yield strength of about 276 megapascals. In Europe the common equivalent is 6082, used for the same purposes, with similar or even slightly higher strength. 6063 is softer, and is used mainly for architectural extrusions. When very high strength is needed, you move to the 2000 and 7000 series.

7075 in the T6 condition reaches a yield strength of about 500 megapascals, close to quenched and tempered steel, at a third of the weight. That is why it is the base material of the aircraft industry, and also common in molds and heavily loaded mechanical parts. 2024 is very fatigue resistant, and is used in aircraft fuselages. But both have weaknesses. Corrosion resistance is low, so 2024 sheet sometimes comes clad with pure aluminum, Alclad. They can hardly be fusion welded. And 7075 in the T6 condition is sensitive to stress corrosion cracking.

So the T73 condition is sometimes used, which has been deliberately overaged: it is slightly less strong, but much more resistant to cracking. The 5000 series is the choice for marine environments and welded structures. 5083 and 5754 are very resistant to seawater, and stay strong even after welding, because they do not depend on heat treatment. Their temper is marked with the letter H. For example, H32 means cold worked and then stabilized, to the quarter hard level.

The temper is part of the name, and without it the specification is incomplete. F is as fabricated, with no control. O is soft, annealed. H is strain hardened by cold work. And T is heat treated. In T tempers, the digit tells you exactly what was done. T3 is solution treated, cold worked, and naturally aged. T4 is solution treated and naturally aged. T5 is cooled from hot working and artificially aged, common in extrusions. T6 is solution treated and artificially aged. And T7 is deliberately overaged.

And now the important part for the shop: the digits 51 that follow, as in T651 or T7351. They mean the material was stretched a few percent after quenching, to relieve internal stresses. Plate or block in the T651 condition distorts much less in machining, so for machined parts you almost always order T651 and not just T6. Alongside the wrought alloys there are casting alloys, which contain a lot of silicon, close to the eutectic point we saw in chapter 3.

A356 is a common casting alloy that can be heat treated to T6, and is used for wheels and equipment housings. In die casting, for instrument housings and automotive parts, alloys such as A380 are used. Aluminum is usually easy to machine, and it can be cut at very high speeds. But in soft aluminum the material sticks to the cutting edge, and the finish suffers. So hard tempers like T6 are preferred, with sharp, polished tools, and good coolant.

For especially fast machining there is 2011, with additions that break the chip. In thin walls aluminum distorts easily, and that is where T651 makes the difference. In welding, as we saw in chapter 4, 6061 loses a large part of its strength next to the weld, and 4043 or 5356 filler wire is used. 13. Copper and Its Alloys

Copper is the best conductor after silver: of electricity and of heat. It conducts electricity about six times better than steel, and one and a half times better than aluminum. That is why almost every electrical conductor, busbar, heat exchanger, and induction coil is made from it. It is corrosion resistant, has an FCC structure, is very ductile, and is easy to form.

But it is heavy, with a density of almost nine grams per cubic centimeter, more than steel, and it is expensive. There are several types of pure copper. The most common is ETP, with a small amount of oxygen. It is cheap and an excellent conductor, but if it is heated in an atmosphere containing hydrogen, for example in brazing, it can crack. For such uses, and for vacuum, oxygen-free copper is used, OFHC.

And water and gas pipes are usually made of DHP copper, which has been deoxidized with phosphorus. Conductivity is measured on the IACS scale, and pure copper sits at around one hundred percent. Almost any addition to copper strengthens it, but lowers its conductivity. So a copper alloy is chosen by the balance between the two properties. An example every plant knows is copper chromium zirconium, which is used to make spot welding electrodes.

It keeps about eighty percent of the conductivity of pure copper, and stays hard even in the heat of welding. Brass, bronze, and beryllium copper sit at the other end of the scale: much stronger, but with a quarter of the conductivity, or even less. In copper and brass too, strength depends on the amount of cold work, so they have tempers as well. There are four main levels: soft, quarter hard, half hard, and hard. In the American standard they are marked with a letter and a number, such as 060 for soft and HO2 for half hard.

In the European standard, the temper is marked with the letter R and the minimum tensile strength, such as R290. It is worth stating the temper in the order, because a soft bar and a hard bar of the same alloy behave completely differently in machining and bending. Brass is copper and zinc. With up to about thirty-five percent zinc, the whole structure is FCC, and the brass is very ductile. 70/30 brass, seventy percent copper and thirty percent zinc, suits deep drawing, such as cartridge cases.

With more zinc, as in 60/40 brass, a second phase forms. The brass is stronger, and good for hot forging, but less ductile in the cold. Zinc is cheaper than copper, so brass is cheaper than pure copper. In the shop, the most common brass is free-cutting brass, C36000, called CW614N in Europe. It contains about three percent lead. The lead breaks the chip into small pieces and acts as a lubricant, so it can be cut at very high speeds with an excellent finish.

On the machinability index for copper alloys, it is the reference, rated one hundred. For comparison, bearing bronzes are around seventy, and pure copper only around twenty. That is why it is the classic material for taps, pipe fittings, and small parts on automatic lathes. Important to know: in contact with drinking water there are now restrictions on lead, and lead-free brass is used.

Brass has two weaknesses. The first: in certain waters brass loses zinc, dezincification. The zinc dissolves, leaving a weak, porous copper skeleton. So for water fittings there is a brass resistant to this, marked DZR. The second: cold-worked brass with internal stresses can crack in the presence of ammonia. This is stress corrosion cracking, once called season cracking.

Stress relief by gentle heating prevents it. Bronze was originally copper and tin, and today the word covers a broad family. Phosphor bronze, with tin and a little phosphorus, is strong and fatigue resistant, and is used for springs, electrical contacts, and bushings. Cast bronzes with tin and lead are the classic material for bearing bushings, because they slide well against steel.

The most common is C93200, also called SAE 660. Its lead is dispersed in tiny droplets, and acts as a lubricant when the oil briefly runs out, so a bronze bearing survives a short dry run much better than a steel one. And aluminum bronze, with about ten percent aluminum and a little iron and nickel, is very strong and resistant to wear and seawater. It is used for ship propellers, pump parts, and heavily loaded bushings.

Cast aluminum bronze, such as C95400, reaches a tensile strength of more than five hundred megapascals. Copper nickel, with ten or thirty percent nickel, is especially resistant to seawater, and also prevents the buildup of marine organisms. That is why it is the standard material for seawater piping and heat exchangers on ships and in desalination plants. And the same alloy is used for coins. Beryllium copper reaches the strength of steel.

It contains less than two percent beryllium, and is strengthened by precipitation hardening, as we saw in chapter 4. It is machined in the soft condition, then aged at a moderate temperature, and it reaches a hardness of about 40 Rockwell C, and a strength of more than 1,200 megapascals. It is used for springs and electrical contacts, and also for non-sparking tools, for places with an explosion hazard. But beryllium dust is dangerous to breathe, so it is ground with extraction, and according to safety procedures.

Ordinary machining, with coolant, is considered safer, but the procedures must still be followed. Copper joins easily by soldering and brazing. In soft soldering, with tin, at around two hundred degrees, water pipes and electrical components are joined. In brazing, with silver alloys or copper phosphorus, at six hundred to nine hundred degrees, the joint is much stronger.

Copper phosphorus alloys clean the surface themselves when brazing copper to copper, but they are not used on steel, because the joint becomes brittle. What does this mean in the shop? Pure copper is soft and gummy, and sticks to the tool, so you work with very sharp tools. Brass, by contrast, is one of the easiest materials to machine. Because of its high conductivity, welding copper needs a lot of heat, so it is often joined by brazing instead. And in contact with aluminum, in electrical connections, galvanic corrosion occurs.

And what drives the cost? Copper itself is expensive, and its price moves with the market. Beryllium copper is far more expensive. On the other hand, copper and brass chips have high scrap value, so it pays to collect them separately, and not mix them with steel chips. To sum up: copper for conductivity, brass for machining, bronze for sliding and seawater, and beryllium copper for strength. In the next chapter we will meet titanium.

14. Titanium

Titanium weighs about 4.5 grams per cubic centimeter, roughly sixty percent of the weight of steel, but its alloys are as strong as quenched and tempered steels. So it has one of the best strength-to-weight ratios there is, and it keeps it up to about 400 degrees. Its passive layer, titanium oxide, is very stable, so it is almost completely resistant to seawater, chlorides, and the human body. But it also has properties that make it hard to work with. Its modulus of elasticity is about half that of steel. Its thermal conductivity is very low, about a seventh of steel's. And it is very expensive, because producing it is complicated and takes a lot of energy.

To produce it, the ore is turned into a chloride, reduced with magnesium, and the resulting sponge is then melted two or three times in a vacuum. That is the main reason for the price. Like iron, titanium has two crystal forms. Below about 880 degrees it has the HCP structure, called the alpha phase. Above that it changes to the BCC structure, called the beta phase. Elements such as aluminum, oxygen, and nitrogen stabilize the alpha phase, and elements such as vanadium and molybdenum stabilize the beta phase. According to the balance between them, the alloys are divided into three families: alpha, alpha beta, and beta.

Commercially pure titanium comes in four grades, from Grade 1 to Grade 4. The difference between them is mainly the amount of oxygen: more oxygen, more strength, and less ductility. 20. Welding and Its Effect on the Material

Welding is really a small casting and a rapid heat treatment, at the same moment. In the middle, in the weld, the metal melts and solidifies again, with a cast structure. Around it is the heat-affected zone, which did not melt, but went through very fast heating and cooling. And beyond it, the base metal, which did not change. The fusion zone, the heat-affected zone, and the base metal are three different materials, within one part. And most welding problems are found in the heat-affected zone. How much heat goes into the material is set by the heat input: the voltage, the current, and the travel speed of the welder. More heat gives a wider affected zone, slower cooling, and coarser grains.

Less heat gives faster cooling, and therefore a harder structure. In steel, the heat-affected zone goes through all the heat treatments we have met, within seconds. Next to the fusion line the grains grow a lot. A little further away they become finer.
