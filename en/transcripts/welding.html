0. Introduction

Look around you: bridges, ships, gas pipelines, car bodies, pressure vessels and machine frames. Every one of them holds hundreds, even thousands of meters of weld. Welding is one of the most important joining methods in industry, and without it most metal structures would simply not exist. Welding is a permanent joint between two or more parts, made by heat, pressure, or both. In most processes, the edges of the parts melt, mix in a common pool, and solidify into one continuous body.

Very often a filler metal is added, such as a wire or an electrode, to fill the gap between the parts. This is the difference from a bolted or riveted joint: there the parts stay separate and are held together mechanically, while in welding they become one body. And it is also the difference from brazing and soldering: there the base metal does not melt at all, and only the filler metal melts and flows between the parts. Forge welding has been known for thousands of years: blacksmiths heated iron in the fire and hammered it until the parts fused. At the end of the nineteenth century the electric arc appeared, and early in the twentieth century the covered electrode was developed.

In the Second World War, welding replaced rivets in shipbuilding and cut construction time dramatically. In the 1940s TIG and MIG were developed, later came electron beam and laser, and in 1991 also Friction Stir Welding (FSW). The advantages are clear: a strong joint, which with proper design is as strong as the base metal, does not leak, and is lighter than a bolted joint. Welding also gives design freedom: a structure can be built from plates and profiles instead of machining it from a solid block. But there is a price: heat causes distortion and residual stress, and changes the material structure in the weld zone.

The joint can't be taken apart, and its quality depends on the welder, the procedure and the inspection. So a good weld is not just a matter of a steady hand, but of design, material, process and control. Every welding process has a number according to ISO 4063, and that number appears on drawings and in welding procedures. For example: 111 is the covered electrode, 135 is MAG, 141 is TIG, and 21 is spot welding. In every chapter we will show the process number right at the start, so you can recognize it on paper too.

The course is built from five groups. In the first we learn the fundamentals: how a weld forms, the electric arc, the types of joints and the welding positions. In the second we meet the arc processes: covered electrode, MIG and MAG, flux-cored wire, TIG, plasma and submerged arc welding. In the third we move on to advanced processes: resistance welding, laser, electron beam, friction welding, brazing and soldering, and robotics. In the fourth we dive into metallurgy: what happens to the material in the weld zone, how to weld steels, aluminum and titanium, and how to control distortion.

And in the fifth we deal with design and quality: welding symbols on drawings, joint calculations, defects, inspection, procedures and qualifications, and finally costs. Every process chapter has the same structure: the principle, equipment, parameters, applications, typical defects, and what drives the cost. In the next chapter we start from the basics: what exactly happens when metal melts and solidifies, and what the heat affected zone is.

1. Welding Fundamentals

In this chapter we will understand what happens inside the weld itself, from the moment the heat arrives until the metal solidifies. Most welding processes in industry are fusion welding: a concentrated heat source melts the edges of the parts, and the molten metal solidifies into one seam. Every such weld has four elements: a heat source, a molten pool, protection of the pool, and very often a filler metal. The heat source can be a gas flame, an electric arc, a laser beam or an electron beam. The important difference between them is how concentrated the heat is, meaning how much power goes into each square millimeter of area.

A flame spreads the heat over a large area: the weld is wide and shallow, and a lot of heat escapes into the surrounding material. An electric arc is far more concentrated, so it melts quickly and produces a narrower, deeper weld. Laser and electron beams are even more concentrated: they form a narrow channel of metal vapor, a keyhole, and weld to great depth in a very narrow seam. The more concentrated the heat, the less heat enters the part, the smaller the affected zone, and the smaller the distortion. Under the heat source a weld pool forms: a small amount of liquid metal, which in steel is hotter than 1,500 degrees.

The pool travels along the joint together with the heat source. At the front of the pool the metal melts, and at its rear it solidifies and forms the weld bead. Inside the pool the metal flows and mixes, so the weld metal is fairly uniform in composition. Solidification starts at the walls of the pool: the grains grow from the fusion line inward, toward the center of the weld. Because of this, the weld center is the last place to solidify, and that is where impurities tend to collect and solidification cracks appear.

The weld metal is a mixture: part of it came from the melted base metal, and part from the filler metal. The share of base metal inside the weld metal is called dilution. For example, if one third of the weld cross-section came from the base metal, the dilution is about 33%. In welding without filler metal, for example TIG without wire, the dilution is 100%. Dilution sets the chemical composition of the weld, so the wire is chosen to suit the mixture, not just the base material.

This is especially critical when welding two different metals, such as stainless steel to carbon steel. If you cut a weld, polish it and etch it with acid, you can see several different zones with the naked eye. In the center is the weld metal: the zone that was liquid and then solidified. Its boundary with the base metal is called the fusion line. Around it is the heat affected zone, the HAZ: metal that did not melt, but got hot enough for its structure to change.

And beyond it lies the base metal, which was not affected. At every point in the HAZ the temperature rises to a peak and falls again, and the closer the point is to the fusion line, the higher the peak. In steel, the zone that was heated above 727 degrees undergoes a structural change. Near the fusion line the grains grow very large, and if cooling is fast, this zone may become hard and brittle. A little farther away the grains are actually refined, and the properties are relatively good.

In hardened aluminum, such as 6061 in the T6 temper, the HAZ softens and loses a large part of its strength. We will expand on this in the metallurgy chapter. For now, remember that the HAZ is often the weak link of the joint. Liquid metal reacts very quickly with air: oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen enter the pool. The result is porosity, oxides and a brittle weld, so every welding process protects the pool in some way. The first way is a shielding gas, such as argon, helium or CO₂ and their mixtures, which pushes the air away from the weld zone.

The second way is flux: a material that melts and forms a layer of slag covering the weld, and also releases protective gases. That is how the covered electrode, flux-cored wire and submerged arc welding work. And the third way is vacuum: in electron beam welding the part usually sits in a chamber emptied of air. An important quantity that ties all this together is heat input: how much heat goes into each millimeter of weld. It is calculated from the voltage, the current and the travel speed, and multiplied by the thermal efficiency of the process.

For example, in MAG welding at 25 volts, 200 amps and a travel speed of 5 millimeters per second, the arc energy is one kilojoule per millimeter. The thermal efficiency of MAG is 0.8, so the heat input is 0.8 kilojoules per millimeter. High heat input widens the HAZ, slows the cooling and increases distortion. Heat input that is too low causes fast cooling, and in some steels a risk of hardening and cracking. That is why many welding procedures limit the heat input to a defined range.

To sum up: a concentrated heat source creates a molten pool, the pool is protected from the air, and it solidifies from the walls toward the center. Around it a HAZ forms, whose properties depend on the material and the heat input. In the next chapter we will meet the most common heat source, the electric arc, and the power sources that feed it.

2. The Electric Arc and Power Sources

In the previous chapter we saw that welding needs a concentrated heat source. In most industrial welding, that source is an electric arc. In this chapter we will understand what the arc is, how it is controlled, and how to choose and operate the power source that feeds it. An electric arc is an electric current flowing through a gas, between the electrode and the workpiece. The high heat turns the gas into plasma: an ionized gas that conducts electricity. Inside the arc the temperature reaches thousands of degrees, far above the melting point of any metal.

The electrical circuit includes the power source, a cable to the electrode, the arc, the workpiece, and a cable back to the machine. In the shop the return cable is often called the "ground", but in fact it is part of the welding circuit, not a safety earth. Welding works at a relatively low voltage and a high current: the arc voltage is about 10 to 40 volts, and the current is tens to hundreds of amps. Before the arc ignites, the machine supplies a higher open-circuit voltage, so that it is easy to strike. The arc voltage depends on its length: a longer arc needs a higher voltage, and spreads the heat over a wider area.

The current mainly determines how much metal melts and how deep the penetration is. The arc is struck by briefly touching the electrode to the work, or without contact, using high-frequency voltage, which is common in TIG. The power source can supply direct current, DC, or alternating current, AC. With direct current, polarity matters: which pole the electrode is connected to. When the electrode is connected to negative, DCEN, in TIG most of the heat goes into the workpiece: penetration is deep and the electrode stays relatively cool.

When the electrode is connected to positive, DCEP, more heat goes to the electrode, and in TIG it gets very hot. In MIG and MAG, on the other hand, you almost always work on DCEP, because that keeps the arc stable and the metal transfer smooth. With alternating current the polarity reverses many times per second, and in the half cycle where the electrode is positive, the arc cleans the oxide layer off the surface. That is why aluminum is TIG welded on alternating current: the cleaning action removes the hard oxide on the aluminum surface. Power sources have two main characteristics: constant current, CC, and constant voltage, CV.

In a constant current source, when the arc length changes, the voltage changes, but the current hardly moves. This suits the covered electrode and TIG, where the welder's hand sets the arc length, and it changes all the time. In a constant voltage source the voltage stays almost constant, and any small change in arc length causes a large change in current. This suits MIG and MAG, where the wire is fed at a constant speed. If the arc gets shorter, the current rises, the wire melts faster and the arc returns to its length. This is called self-regulation.

So in MIG and MAG, it is the wire feed speed that actually sets the current. The older power sources are heavy transformers and rectifiers working at mains frequency. An engine-driven generator is used in the field, where there is no electricity. Today most machines are inverters: they convert the power to a high frequency, so the transformer inside is much smaller and lighter. An inverter controls the current very quickly, which enables synergic programs, pulsing and precise arc control.

Every power source heats up while working, so it is given a duty cycle. The duty cycle is the percentage of a ten-minute period in which the machine can weld at a given current without overheating. For example, 60% at 300 amps means six minutes of welding and four minutes of rest, in every ten minutes. At a lower current, the duty cycle increases: 100%, meaning continuous operation, is reached at about 232 amps. It is important to choose a machine by the actual current and working time, especially in automated and robotic welding.

The arc is dangerous in three main ways: radiation, fumes and electricity. The arc emits strong ultraviolet and infrared radiation, which burns the skin and inflames the cornea of the eye. So welding is done with a helmet and a filter of the right shade, usually between 9 and 13 depending on the current, and people standing nearby are protected too. Welding fumes contain metal oxides, and when welding stainless steel, also hexavalent chromium. Welding fumes are classified as carcinogenic, so local extraction and ventilation are mandatory, and when needed, a respirator as well.

The open-circuit voltage of the machine can cause electric shock, especially in wet conditions or inside a closed vessel. Work with dry gloves and sound insulation, connect the return cable close to the weld area, and check the cables before starting. And don't forget the fire hazard: hot sparks fly for meters, so flammable materials are kept away. To sum up: the arc is a current flowing through ionized gas, at low voltage and high current. Polarity and current type determine where the heat is concentrated, and the machine characteristic, constant current or constant voltage, is matched to the process.

The duty cycle sets how long you can weld continuously, and safety starts with protection from radiation, fumes and electricity. In the next chapter we deal with joints: joint types, edge preparation and welding positions.

3. Joints, Edge Preparation and Positions

Before welding, you have to decide how the parts meet, how the edges are prepared, and in which position the weld will be made. These decisions set the strength, the amount of material, the distortion and the cost of the weld. There are five basic joint types. A butt joint: two parts in the same plane, edge to edge. A T joint: one part perpendicular to the middle of the other.

A corner joint: two parts meeting at their edges at an angle, like the corner of a box. A lap joint: one part lying on top of the other. And an edge joint: two parallel parts whose edges are welded together, mainly in thin sheet. The joint type is the geometry. The weld type is the way it is filled. The two main weld types are the butt weld, which fills a groove between the parts, and the fillet weld, which lies in the corner formed between them.

A full penetration butt weld carries the load through the entire thickness of the part, so it is usually as strong as the material, but it requires preparation and accuracy. A fillet weld is easier and faster to prepare, so it is the most common in structures: in T, lap and corner joints. The size of a fillet weld is defined in two ways: the leg length z, or the throat thickness a, the shortest distance from the root to the face of the triangle. In an equal-leg fillet weld, the throat is about seven tenths of the leg: a 7 millimeter leg gives a throat of about 5. Drawings to ISO usually specify a, and drawings to AWS specify the leg size, so it is important to check what is written.

In a butt weld, a thin part can be welded with square edges, without preparation. In a thick part the arc will not penetrate the full thickness, so the edges are beveled to form a groove. The most common preparation is a V, with an included angle of about sixty degrees. At the bottom of the groove a root gap is left, and often a small flat land, the root face, which supports the pool and keeps the metal from falling through. For thicker plates you move to an X preparation, beveled from both sides.

For example, in a 20 millimeter plate, an X preparation needs about half the filler metal of a V preparation, and the distortion is better balanced. In very thick plates U and J preparations are used, where the walls are almost straight and the groove volume is even smaller. When welding from one side, backing can be added: a strip of metal or ceramic under the root. And when welding from both sides, the root is often cleaned out from the other side by back gouging, before continuing. The preparation is made by machining or by plasma or flame cutting, and then the edges are cleaned of oil, paint and oxides.

Before the welding itself there is a stage that determines much of the quality: the fit-up. At this stage the parts are positioned, the edges are aligned, and you make sure the root gap is uniform along the whole length. A root gap that is too narrow causes lack of penetration, and one that is too wide causes burn-through on the other side and high consumption of filler. A misalignment between the plates also damages the root and concentrates stress. To hold the fit-up during welding, the parts are joined with short tack welds.

A tack weld must be of the same quality as the weld itself, because it is usually absorbed into it. They are usually placed at regular intervals, starting from the center or alternating between both sides, so the gap does not close up. The fit-up can also be held with jigs, clamps and temporary strongbacks. Now back to penetration: not every butt weld needs full penetration. With partial penetration the weld fills only part of the thickness, and the unwelded part remains like a built-in internal crack.

This suits low static loads and saves material, but it is worse under fatigue loads, because the unwelded root concentrates stress. A fillet weld does not have to be continuous either: an intermittent weld consists of short segments, with fixed spaces between them. It saves material and time, and reduces the heat and the distortion, so it is common for stiffeners and sheet joints in light structures. But an intermittent weld does not seal, and the gaps between segments can trap moisture and rust. On the drawing it is specified by the segment length and the spacing between segments, and we will see this in the chapter on welding symbols.

The position is the direction of the weld relative to gravity. ISO 6947 marks the positions with letters: PA is the flat position, and PB is a horizontal fillet weld. PC is a horizontal weld on a vertical wall, and PE is overhead welding. PF and PG are vertical positions: PF is vertical up, and PG is vertical down. The American AWS standard uses a number and a letter: G for a groove weld, and F for a fillet weld.

For example, 1G is the flat position, 2G horizontal, 3G vertical and 4G overhead. For pipes there are also 5G, a fixed horizontal pipe that the welder goes around, and 6G, a pipe inclined at forty-five degrees, which is the hardest position. The position directly affects the work: in the flat position you can weld at high current and speed, while overhead the pool tends to fall. So parts are rotated to the flat position whenever possible, and in welder qualification the position is one of the parameters tested. To sum up: the joint type is the geometry, and the weld type, butt or fillet, is the way it is filled.

Edge preparation is set by the thickness, and the position sets the work rate and the difficulty. In the next chapter we begin the arc processes, with covered electrode welding, process 111.

4. Covered Electrode Welding

Covered electrode welding, process 111, is the oldest and simplest arc process. In English it is called SMAW or MMA, and in the shop simply "stick welding". In this chapter we will look at the principle, the equipment, the parameters, the applications, the typical defects, and what drives its cost. The electrode is a metal rod coated with a layer of mineral material, the flux. The arc forms between the tip of the electrode and the workpiece, and melts both the core wire and the base metal.

The core wire melts and serves as the filler metal, so the electrode gets shorter during welding. The coating burns and melts together with the rod, creating a cloud of gas that shields the arc and the pool. Part of the coating turns into slag, which floats on the pool, protects it while it solidifies and slows the cooling. After welding the slag is removed with a chipping hammer and a wire brush, and only then do you continue to the next layer. The coating has more roles: it stabilizes the arc, adds alloying elements, and keeps oxygen away from the pool.

The coating type determines the character of the electrode. A rutile electrode is easy to use, with a soft arc and a nice bead, and is used for general work. A basic, low hydrogen electrode gives a strong weld that performs well under impact loads and at low temperatures, and resists hydrogen cracking. But it absorbs moisture from the air, so it is baked in an oven according to the manufacturer's instructions, and kept warm until use. A cellulosic electrode gives deep penetration, and is used mainly for root passes in pipelines, in the vertical down position.

In the American AWS standard, an electrode such as E7018 reads like this: seventy is the minimum strength, in thousands of psi. The third digit, one, says the electrode suits all positions, and the last two digits together describe the coating type and the current. E6013 is a common rutile electrode, E7018 is the common basic one, and E6010 is cellulosic. In Europe electrodes are classified to ISO 2560, with a code describing strength, impact test temperature, coating type and hydrogen content. The equipment is simple: a constant current power source, an electrode holder, and a return cable with a clamp.

You can work on direct or alternating current, depending on the electrode type. No gas cylinder is needed, so the equipment is easy to carry and suits field work, work at height and tight spaces. Basic electrodes also need a baking oven and a portable holding oven. And of course a helmet, gloves, a slag hammer and a wire brush. The first parameter is the electrode diameter. The common diameters are 2.5, 3.2 and 4 millimeters.

The current is set by the diameter: a 3.2 millimeter electrode usually runs between about 90 and 140 amps, depending on type and position. Too low a current causes an unstable arc and lack of fusion, and too high a current causes undercut, spatter and overheating of the electrode. The arc length should be about the electrode diameter. An arc that is too long causes porosity and spreads the heat. The electrode is tilted back, at a drag angle of about ten to twenty degrees, so the slag stays behind the arc. Travel speed sets the bead shape: too slow gives a wide, hot bead, and too fast gives a narrow bead with partial penetration.

Every electrode change leaves a stub of a few centimeters, and it is important to start the next electrode correctly, so no defect forms at the tie-in. The covered electrode is the field process: steel structures, bridges, ships, pipelines, and repair and maintenance work. It suits carbon and low alloy steels, stainless steels, cast iron and hardfacing. The advantages: cheap and portable equipment, all-position work, and no gas needed, so you can weld even in the wind. It also reaches places that are hard to get a MIG torch into.

The disadvantages: low productivity, because you change electrodes and remove slag all the time. It requires high skill, and is less suitable for thin sheet and aluminum. And every stop and start of an electrode is a point where a defect can form. The most typical defect is slag inclusion: slag left between layers because it was not cleaned well. Porosity is caused by damp electrodes, an arc that is too long, or a dirty workpiece.

With basic electrodes, porosity sometimes appears at the start of the bead, so the arc is struck a little ahead of the point and brought back. Undercut, a groove along the edge of the bead, is caused by too high a current or a long arc. Lack of fusion is caused by low current, excessive travel speed or a wrong angle. Hydrogen cracks appear mainly in hardenable steels, when damp electrodes are used. And arc strikes form when the arc is struck outside the weld area, and they can become the starting point of a crack.

In covered electrode welding, most of the cost is labor, not material. The arc burns only about a quarter to a third of the working time, and the rest goes to changing electrodes, removing slag and preparation. Only about 60% of the electrode weight becomes weld metal. The rest is stubs, slag and spatter. The deposition rate is low, about one to 2.5 kilograms per arc hour, far less than with wire processes. And with basic electrodes there is also baking, storage, and the waste of electrodes exposed to moisture.

So for long jobs in the shop you usually switch to MIG, MAG or flux-cored wire, and the covered electrode stays for the field, for repairs and for small quantities. To sum up: in the covered electrode, the coating supplies gas, slag and alloying elements. It is a simple, portable and versatile process, but slow and dependent on the welder. In the next chapter we will meet MIG and MAG, the processes that feed the filler metal as a continuous wire.

5. MIG/MAG Welding

MIG/MAG welding, processes 131 and 135, is the most widely used welding process in industry today. Its standard name is GMAW, and in the shop it is sometimes simply called "CO₂ welding". In this chapter we will look at the principle, the shielding gases, the equipment, the metal transfer modes, the parameters, the defects and what drives its cost. Instead of a short electrode, here a continuous metal wire is fed from a spool through the torch. The wire passes through a copper contact tip, which delivers the current from the power source.

The arc forms between the wire tip and the workpiece, and melts the wire and the base metal into a common pool. Shielding gas flows from a cylinder, through the gas nozzle, and protects the arc and the pool from the air. There is no coating and no slag as with the electrode: all the protection comes from the gas. In most work the process is semi-automatic: the machine feeds the wire, and the welder guides the torch. It is usually welded on direct current, with the wire connected to the positive pole.

The difference between 131 and 135 is the shielding gas. In MIG, process 131, the gas is inert: argon, helium or a mixture of them. It does not react with the pool. MIG is used mainly for aluminum, copper and non-ferrous metals. In MAG, process 135, the gas is active: carbon dioxide, or argon with an addition of carbon dioxide or oxygen. The common mixture for steel is 82% argon and 18% carbon dioxide.

Pure carbon dioxide is cheaper and gives deep penetration, but the arc is less stable and there is more spatter. For stainless steel, argon with a few percent of oxygen or carbon dioxide is used. The MIG/MAG power source works at constant voltage, not at constant current as with the electrode. That way the arc length stabilizes by itself: if the torch moves away, the current drops, the wire melts more slowly and the arc shortens back. The wire feeder pushes the wire with drive rolls: a V groove for steel wire, and a U groove for soft aluminum wire.

Inside the torch cable there is a liner for the wire, a hose for the gas and a cable for the current. The common wire diameters are 0.8, 1.0 and 1.2 mm. And alongside them: a gas cylinder with a regulator and flowmeter, and a return cable with a clamp. The most important feature of MIG/MAG is the way metal is transferred from the wire to the pool. In short circuit transfer, short arc, the wire touches the pool and melts in tens to hundreds of short circuits per second.

This happens at low current and voltage, so it suits thin sheet, root passes and all positions. In globular transfer, large droplets fall from the wire into the pool irregularly, with a lot of spatter. In spray transfer, above the transition current, the wire melts into a stream of tiny droplets, with no short circuits. It requires an argon-rich gas, gives high penetration and productivity, and suits thick plates in the flat position. In pulsed transfer, the machine switches the current between a background value and a peak, and each pulse detaches one droplet.

This gives the advantages of spray at lower heat: little spatter, all positions, and it is especially suited to aluminum and stainless steel. The two main settings on the machine are voltage and wire feed speed. The voltage mainly sets the arc length and the bead width. The wire feed speed sets the current and the amount of metal deposited. On synergic machines you select the wire, diameter and gas, and the machine matches the voltage to the feed speed.

The free wire length, the stickout, is usually 10 to 15 mm in short circuit transfer. Too long a stickout reduces the current and the penetration. Gas flow is about 12 to 18 liters per minute. Too high a flow is also harmful, because it creates turbulence that draws in air. With a push angle, the bead is flatter and wider and the penetration shallow. With a drag angle, the penetration is deeper and the bead narrow and high. On aluminum you always push the torch, so the gas leads the arc and protects the surface. MIG/MAG is almost everywhere: steel structures, vehicles, machinery, and above all robotic welding.

The advantages: high productivity, no electrode changes and no slag between layers. It is relatively easy to learn, suits most metals, and is easy to automate. The disadvantages: wind blows the shielding gas away, so outdoors a shelter is needed. Even a wind of about 2 meters per second harms the shielding. The equipment is heavier and more complex, and the torch has to get close to the joint. And with short circuit transfer on thick plates, there is a real risk of lack of fusion that cannot be seen from outside.

The most common defect is porosity from loss of gas shielding: wind, wrong flow, an empty cylinder or a nozzle clogged with spatter. Lack of fusion, cold lap, appears when the voltage is too low or the speed too high: the bead lies on the surface without fusing. On thin sheet, too high a current or too slow a speed causes burn-through: a hole in the sheet. Heavy spatter comes from the wrong voltage, pure carbon dioxide or dirty wire. And there are also feeding faults: a bird's nest, when the wire tangles at the drive rolls, and burnback, when the wire fuses to the contact tip.

Compared with the covered electrode, MIG/MAG is cheaper per kilogram of weld metal, mainly because of the longer arc-on time. The deposition rate is high: about 2 to 5 kg per arc hour, depending on the diameter and the transfer mode. And almost all of the wire becomes weld metal, compared with about 60% of the electrode. What does add cost: shielding gas, especially argon-rich mixtures, and consumables such as contact tips and nozzles. Grinding off spatter is expensive labor time, so the right choice of gas and parameters saves money.

And the biggest saving of all: mechanization and robots, which extend the arc-on time and improve consistency. To sum up: in MIG/MAG a continuous wire and a shielding gas replace the electrode and its coating. The transfer mode, the voltage and the wire feed speed determine the quality of the weld. 6. Flux-Cored Arc Welding

Flux-cored arc welding, processes 136 and 114, combines the continuity of MIG/MAG with the flux of the covered electrode. It is called FCAW, and it is one of the key processes in shipyards, heavy structures and field work. In this chapter we will look at the wire construction, the wire types, the equipment, the parameters, the defects and what drives its cost. A flux-cored wire is a thin metal tube filled with powder. The wire is fed continuously through the torch, exactly as in MIG/MAG.

In the arc, the metal sheath melts and forms most of the weld metal, and the powder inside melts with it. The sheath is very thin, so the current concentrates in it and the wire melts fast. That is the secret of its high productivity. The powder does what the coating does on an electrode: it stabilizes the arc, removes oxygen and adds alloying elements. It also forms a layer of slag that covers the weld and protects it while it cools. And in some wires it even produces the shielding gas itself, so no cylinder is needed.

There are two main families of flux-cored wires. In process 136, the wire works with an active shielding gas, usually carbon dioxide or a mixture of argon and carbon dioxide. The protection is double, from both the gas and the flux, so the weld is high quality and the arc is smooth. In process 114, the wire is self-shielded: the powder generates gas and slag, and there is no gas cylinder at all. This is the field process: structures, pipelines and construction sites, even in the wind.

Here too there are rutile wires, easy to use in all positions, and basic wires, stronger and with less hydrogen. The slag of a rutile wire freezes fast, and supports the pool in vertical and overhead welding. And there is also metal-cored wire, process 138: the powder is mostly metal powder, and there is almost no slag. The equipment is almost identical to MIG/MAG: a constant voltage power source, a wire feeder and a torch. But the drive rolls are knurled or have a special groove, to pull the wire without crushing the tube.

The wire is softer than a solid wire, so the rolls are not tightened too hard. The common wire diameters are larger: 1.2, 1.4 and 1.6 mm. With gas-shielded wires, the wire is usually connected to the positive pole. With most self-shielded wires the polarity is reversed: the wire is on the negative pole. Always check the manufacturer's instructions. And in process 114 there is no cylinder, and you can work with a dedicated torch without a gas nozzle.

Here too the main settings are voltage and wire feed speed. Too high a voltage lengthens the arc, widens the bead and increases the risk of porosity. But the stickout is longer: with gas-shielded wires, about 20 to 25 mm. With self-shielded wires it is especially critical: the heat in the stickout activates the powder, and too short a stickout causes porosity. The well-known rule: if there is slag, you drag.

Dragging at an angle of about 10 to 15 degrees keeps the slag behind the pool and prevents slag trapped inside the weld. With metal-cored wire, without slag, you can push as with solid wire. And in vertical up welding, a rutile wire allows a relatively high current, because the slag holds the pool in place. Flux-cored wires are common in shipyards, heavy steel structures, offshore platforms, pipelines and hardfacing. The big advantage: a high deposition rate, even in difficult positions.

Good penetration, and less sensitivity to rust and mill scale than solid wire. It is also more forgiving for the welder than a covered electrode. And with process 114: working in the wind, without cylinders and without shelters. The disadvantages: slag must be cleaned between layers, as with the electrode. The wire costs more per kilogram than solid wire.

There is much more fume, especially with self-shielded wires, so extraction and ventilation are mandatory. And some wires absorb moisture, like electrodes, so they are stored sealed and dry. The typical defect is slag trapped inside the weld: when the torch is pushed, or the layers are not cleaned well. With self-shielded wires, elongated porosity appears when the stickout is too short or the voltage too high. With gas-shielded wires, gas marks can appear, small pits on the weld surface, from too high a current or from dirt and moisture.

Lack of fusion appears when the speed is too slow, and the pool and slag run ahead of the arc. And a damp wire brings in hydrogen, which can cause cracks in hardenable steels. Most of these defects are avoided with the right angle, the stickout recommended by the manufacturer and good cleaning. Flux-cored wire costs more per kilogram than solid wire, sometimes about twice as much. But most of the cost is labor, and here flux-cored wire wins.

Its deposition rate is high, especially in vertical positions, where solid wire and electrodes are slow. On the other hand, cleaning slag between layers adds time, and the wire efficiency is slightly lower than that of solid wire. With process 114 you save gas, cylinders and wind shelters, but invest more in fume extraction. The bottom line: in thick plates, long seams and difficult positions, flux-cored wire is usually the cheapest per meter of weld. To sum up: a flux-cored wire is a tubular wire with flux inside.

7. TIG Welding

TIG welding, process 141, is the cleanest and most precise manual welding process. Its standard name is GTAW, and in many shops it is simply called "argon welding". In this chapter we will look at the principle, the tungsten electrode, the current types, the technique, the defects and what drives its cost. In TIG the electrode does not melt: it is made of tungsten, and its only job is to hold the arc. The arc forms between the tungsten tip and the workpiece, and melts the base metal.

An inert gas, usually argon, protects the tungsten, the arc and the pool. Filler metal is added separately, usually by hand, as a thin rod dipped into the pool. On thin sheet you can even weld with no filler at all, just by fusing the two edges. In other words: one hand on the torch, the other hand on the rod, and often a foot on the current pedal. The result: no spatter, no slag, and the heat is controlled with great precision.

Tungsten melts only at about 3,400 degrees, so it survives the arc. Oxides are added to the tungsten to make ignition easier and stabilize the arc, and each type is marked by a color. Ceriated is marked gray, lanthanated gold or blue, and thoriated red. Thorium is slightly radioactive, so it is being replaced by other types. For direct current the tungsten is ground to a point, always along the electrode and never across it. A sharp point gives a narrow, focused arc; a blunter point gives a wider arc.

The shielding gas is usually pure argon. Adding helium gives a hotter arc, for aluminum and copper in thick plates. And the gas flows before ignition and continues after the arc goes out, to protect the tungsten and the weld while they cool. Choosing the current in TIG is critical. Most metals are welded on direct current, with the tungsten on the negative pole. That way about two thirds of the heat goes into the workpiece, and the tungsten stays relatively cool. This is the setting for steel, stainless steel, titanium and copper.

For aluminum and magnesium, alternating current is used. In the positive half the arc breaks up the hard oxide layer on the aluminum, and in the negative half it penetrates deep. On modern machines you set the balance between the two halves: more cleaning or more penetration. And on thin sheet, pulsing is used to control the heat and prevent burn-through and distortion. The power source works at constant current, as with the covered electrode.

Ignition is done with high frequency or a gentle lift, without touching the tungsten to the work, so it is not contaminated. The torch is air or water cooled, and at its tip a ceramic cup directs the gas. The current is switched with a button on the torch, or with a foot pedal that lets you change it while welding. The torch is tilted about ten to fifteen degrees from vertical, and pushed forward. The rod is fed at a low angle into the leading edge of the pool, not into the arc.

The tip of the rod always stays inside the gas envelope, so it does not oxidize. And the arc length is very short, about the diameter of the tungsten. A rule of thumb for steel: about 40 amps per millimeter of plate thickness. The common tungsten diameters are 1.6, 2.4 and 3.2 mm, depending on the current. Argon flow is about 6 to 12 liters per minute, depending on the cup size.

On stainless steel and titanium, argon is also purged on the root side, so the inside does not oxidize. At the end of the seam the current is ramped down gradually, to fill the crater and prevent a crack. And above all, cleaning: grease, paint and oxide ruin the weld. TIG is the process of choice for stainless steel, aluminum and titanium. It is used for thin sheet, pipe root passes, and in the aerospace, food and pharmaceutical industries.

The advantages: the highest quality, a clean and good-looking weld, with no spatter and no slag. Precise heat control, all positions, and almost all metals. The disadvantages: it is very slow, and the deposition rate is low. It requires high skill: two hands and a foot working together. It is very sensitive to cleanliness and wind.

And on thick plates it does not pay off on its own. A defect typical of TIG is tungsten inclusion: when the tungsten touches the pool, or the current is too high for its diameter. Porosity is caused by loss of gas, wind, or metal and rod that were not cleaned of oil and moisture. On stainless steel, without a root purge, the inside oxidizes and turns black. That is a defect that rejects piping. Lack of fusion appears when the current is too low, or when rod is added before a pool has formed.

A crater crack appears when the arc is switched off all at once. And touching the rod to the tungsten contaminates it: stop and regrind. TIG is the slowest welding process: about half a kilogram to one kilogram of metal per hour. So almost the entire cost is the labor of a skilled welder. The argon, tungsten and rods are a small part of the cost.

But preparation, cleaning, root purging and regrinding the point add time. The common solution: the root in TIG, and the fill with a faster process, such as MIG/MAG or the electrode. And for long seams in plain steel, TIG is almost never the cheap choice. In series production, mechanized or orbital TIG for pipes shortens the time and keeps the quality consistent. To sum up: in TIG the tungsten does not melt, and the filler is added separately.

Direct current for most metals, and alternating current for aluminum. Maximum quality at the price of time. In the next chapter: plasma arc welding, process 15.

8. Plasma Arc Welding

Plasma arc welding, process 15, is a close relative of TIG, but with a far more concentrated and hotter arc. Its standard name is PAW, and it is used mainly in precision and mechanized production. In this chapter we will look at the principle, the three operating modes, the equipment, the comparison with TIG, the defects and what drives its cost. Think of a water hose: when you press your finger over the end, the jet becomes narrow, fast and strong. Plasma does this to the arc: the tungsten electrode sits inside a copper nozzle with a very small orifice.

The plasma gas, usually argon, flows through this orifice, and the arc is constricted through it. The constricted arc is narrow, stable and concentrated, and the temperature inside it reaches more than twenty thousand degrees. This heat is concentrated on a very small area, so it penetrates deep and fast. Around the nozzle a separate shielding gas flows, which protects the pool, just as in TIG. Ignition starts with a small pilot arc, between the tungsten and the nozzle, lit by high frequency.

When the torch approaches the work, the arc transfers to it. This is the transferred arc, and this is the arc that welds. By the way, plasma cutting works on the same principle, only with a strong gas flow that blows away the molten metal. Plasma welding has three operating modes, according to the current. Microplasma works at tiny currents, from a tenth of an amp up to about fifteen amps. It welds foils and very thin sheet, sensors and bellows, with a precision that is hard to reach with TIG.

In melt-in mode, at medium current, it works like an improved TIG: a more stable arc, and a normal pool. And the most special mode is keyhole. At high current, the plasma jet penetrates the whole thickness of the plate and opens a small hole in it. As the torch moves on, the molten metal flows around the hole and closes behind it, forming a full penetration weld. You can see it from below too: a small plasma jet emerges from the root side during welding.

That way you weld in a single pass, without edge preparation and without filler, up to about eight millimeters in stainless steel. At the end of the seam the current and the plasma gas are ramped down gradually, so the hole closes without leaving a defect. The power source works at constant current, as in TIG, with a control unit for the pilot arc and both gases. The torch is water cooled, and the nozzle is a consumable, available in several orifice diameters. There are more parameters than in TIG: current, orifice diameter, plasma gas flow, shielding gas, travel speed, and how deep the tungsten sits inside the nozzle.

The plasma gas flow is small, usually less than a few liters per minute. Too much gas already starts to cut. For stainless steel a little hydrogen is added to the argon, which heats the arc and leaves a clean weld. And for aluminum and copper, helium additions are used as well. If needed, cold filler wire is added, fed into the pool from the side. Most of the work, and keyhole in particular, is mechanized: a carriage, a track or a robot.

What is the practical difference between plasma and TIG? The TIG arc is wide and cone-shaped, so any change in the distance to the work changes the width of the weld. The plasma arc is almost cylindrical, so it is much less sensitive to distance. The energy is concentrated, so you weld faster, with a narrow heat affected zone and little distortion. And the tungsten is protected inside the nozzle: it never touches the pool, and there are no tungsten inclusions in the weld.

On the other hand, there are more parameters to set, and the torch is larger, so it is hard to reach tight places with it. Plasma is used to produce longitudinally welded stainless tubes, in aerospace, medical equipment and instrumentation. And a version of it with metal powder is used for hardfacing surfaces that wear. The advantages: deep penetration in one pass, and high speed. A narrow weld, little distortion, and excellent repeatability in series production.

And with microplasma: precise control of tiny parts. The disadvantages: the equipment is expensive and complex, and the torch is delicate and needs maintenance. The fit-up between the parts must be precise, because the arc is narrow and does not bridge gaps. And keyhole works mainly in the flat position, and usually only mechanized. A typical keyhole defect is porosity and tunneling, when the keyhole is unstable.

At the end of the seam, without a gradual ramp-down, a hole or crater is left that does not close. Too high a speed causes undercut at the edges of the weld. A gap or misalignment between the parts causes lack of fusion, because the arc simply misses. And there is a defect that damages the equipment: double arcing, when the arc also jumps to the nozzle and burns it. It is caused by too high a current for the orifice, too little plasma gas, or a nozzle that touched the work.

Most of these defects are avoided with precise preparation, proven parameters and a nozzle in good condition. A plasma machine costs about two to three times as much as a TIG machine. Nozzles, tungsten and gas are a running expense, and the nozzle wears over time. But in series work, the speed pays back the investment. One pass instead of several, without edge preparation and without filler metal.

Less distortion, meaning less straightening and machining after welding. But for a one-off repair or manual work, TIG is still cheaper and simpler. So plasma pays off when there are many identical, long seams, in stainless steel or titanium. To sum up: plasma is TIG with a constricted, concentrated and hot arc. Microplasma for tiny parts, and keyhole for full penetration in a single pass.

9. Submerged Arc Welding

Submerged arc welding, process 12, is the productivity champion of the welding world, mainly in thick plates and long seams. Its standard name is SAW, and it is almost always mechanized. In this chapter we will look at the principle, the flux and the wire, the equipment, the parameters, the defects and what drives its cost. A continuous wire is fed into the joint, exactly as in MIG/MAG. But ahead of the arc, the joint is covered with a heap of granular flux.

The arc burns underneath the flux, so you cannot see it at all. There is no flash, almost no fume and no spatter. Because the arc is hidden, no dark helmet is needed; safety glasses are enough. Part of the flux melts and forms a layer of slag that protects the pool and the weld while it cools. The flux that did not melt is vacuumed back and used again.

The process works at very high currents, from a few hundred to more than a thousand amps on a single wire. And the result: deep penetration, a smooth and even bead, and slag that often peels off by itself. The flux is half of the process. There is fused flux, which was melted and crushed, and agglomerated flux, whose grains were bonded together. Alloying elements can be added to agglomerated flux, so it is very widely used.

A basic flux gives good impact toughness and low-temperature performance; an acid flux gives a nice bead and high speed. The flux absorbs moisture, so it is dried and kept dry, exactly like basic electrodes. Damp flux brings hydrogen into the weld, and from there the road to cracking is short. The wire and flux are selected and classified together, as a pair, because both determine the weld composition. Roughly, for every kilogram of wire, about a kilogram of flux is consumed.

The welding head travels on a tractor along a rail, or on a boom that moves over the part. For circumferential seams, the part rotates on turning rolls and the head stays in place. On the welding head there is a wire feeder, a contact tip, and a hopper that lays the flux ahead of the arc. The power source can work on direct or alternating current. To raise productivity, several wires are welded one behind the other, in the same pool.

Or two thin wires together, through the same contact tip. And instead of a wire, you can weld with a wide strip, to clad large areas, for example the inside wall of a pressure vessel. And for very thick plates there is also narrow gap welding, which saves a lot of filler metal. The current mainly sets the penetration. The voltage sets the bead width, and the travel speed sets its size.

The common wire diameters are 2.4, 3.2 and 4 mm. The stickout is relatively long, usually 25 to 35 mm. The flux layer should be just deep enough to cover the arc. Too thin a layer causes flashing and porosity. Too thick a layer spoils the bead shape. The flux spills, so welding is done only in the flat position, or as a horizontal fillet weld.

And the first pass needs backing under the root: a copper bar, a flux bed, or a root made with another process. Submerged arc welding is used in pressure vessels, large pipes, shipbuilding, wind towers, bridges and beams. The big advantage: a very high deposition rate, sometimes tens of kilograms per hour. Deep penetration, meaning fewer passes and less preparation. A plate of about fifteen millimeters can be welded in just two passes, one from each side.

High and consistent quality, no flash, little fume, and no welder fatigue. The disadvantages: only the flat position or a horizontal fillet weld. The equipment is heavy and expensive, and pays off only on long straight or circumferential seams. You cannot see the arc, so the head must be guided precisely along the joint. And the high heat can reduce the impact toughness in the heat affected zone, in some steels.

A classic defect in submerged arc welding is a solidification crack in the center of the weld. It appears when the bead is too deep and narrow, and the metal solidifies from both sides and cracks in the middle. It is avoided when the bead is wider than it is deep: lower the current a little, or raise the voltage. Slag inclusions between layers appear when cleaning is poor, or when there is undercut at the edges of the bead. Porosity is caused by damp flux, rust and oil, or insufficient flux cover.

Without backing, the high current simply burns through the root. And a wire that strays from the joint line causes lack of fusion. That is why seam tracking is used. And with direct current, a magnetic field can deflect the arc. That is why multi-wire systems also use alternating current. The equipment is expensive: a welding head, a tractor or boom, turning rolls, and a flux recovery system. Preparation, alignment and fixturing also take time, before the arc is struck.

But once it is struck, it runs for a long time: the arc-on time is very high, and the deposition rate is enormous. The flux costs money, but a large part of it is recycled. The wire and flux are only a small part of the cost. Here too, time is money. For long seams in thick plates, it is usually the cheapest process per meter of weld. And for small, short jobs or varying positions, it is simply not suitable.

To sum up: in submerged arc welding the arc burns under the flux, at very high currents. Excellent productivity and quality, but only mechanized, and only in the flat position. In the next chapter: resistance welding, processes 21, 22 and 23: spot, seam and projection.

10. Resistance Welding

Resistance welding, processes 21, 22 and 23, welds without an arc, without gas and without filler metal: just current, pressure and time. This is the method that holds most of the world's car bodies together. In this chapter we will look at the principle, the weld cycle, spot, seam and projection welding, the materials, the defects and what drives its cost. Two copper electrodes press the sheets against each other. A very high current passes through them, thousands to tens of thousands of amps, for a fraction of a second.

Every current path has a resistance, and the resistance turns into heat. The heat depends on the square of the current: double the current gives four times the heat. The highest resistance is at the contact between the two sheets, and that is where the metal melts first. A small lens of molten metal forms between the sheets, and it solidifies into a weld nugget. The electrodes are made of a copper alloy and water cooled, so they themselves do not heat up and stick.

The pressure keeps good contact, and stops the molten metal from escaping. Every spot goes through a fixed cycle of four stages. Squeeze: the electrodes close on the sheets and build up force, even before the current. Weld: the current flows, usually for only tenths of a second. Hold: the current stops, but the pressure remains until the nugget solidifies.

Release: the electrodes open, and you move to the next spot. The three main parameters are current, time and electrode force. Too low a force causes expulsion; too high a force reduces the contact resistance, and the nugget comes out small. A rule of thumb for the nugget diameter: five times the square root of the sheet thickness in millimeters. For example, for a one millimeter sheet, a nugget of about five millimeters in diameter.

Spot welding, process 21, is the most common. The body of a single car holds thousands of spot welds, and most of them are made by robots. On a robot, the welding gun sits on the arm, and moves from spot to spot. In the workshop a pedestal machine is used, where the part is brought to the electrodes. Modern power sources are medium frequency inverters, which deliver a controlled and precise direct current.

The electrode tips wear and mushroom, so they are dressed regularly. In seam welding, process 22, the electrodes are rotating wheels. The current flows in pulses, and the spots overlap to form a continuous, leak-tight seam. That is how fuel tanks, drums and cans are welded, which must be tight to liquid and gas. In projection welding, process 23, small projections are embossed in one of the parts.

The projections concentrate the current, so several spots can be welded at once. That is how nuts and studs are welded to sheet metal, on a production line, in a single second. And there is also resistance butt welding, for joining bars, chains and railway rails. In flash welding, the ends are heated by sparks and then forced together, and the molten metal is squeezed out. Low carbon steel is the easiest material for resistance welding.

Galvanized sheet needs a higher current, and the zinc wears the electrodes quickly. Stainless steel welds well, because its resistance is high, so less current is needed. Aluminum is the challenge: it conducts very well, so it needs enormous currents, and it sticks to the electrodes. And high strength steels need high force and precise control, so the nugget is not brittle. And with all materials, the sheets must be clean: oil, paint and rust change the resistance and the result.

The advantages: very fast, a fraction of a second per spot. No filler metal, no gas and no edge preparation. Little distortion, and full automation with ease. The disadvantages: only lap joints, which add weight and material. Spot welding needs access from both sides of the part.

The thickness is limited, usually to sheets of a few millimeters. And it is hard to test a spot without destroying it. The common defect is a nugget that is too small, or a stuck weld that never melted at all. It is caused by low current, short time, or worn electrodes. A mushroomed electrode spreads the current over a larger area, and the nugget gets smaller. That is why machines step the current up gradually over the life of the electrode.

Too high a current, or too low a force, causes expulsion: molten metal ejected from the nugget. Too deep an electrode indentation in the sheet weakens the joint and spoils the appearance. Spots that are too close together cause current shunting: part of the current escapes through the previous spot. Quality is checked by destructive tests: peeling or chiseling the sheets apart, and measuring the nugget diameter. And in production, ultrasonic testing and monitoring of the current during welding are also used.

Machines and robots cost a lot, and draw high electrical power. Consumable electrodes and regular tip dressing are a fixed expense. But the cost per spot is very low: a fraction of a second, with no filler metal and no gas. The overlap flanges add some material and weight, and that is a price paid in the design. So it pays to design the product for resistance welding from the start: flanges, access and spot spacing.

In mass production of sheet metal, there is hardly a cheaper or faster method. To sum up: resistance welding is current, pressure and time, with no arc and no filler. 11. Laser Beam Welding

Laser beam welding, process 52, concentrates enormous energy on a very small spot, using a beam of light. It is fast, precise and almost distortion-free, so today it dominates the automotive, battery and medical device industries. In this chapter we will look at the principle, the laser types, the equipment and parameters, hybrid welding, the materials, the defects and what drives its cost. A laser beam is concentrated light, at a single wavelength, that can be focused by a lens. The lens focuses the beam to a spot a few tenths of a millimeter in diameter.

The power density at this spot reaches millions of watts per square centimeter. At a relatively low density, the laser heats the surface, and the heat spreads inward by conduction. The weld is wide and shallow, as in TIG. At a high density, the metal vaporizes, and a narrow hole full of vapor opens in it: a keyhole. The beam penetrates deep through the hole, and the molten metal closes behind it, just as in plasma welding. The result: a narrow, deep weld, whose depth can reach ten times its width.

A shielding gas, argon, helium or nitrogen, protects the pool and disperses the vapor plume. And there is no contact between the tool and the part: no electrode that wears, and no force on the part. There are several main types of laser sources. The carbon dioxide laser is the oldest: a gas laser whose beam is delivered by mirrors. Today fiber lasers and disk lasers dominate: efficient, compact, and their beam travels through a flexible optical fiber straight to the robot head.

Their efficiency is far higher than that of the carbon dioxide laser, so they save electricity. Diode lasers are used for conduction welding and brazing, with a wide and uniform spot. Short-pulse lasers are used for micro welding: jewelry, medical devices and electronics. Power ranges from tens of watts up to tens of thousands of watts. The laser head includes a lens that collimates the beam, a focusing lens, and a protective window that is replaced when it gets dirty.

A crossjet, an air knife under the lens, blows away spatter and fumes before they reach the window. In remote welding, a scanner with mirrors moves the beam quickly, and the robot does not need to travel to every seam. The main parameters are power, speed, focal position and spot size. With wobble, a small fast oscillation of the beam, the weld is widened and small gaps are filled. But the fit-up must be very precise: the allowed gap is usually a few tenths of a millimeter.

So the parts are clamped firmly, and seam tracking is used. Hybrid welding combines a laser with a MIG/MAG arc in the same pool. The laser provides deep penetration, and the arc adds filler metal and bridges gaps. That way thicker plates are welded, faster, and with less demanding preparation. The method is common in shipyards, large panels and pipelines.

Steels and stainless steels weld excellently with a laser. Aluminum reflects a large part of the beam, and tends to hot cracking, so it needs high power and sometimes filler metal. Copper reflects almost all infrared light, so green or blue lasers are used for it. A laser can also weld dissimilar metals to each other, for example copper and aluminum tabs in batteries. And titanium welds well, as long as it is shielded with gas even after the beam has passed.

In galvanized sheet in a lap joint, the zinc vapor is trapped between the sheets and causes porosity, so a tiny gap is left between them. Lasers are used in car bodies, roofs and tailored blanks, batteries, transmissions, medical devices and jewelry. The advantages: speeds of meters per minute. A very narrow heat affected zone, and minimal distortion. No filler metal, access from one side only, and full automation.

The disadvantages: very expensive equipment. Very high precision required in preparation and fixturing. A safety hazard: the beam is invisible and damages the eyes, so work is done in a closed, protected cell. And the fast cooling can create a hard, brittle zone in high carbon steels. Porosity appears when the keyhole collapses and traps gas, especially at deep penetration.

Spatter and an underfilled bead are caused by too much power, or the wrong focus. Because the beam is narrow, a small deviation from the joint line causes lack of fusion. Hot cracks appear in aluminum and in steels with sulfur, when the metal solidifies quickly. And at too high a speed, humping appears: the bead breaks into lumps instead of a smooth seam. And a drifting focus, or a dirty protective window, causes partial penetration.

Most of these defects are detected by monitoring: cameras and sensors that follow the pool in real time. The investment is very high: a laser source, a robot, optics and a closed safety cell. But the running cost is low: no filler metal, low energy consumption per meter, and very high speed. Less distortion, meaning less straightening and machining after welding. On the other hand, the required precision shifts cost into preparation: accurate cutting, fixturing and control.

So a laser pays off mainly in automated mass production. And in one-off work, preparation and setup take longer than the welding itself. To sum up: a laser is energy concentrated in a beam of light, in conduction or keyhole mode. Fast, precise and distortion-free, but it needs a high investment and precise preparation. 12. Electron Beam Welding

Electron beam welding, process 51, welds with a beam of fast electrons, inside a vacuum chamber. This is the process with the deepest penetration: a single weld can join steel more than 100 mm thick. In this chapter we will look at the principle, the machine, the parameters, the vacuum levels, the materials, the defects and what drives its cost. In the electron gun, a heated cathode releases electrons. A high voltage, usually between 30 and 200 kilovolts, accelerates them to about half the speed of light.

A magnetic coil focuses the beam to a spot of a few tenths of a millimeter, and deflection coils move it quickly. When the electrons hit the metal, their energy turns into heat, all at once. The power density is very high, and the metal vaporizes and opens a keyhole, as with a laser. The hole penetrates deep, and the weld comes out very narrow: the depth can reach 25 times the width. Almost all the energy goes into the part, so the process is very energy efficient.

And why a vacuum? Because in air, the electrons collide with gas molecules and scatter. The vacuum also protects the metal from oxygen and nitrogen, without any shielding gas. The machine includes the electron gun, a high voltage supply, a vacuum chamber and pumps. The parts sit in the chamber on a moving table, or on a fixture that rotates them, and the gun stays in place. Before every weld the air is pumped out of the chamber, which takes from a few seconds to many minutes, depending on the chamber size.

When the electrons hit the metal, X-rays are also produced, so the chamber walls are made of thick steel, and sometimes lead as well. The operator watches the weld through a shielded window or a camera, and never comes near the beam. And the same beam is also used for seam finding: it scans the part and locates the joint line before welding. The machines are computer controlled, and every weld repeats exactly the same parameters. The main parameters are the accelerating voltage, the beam current, the welding speed and the focal position.

The beam power is the voltage times the current: for example, 60 kilovolts times 100 milliamps is 6 kilowatts. More power, or less speed, gives deeper penetration. A focus slightly below the surface usually gives the best penetration. The deflection coils can move the beam in a small pattern, such as a circle, to stabilize the pool and widen the weld. At the end of the seam the current is ramped down gradually, so the hole closes without a cavity.

The preparation is similar to laser: a square butt joint, no groove, with a tight fit-up and no filler metal. There are three main operating levels. High vacuum gives the sharpest beam and the deepest penetration, and this is the common method. In partial vacuum, pumping is faster and the beam slightly wider, which suits series production, for example automotive gears. And there is also non-vacuum welding, in air: the distance to the part must be very short, and the penetration is smaller.

In ISO numbering, vacuum welding is process 511, and in air it is process 512. Thanks to the vacuum, this is the preferred process for metals that react easily with air, such as titanium and zirconium. And also for metals with a very high melting point: tungsten, molybdenum, niobium and tantalum. Copper welds better than with a laser, because the electrons are not reflected from the surface the way light is. Dissimilar metals can also be welded, such as copper to steel, or copper to niobium.

Steels weld well, but residual magnetism must be removed, otherwise the magnetic field deflects the beam. And metals that vaporize easily, such as zinc, cadmium and magnesium, as well as brass and galvanized sheet, are not suitable: the vapors contaminate the chamber and cause porosity. The process is used in aerospace, jet engine parts, the nuclear industry, particle accelerators and gearbox gears. The advantages: deep penetration in a single pass, instead of dozens of arc passes. A very narrow heat affected zone and minimal distortion, so even finish-machined parts can be welded.

A very clean weld thanks to the vacuum, with no filler metal and no gas. And a computer controlled process that repeats itself exactly. The disadvantages: very expensive equipment, and the part size is limited by the chamber size. Pump-down time before every weld, and a very tight fit-up between the parts. X-rays that require shielding, and fast cooling that can create a hard, brittle zone in high carbon steels.

The most dangerous defect: the beam misses the joint line. The weld looks fine from above, but below there is lack of fusion. This happens because of residual magnetism, or an inaccurate fit-up, especially in thick parts. With partial penetration, the root comes out uneven, with sharp spikes and porosity at the bottom. This is called spiking. Porosity also forms from dirt, oil, or the vapor of metals that vaporize easily. At too high a power, metal is ejected from the pool, leaving an underfilled weld.

And at the end of the seam, if the beam is switched off too quickly, a cavity or crack is left. So welds are checked by radiography or ultrasonics, and in series production sample parts are also sectioned. The investment is very high: a gun, a high voltage supply, a vacuum chamber, pumps and shielding. The pump-down time is added to every cycle, so several parts are loaded into the chamber together. The preparation is expensive: precise machining of the edges, cleaning and demagnetizing.

But in thick parts, a single pass replaces dozens of passes, with no filler metal and no gas. And less distortion, meaning less straightening and machining after welding. So the electron beam pays off for thick, expensive or critical parts, and in large series production. To sum up: a beam of fast electrons inside a vacuum, welding to great depth in a narrow seam. Clean, precise and distortion-free, but it needs expensive equipment, a vacuum chamber and a precise fit-up.

13. Friction Welding

Friction welding, processes 42 and 43, joins metals without melting them. The heat comes from friction, the metal softens, and high pressure joins the parts into one body. In this chapter we will look at rotary friction welding, linear friction welding, FSW, the materials, the defects and what drives their cost. In rotary friction welding, one part spins fast, and the other part is held still. They are pressed against each other, and the friction heats the contact faces within seconds.

The metal gets very hot, but does not melt: it softens, like modeling clay. The soft material is pushed outward, taking the oxides and dirt from the surface with it. Then the rotation stops and the pressure is increased: this is the forging stage. The parts get slightly shorter, and around the joint a ring of expelled material forms, called the flash. The result: a bond across the whole cross-section, with no molten pool, and therefore no porosity and no hot cracks.

The whole cycle takes just a few seconds, and it is fully automatic. There are two main drive methods. With direct drive, a motor spins the part at a constant speed, and at the end a brake stops it. With the inertia method, a flywheel is spun up, the motor is disconnected, and the energy stored in the flywheel performs the whole weld. In ISO numbering, direct drive is process 421, and inertia is process 422.

The main parameters are rotation speed, friction pressure, forge pressure, and the friction time or the amount of upset. The machine measures the upset in every weld, and that is also a control tool: if it deviates, the part is suspect. At least one of the parts has to be round, such as a bar or a tube, so that it can be rotated. And finally the flash is removed by turning, or by a cutting tool inside the machine. In linear friction welding, the part does not rotate, but moves back and forth in a short, fast stroke.

That way, parts that are not round can be welded too. The famous application: joining blades to a disk in jet engines, which is called a blisk. No bolts and no locking slots: the disk and the blades become one part, lighter and stronger. FSW, process 43, was developed in England in 1991, and it changed aluminum welding. Here the tool rotates, not the parts: a tool with a wide shoulder and a short pin at its tip.

The two plates are butted together, and clamped firmly onto a support plate, the backing. The rotating tool plunges into the joint line, until the shoulder touches the surface of the plates. The friction, mainly under the shoulder, softens the metal around the pin. The tool travels along the seam, and the pin stirs the material from both sides and moves it backward. The shoulder presses from above and forges the material, leaving behind a solid weld, with a surface of arc-shaped ripples.

The parameters: rotation speed, travel speed, downward force, and a small tilt angle of the tool. The forces are very large, so a stiff machine or a strong robot is needed, and firm clamping. And at the end of the seam, when the tool withdraws, a hole is left. So you finish on a run-off tab that is cut away later, or use a retractable pin. FSW excels in aluminum, including alloys such as 2024 and 7075, which can hardly be fusion welded because of hot cracking. Copper and magnesium also weld well.

Steel and titanium are possible, but they need tools made of hard, expensive materials, which wear quickly. With rotary welding almost any combination can be joined: steel to aluminum, copper to aluminum, and nickel alloys to steel. Because without melting, far fewer brittle compounds form than those that appear when the two metals melt together. And materials that are hard to heat by friction, such as cast iron with graphite, are less suitable. Rotary welding is used for drive shafts and axles, engine valves, hydraulic piston rods, drill pipes and turbocharger shafts.

FSW is used for ship deck panels, railway carriages, rocket fuel tanks and battery trays for electric vehicles. The advantages: no melting, so no porosity, no hot cracks and no spatter. High strength, little distortion, and good properties in the joint zone. No filler metal, no shielding gas and no welding fume, and full repeatability. The disadvantages: heavy, expensive machines, and large forces that require firm clamping.

Joint shapes are limited: in rotary welding a round part, and in FSW mainly straight, simple seams. And in rotary welding there is material loss in the upset, and the flash has to be removed. In FSW, insufficient heat or pressure creates a long tunnel-shaped cavity inside the weld, usually on one side of it. A pin that is too short leaves an unbonded line at the root. This is called a kissing bond, and it is very hard to detect. Too much heat creates a lot of flash at the sides, a rough surface and a weld that is too thin.

In rotary welding, insufficient pressure or time leaves an unbonded area, usually at the center of the cross-section. And dirt or paint on the contact faces, or too short a friction time, leave oxides inside the joint. So the process itself is controlled: speed, forces and upset are measured and recorded for every weld. And samples are checked by sectioning, bend testing and ultrasonics. The investment is high: a heavy, stiff machine, with a powerful hydraulic system.

But in rotary welding the cycle takes seconds, with no consumables, so the cost per joint in mass production is very low. Extra length must be added to the parts for the upset, and the flash removed by machining. In FSW the tool and the fixturing are expensive, and the speed in steel is low. On the other hand, in aluminum it saves edge preparation, straightening after welding and defect repairs. So friction welding pays off in series production, in critical joints, and in joining dissimilar metals.

To sum up: friction welding joins in the solid state, with frictional heat and forging pressure. Rotary for round parts, linear for blades, and FSW for aluminum plates. In the next chapter: soldering and brazing, where the base metal does not melt at all.

14. Soldering and Brazing

Soldering and brazing join metals with a filler metal that melts, while the base metal stays solid. The dividing line is 450 degrees: below it is soldering, and above it is brazing. In this chapter we will look at the principle, the two types, the heating methods, the materials, the defects and what drives the cost. For the joint to succeed, the molten filler metal has to wet the metal surface: spread over it, and not bead up like a drop of water on oil. Good wetting is possible only on clean metal, without an oxide layer.

That is why flux is used: it removes the oxides, and protects the surface during heating. The gap between the parts is very narrow, and the filler metal is drawn into it by itself, by capillary action, like water in a thin tube. In brazing, the typical gap is between 0.05 and 0.15 mm. Too large a gap weakens the capillary force, and too small a gap does not let the metal in. At the contact surface a thin layer forms where the metals intermix, and that is what holds the joint.

And because of the large contact area, a lap joint can be stronger than the filler metal itself. Soldering, process 94, works below 450 degrees, usually around 200 to 250. The filler metal is tin based: once tin-lead, and today mainly lead-free alloys, with a little silver and copper. It is used in electronics, copper water piping, radiators and food cans. Heating is done with a soldering iron, a flame, or a reflow oven, where hundreds of components on a board are soldered at once.

In electronics the flux is mild, rosin based. In plumbing an acid flux is used, which must be cleaned off afterward. The strength of a soldered joint is low, so it suits sealing and electrical connections, not load-bearing parts. And it is not suitable for high temperatures, because the filler metal softens. Brazing, process 91, works above 450 degrees, usually between 600 and 1100. Silver-based filler metals melt at around 600 to 800 degrees, flow well, and suit almost any metal.

Copper-phosphorus is used to join copper to copper, without flux, because the phosphorus cleans the oxides by itself. Brass and bronze are cheaper, and are used for steel and cast iron. Nickel-based filler metals work above 1000 degrees, usually in a vacuum furnace, for hot parts in engines. And for aluminum there is an aluminum-silicon filler metal, which melts only slightly below aluminum itself, so precise heat control is needed. The common joint is a lap joint, with an overlap of about 3 times the thickness of the thinner part.

The filler metal is placed on one side, and heated until it is drawn through to the other side. There are several main heating methods. Torch brazing, done by hand: flexible and cheap, but the quality depends heavily on the operator. In induction brazing, a coil heats only the joint area, within seconds, and the same way every time. Suited to series production. In a furnace, with a controlled atmosphere or in vacuum, hundreds of joints are brazed at once, and in vacuum even without flux.

There is also dip brazing in a bath, and resistance brazing with electrical heating. And there is braze welding, process 97: the filler metal is laid like a weld bead, without capillary action. That is how cast iron is repaired with bronze, for example. Brazing easily joins dissimilar metals: copper to steel, brass to stainless steel. That is how carbide tips are joined to a steel body, in cutting tools, drills and saws. With special filler metals even ceramics can be joined to metal.

Aluminum is harder, because of its tough oxide layer, and stainless steel needs an especially active flux or a vacuum furnace. And some silver filler metals once contained cadmium. It is toxic, and today cadmium-free alloys are used. Brazing is used in air conditioning and refrigeration piping, heat exchangers, car radiators, cutting tools, jewelry and bicycle frames. The advantages: lower heat than welding, so less distortion and less change in the properties of the base metal. Dissimilar metals and very thin parts can be joined.

In a furnace many joints are brazed at once, and the joints are clean and leak-tight, with almost no machining afterward. The disadvantages: the strength is lower than welding, especially in soldering. The service temperature of the joint is limited by the filler metal. A precise gap and very clean surfaces are needed, and flux residues can cause corrosion. And in joints of dissimilar metals, galvanic corrosion can develop in a humid environment.

The common defect is incomplete fill: voids inside the overlap, from a wrong gap or uneven heating. Flux that did not escape stays trapped in the joint, weakens it and causes corrosion. Poor wetting, when the filler metal beads up and does not spread, comes from a dirty surface or the wrong flux. Overheating burns the flux, and the filler metal starts to dissolve and erode the base metal. In electronics, a cold solder joint looks gray and dull, and the electrical contact is unreliable.

And when brazing carbide to steel, cracks can appear, because the two metals contract at different rates during cooling. Joints are checked visually, to see the metal came out on the other side of the overlap, and by leak testing, ultrasonics, radiography and sectioning. Silver-based filler metal is expensive, and the price rises with the silver content. Copper-phosphorus and brass are much cheaper. Manual torch brazing takes labor and skill, and every joint takes time. A vacuum furnace is a high investment, and a cycle takes hours, but each cycle brazes hundreds of joints, so the cost per joint is low.

Induction needs a coil made for each part, but it pays off in series production. Preparation adds cost: cleaning, machining to a precise gap, and removing the flux after brazing. So brazing pays off for thin parts, for joining dissimilar metals, and for many small joints in series production. To sum up: in soldering and brazing only the filler metal melts, and it is drawn into a narrow gap by capillary action. Soldering below 450 degrees, for electronics and sealing. Brazing above 450, for strong joints.

15. Robotic Welding and Automation

Robotic welding and automation: how a machine welds thousands of seams, at the same quality, shift after shift. Most robotic welding in the world is MIG/MAG, but robots also work with TIG, laser and spot welding. In this chapter we will look at the levels of automation, the welding cell, programming and sensors, the common problems and what drives the cost. There are several levels of automation in welding. In manual welding, the welder does everything: moves the torch, and with a covered electrode also feeds the electrode in as it burns.

In semi-automatic welding, like ordinary MIG/MAG, the machine feeds the wire, and the welder moves the torch by hand. In mechanized welding, a mechanism moves the torch, and the operator watches and corrects: a carriage traveling on a rail, an orbital head around a pipe, or a boom over a submerged arc machine. In automatic welding, the machine performs the whole cycle on its own, and the operator only loads and unloads parts. And robotic welding is flexible automation: the same robot can weld different parts, you just change the program. The higher the level of automation, the more consistent the quality, but the greater the demand for accurate, consistent parts.

A robotic welding cell includes a robot arm, usually with six axes, which reaches almost any angle. The controller drives the arm, and talks to the power source and the wire feeder, to change parameters along the seam. On the torch there is a collision sensor, which stops the robot if the torch hits something. A positioner moves the part: it rotates and tilts it, so the seam is always in the most convenient position, usually flat. Fixtures hold the parts in exactly the same place, in every cycle.

At a cleaning station, the robot cleans spatter from the nozzle, sprays anti-spatter, and cuts the wire tip. And all of it inside a safety fence with gates and sensors, and dark screens against the arc radiation. Many cells have two stations: the robot welds on one side, while the operator loads a new part on the other. The common way to program is with a teach pendant: you move the robot point by point, and store each position. At each point you also define the torch angle, the speed, and the welding parameters.

With collaborative robots, cobots, you can simply grab the arm by hand, and guide it along the seam. In offline programming the program is written on a computer, on a 3D model of the part, without stopping production. The simulation shows in advance collisions, points that cannot be reached, and the cycle time. And in any case, the torch tip, the TCP, must be calibrated, otherwise all the points shift together. A robot does not see like a welder. If the part moves slightly, it will weld in the air. That is why sensors are used.

With touch sensing, the robot touches the part with the wire tip, at low voltage, and finds the position of the walls before welding. With through-arc seam tracking, the torch weaves from side to side, and the controller measures the changes in current, and corrects the path while welding. A laser sensor running ahead of the torch scans the groove, and corrects both the position and the parameters according to the gap width. Monitoring systems record current, voltage and speed on every seam, and warn of deviations. That way every part has a full record of how it was welded.

Automotive is the familiar example: car body lines, with hundreds of spot welding robots. MIG/MAG robots weld frames, seats, exhaust systems, agricultural machinery and heavy equipment. Mechanized welding is used for pipelines, tanks and large structures, where a robot cannot reach everywhere. Collaborative robots are entering small workshops, for short series, because they are easy to program and need no full fence. The advantages: consistent quality, seam after seam.

The arc is on for a larger share of the time: on a robot about 60 to 80 percent, compared with 20 to 30 in manual work. Higher speed, less spatter, and less cleaning and repair work. The welder leaves the zone of fume and radiation, and works as an operator and programmer. The disadvantages: a high investment in the cell, the fixtures and the programming. The robot needs accurate, consistent parts. It does not compensate for a crooked cut the way a welder does.

In small series, programming time and fixture preparation can cost more than the welding itself. And you need people who know both welding and robotics, and they are not easy to find. The most common problem: the seam is out of position, because the parts are inconsistent, or the torch or fixture moved. A gap that varies from part to part causes burn-through in thin parts, or lack of fusion in a narrow gap. A nozzle clogged with spatter harms the gas shielding, and porosity forms.

Wire that does not feed smoothly, because of a worn liner or old consumables, produces an uneven bead. A torch bent in a collision, without recalibration, shifts all the seams. And a robot repeats the same mistake on every part, so a single defect quickly becomes a defective batch. The solution: first-part inspection, monitoring, regular maintenance, and sample testing. A robotic cell costs more than just a robot: also a power source, a positioner, a safety fence, fixtures, programming and training.

The fixtures alone can cost as much as the robot, and every new part needs a new fixture. The return on investment depends mainly on volume: how many identical parts, how many shifts, and how many years. And also on the accuracy of the parts: precise cutting and bending save sensors and repairs. A big saving comes from high arc-on time, and fewer repairs and less cleaning. So a robot pays off in medium and large series, and cobots or offline programming lower the threshold for small series too.

To sum up: from manual welding, through semi-automatic and mechanized, to the flexible robot. The robot gives consistent quality and high arc-on time, but needs accurate parts, fixtures, sensors and programming. 16. Welding Metallurgy

In this chapter we dive inside: what happens to the structure of the metal in the weld zone, and why most cracks are born there. In chapter one we met the weld pool and the HAZ. Now we will understand the mechanisms behind them. We will go through the thermal cycle, the zones of the HAZ, the structural changes in steel, the CE, hydrogen cracks, hot cracks, and the methods of control. Every point near the weld goes through a thermal cycle: it heats up fast to a peak temperature, and then cools down. The farther the point is from the fusion line, the lower its peak temperature.

But the peak is not the only thing that sets the structure. The cooling rate matters just as much. In steels it is measured by the cooling time from eight hundred to five hundred degrees, called t8/5. A short time means fast cooling, and a long time means slow cooling. High heat input lengthens the cooling time, and preheating lengthens it even more. And a thick plate cools faster than a thin one, because the heat escapes in all directions.

In steel, the HAZ is divided into several zones, according to the peak temperature. Right next to the fusion line, above 1,100 degrees, the grains grow very large: this is the coarse grain zone. Coarse grains lower the fracture toughness, and with fast cooling this zone also hardens. So it is usually the weakest spot. A little farther away, around 900 degrees, the grains are refined, and the properties are actually good. Between 727 and 900 degrees, only part of the structure transforms: this is the intercritical zone.

And below 727 degrees, the structure does not transform, but a steel that was hardened may soften there. In multi-pass welding, each pass reheats the one before it, and sometimes improves its structure. Above 727 degrees, steel turns into austenite. What happens to the austenite on cooling depends on the rate. With slow cooling, ferrite and pearlite form: a soft, ductile structure.

With faster cooling, bainite forms: stronger and harder. And with very fast cooling, martensite forms: very hard, but brittle. The more carbon in the steel, the harder and more brittle the martensite, and the easier it is to form. Alloying elements such as manganese, chromium and molybdenum also promote martensite. This is the hardenability of the steel. To estimate the risk in advance, the carbon equivalent, CE, is used.

The formula adds to the carbon part of the effect of manganese, chromium, molybdenum, vanadium, nickel and copper. Below four tenths, the steel is usually easy to weld, without preheat in ordinary thicknesses. Between four and six tenths preheat is needed, and the higher the CE and the thickness, the higher the temperature. And above six tenths, the steel is difficult to weld, and requires a special procedure. The most dangerous crack in steels is the hydrogen crack, also called a cold crack.

It forms only when three conditions occur together: hydrogen in the weld, a hard structure, and stress. The hydrogen comes from moisture in the electrode coating or the flux, from oil, from rust, and from humid air. The arc breaks down the water, and the hydrogen enters the pool, and then diffuses into the HAZ. The crack usually appears in the HAZ, under the weld or at the weld toe, and sometimes only hours or days after welding. So in sensitive steels the weld is inspected only after 48 hours.

It is prevented by removing at least one of the three conditions: low hydrogen consumables, oven-dried electrodes, and clean, dry surfaces. Preheat slows the cooling, and gives the hydrogen time to escape, and sometimes the weld is also heated afterward, to drive out the remaining hydrogen. A different kind of crack forms during solidification itself, so it is called a hot crack. The grains in the weld metal grow from the walls of the pool, in columns, toward the center. At the end of solidification a thin layer of liquid remains in the center, with impurities such as sulfur and phosphorus, which melt at a lower temperature.

When the weld contracts, this layer tears, and a crack forms along the weld centerline. A weld that is too narrow and deep increases the risk, because the grains from both sides meet on a single line in the middle. So a reasonable depth-to-width ratio is kept, sulfur and phosphorus are limited, and a suitable filler metal is chosen. High restraint of the parts, which does not let them contract, increases the risk of both types of crack. Metals that do not change phase are also affected by the heat.

A metal that was work hardened softens in the HAZ, because the grains recrystallize. And alloys hardened by heat treatment, such as aluminum in the T6 temper, lose a large part of their strength in the HAZ. And every weld leaves residual stresses: the weld contracts on cooling, and the surrounding material resists. These stresses can reach the yield strength, and add to the risk of cracking, distortion and corrosion. Metallurgy is controlled through the welding procedure, the WPS.

The procedure defines the preheat temperature, the maximum interpass temperature, and the heat input range. In thick or sensitive steels, post weld heat treatment, PWHT, is added. In carbon steels the part is usually heated to 600 to 650 degrees, and cooled slowly. This relieves most of the residual stress, tempers the martensite, and drives out hydrogen. Preheat and heat treatment add time, energy and cost, so they are specified only when really needed.

To sum up: the structure in the weld zone is set by the peak temperature and the cooling rate. Hydrogen cracks form from hydrogen, a hard structure and stress. Hot cracks form during solidification, at the weld center. And all of this is controlled through the consumables, the preheat, the heat input and the heat treatment. 17. Welding Steels

Steel is the most welded material in the world, but the word steel covers hundreds of grades, and each family behaves differently in welding. In this chapter we will apply the metallurgy from the previous chapter to the main families. We will go through carbon steels, structural and high strength steels, chromium-molybdenum steels, four families of stainless steel, and joining dissimilar steels. In carbon steels, the carbon content determines almost everything. Low carbon steel, up to about a quarter of a percent, welds easily, and usually without preheat.

In medium carbon steel, a quarter to half a percent, the HAZ hardens, so preheat and low hydrogen consumables are needed. And in high carbon steel, above half a percent, as in springs and tools, welding is very difficult, and requires high preheat and post weld heat treatment. Free-machining steels, with added sulfur, are hardly suitable for welding, because the sulfur causes hot cracking. In galvanized steel, the zinc fumes are toxic and cause porosity, so the coating is removed in the weld area, and the area is well ventilated. And rust, paint and oil bring in hydrogen and porosity, so everything is cleaned down to bright metal.

Structural steels, such as S235 and S355, are the most common, and weld well. The number gives the yield strength in megapascals. High strength steels, above 460 megapascals, get their strength from fine grains and heat treatment. With them it is important to work within a narrow heat input window: too much softens the HAZ and coarsens its grains, and too little hardens it. A filler metal of matching strength is selected, and the fracture toughness at low temperature is also tested.

And almost always low hydrogen consumables are needed, and preheat according to the thickness. Chromium-molybdenum steels resist high temperature, and are used in power plants, boilers and steam piping. They harden strongly, so they need high preheat, sometimes 200 to 300 degrees. The temperature is maintained throughout welding, and sometimes the part is not allowed to cool at all until the heat treatment. Post weld heat treatment is almost always mandatory, to soften the HAZ and restore the toughness.

And the filler metal must match the composition, otherwise the weld will be weak at the service temperature. Stainless steel contains at least eleven percent chromium, which forms a thin oxide layer on the surface that protects against corrosion. There are four main families, and each behaves differently in welding. Austenitic, such as 304 and 316, is the most common. And there are also ferritic, martensitic, and duplex.

Austenitic is the easiest to weld: it does not harden, and it is not sensitive to hydrogen cracking. But it expands more and conducts heat less, so it distorts more than carbon steel. To prevent hot cracking, the filler metal contains a little ferrite, usually a few percent. Between 450 and 850 degrees, the chromium combines with carbon, and unprotected zones form along the grain boundaries. This is sensitization to intergranular corrosion. The solution is very low carbon steels, marked with the letter L, or steels stabilized with titanium or niobium.

On the root side argon is purged behind the weld, otherwise the root oxidizes and looks like burnt sugar. The heat tint is removed by pickling and passivation, to restore the protective layer. And separate brushes and tools are used, because carbon steel particles cause rust on the stainless. Ferritic steel, such as 430, suffers from grain growth in the HAZ, so it is welded with low heat input. Martensitic steel, such as 410 and 420, hardens like high carbon steel, and requires preheat and heat treatment.

Duplex, such as 2205, is made of roughly half austenite and half ferrite, and combines high strength with excellent corrosion resistance. To keep this balance, you work within a defined heat input window, and do not cool too quickly. The filler metal is richer in nickel, and sometimes nitrogen is added to the shielding gas. And the maximum interpass temperature is especially important here. Joining stainless steel to carbon steel is very common, for example in supports and piping.

The problem is dilution: the carbon steel dilutes the weld, and brittle martensite can form in it. So an especially rich filler metal is used, such as 309L, which remains austenitic even after dilution. The Schaeffler diagram predicts the weld structure in advance from the composition. And in joints that operate at high temperature, a nickel-based filler metal is used, because its expansion is close to that of both steels. What drives the cost of welding steels?

Stainless and nickel filler metals cost several times more than carbon steel filler metals. Preheat and heat treatment add equipment, energy and labor hours, especially in chromium-molybdenum steels. With stainless steel you add root purge gas, pickling, and passivation. And in sensitive steels inspections are added: hardness, ferrite content, and testing after 48 hours. So the choice of steel at the design stage has a major effect on the welding cost.

To sum up: in carbon and high strength steels, control lies in the carbon, the hydrogen and the cooling rate. In chromium-molybdenum steels, high preheat and heat treatment are mandatory. And in stainless steel, you preserve the corrosion resistance: low carbon, root shielding, cleaning, and a suitable filler metal. In the next chapter: welding aluminum and titanium.

18. Aluminum and Titanium

Aluminum and titanium are light, strong and corrosion-resistant metals, and both require a completely different approach from steel. In aluminum the challenge is the heat, the oxide and the porosity. In titanium the challenge is the air itself. We will look at the properties of each metal, the alloys and filler metals, the working methods, the defects and what drives the cost. Aluminum carries a thin, hard oxide layer, which melts only at about 2,000 degrees, while the aluminum itself melts at 660. If it is not broken up, it stays in the weld and prevents fusion.

Aluminum conducts heat about four times better than steel, so the heat escapes quickly, and a higher current is needed. At the start of the seam the part is still cold, and toward the end it is already very hot, so the current is reduced or the speed increased along the seam. It does not change color before melting, so it is hard to see when it is about to melt. It expands about twice as much as steel, so it distorts more. And hydrogen dissolves easily in liquid aluminum, and comes out of it during solidification. That is the main source of porosity.

Aluminum alloys are divided into series, according to the main alloying element. The 1000, 3000 and 5000 series are not heat treatable, and weld well. The 6000 series, such as 6061, is heat treatable, and is sensitive to hot cracking when welded without filler metal. The 2000 and 7000 series, used in aerospace, are the hardest to fusion weld, so they are joined by FSW or rivets. The two common filler metals are 4043, with silicon, and 5356, with magnesium.

4043 flows better and is less sensitive to cracking. 5356 is stronger, and its color matches better after anodizing. And in hardened alloys, such as 6061 in the T6 temper, the HAZ loses about a third of its strength, and this must be taken into account already in the design. In TIG, aluminum is welded on alternating current: the positive half breaks up the oxide, and the negative half penetrates deep. In MIG you work with spray or pulse transfer, and always push the torch. The shielding gas is argon, and for thick plates helium is added, to get a hotter arc.

Aluminum wire is soft, so it is fed through a short liner, or with a push-pull system, so it does not buckle. Cleaning is half the success: first degrease with acetone or alcohol, and only then brush with a dedicated stainless brush. If you brush first, you drive the grease into the surface. And weld soon after cleaning, before the oxide comes back and absorbs moisture. Thick plates are preheated, but moderately, so as not to damage the hardened alloys.

And at the end of the seam the crater is filled, otherwise a crack forms in it. The common defect in aluminum is porosity, from moisture, grease and dirty oxide. Hot cracks appear at the weld center and in the crater, mainly when hardened alloys are welded without a suitable filler metal. Lack of fusion is caused by oxide that was not broken up, or by too cold a start. And large distortion is caused by the high expansion, so parts are firmly clamped and the welding sequence is planned.

And when the strength of the HAZ is critical, it can be restored by a full heat treatment after welding, if the part allows it. Titanium is as strong as steel, almost half as heavy, and highly corrosion resistant. So it is used in aerospace, the chemical industry and medical implants. But at high temperature it absorbs oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen from the air, and becomes brittle. It also conducts heat poorly, so the weld zone stays hot for a long time, and must be protected for a long time. So it has to be shielded from all sides, until it cools below about 400 degrees: the weld, the HAZ and the root.

A trailing shield is used, which flows argon over the weld behind the torch, along with argon purging on the root side. Small parts are sometimes welded inside a closed chamber filled with argon. The color of the weld shows whether the shielding was enough: silver or light straw is good, blue and purple are a warning sign, and gray or white is rejected. Titanium is welded mainly with TIG, and also with plasma, laser and electron beam. Cleanliness is critical: clean with acetone or alcohol, work with clean gloves, and never touch the weld area with bare hands.

The two common grades are commercially pure titanium, and the six-four alloy, with six percent aluminum and four percent vanadium. The filler metal is the same grade as the part: pure titanium, or six-four alloy. Titanium does not harden like steel, and needs no preheat. The heat input is relatively low. And it cannot be fusion welded directly to steel or aluminum, because brittle compounds form. For that, special transition joints are used. The color is checked right after welding, before any cleaning, and a weld with a borderline color is handled according to the procedure.

In aluminum, the cost of cleaning and preparation is high, and AC TIG is relatively slow. A feeding system suited to soft wire is needed, and porosity repairs take time. In titanium, argon is a significant cost: a trailing shield, root shielding, and sometimes a whole chamber. The filler metal is expensive, the welder needs special training, and every weld is inspected by its color. And a rejected weld in titanium is especially expensive, because the whole contaminated zone must be removed and rewelded.

To sum up: in aluminum you break up the oxide, clean thoroughly, manage the heat, and choose a suitable filler metal. In titanium you shield from the air on all sides until it cools, and check the color. In the next chapter, the last of the fourth part: distortion and residual stress, and how to control them.

19. Distortion and Stress

Every weld shrinks as it cools. The result is distortion you can see, and stresses that remain inside the part and cannot be seen at all. In this chapter we will understand why this happens, what types of distortion there are, and how to control them: in design, in production, and by straightening afterward. And we will finish with residual stresses: what they do to the part, and how to relieve them. When a narrow strip of metal is heated, it wants to expand. But the cold metal around it prevents that, so it is compressed, and at high temperature it is also permanently deformed.

On cooling, this zone shrinks, and now it is shorter than it was before. The surrounding metal resists the shrinkage, and the zone remains in tension. The weld and the zone next to it are in tension, and the material around them is in compression. These are the residual stresses. If the part is free to move, the stresses release themselves as distortion. And if it is strongly restrained, it stays straight, but the stresses inside it are higher.

There are several main types of distortion. Transverse shrinkage: the parts move closer together, perpendicular to the weld. Longitudinal shrinkage: the weld gets shorter along its length. Angular distortion: the plates fold around the weld, because there is more molten metal on the top side than on the bottom. This is the most common distortion. Bending: when the weld is not at the center of the section, the longitudinal shrinkage bends a beam or profile along its length.

Waviness: in thin sheet, the compression around the weld makes the sheet ripple, called buckling. And rotational distortion: during welding, the gap at the end of the seam closes or opens. In practice, several types appear together in the same part, and all of them have to be dealt with. The amount of distortion depends on several factors. The heat input: more heat, more shrinkage.

The volume of weld metal: a fillet weld larger than required, or too wide a groove, adds shrinkage without adding strength. The number of passes: each pass adds another cycle of heating and shrinkage, and especially increases angular distortion. The material: stainless steel and aluminum expand more and conduct heat differently, so they distort more than carbon steel. Restraint: a restrained part distorts less, but high stresses remain in it. And symmetry: a weld on one side only pulls the part toward it.

The cheapest control starts right at the design stage. Design the weld to the required size, and no larger. Double the fillet size means about four times the metal. When possible, use intermittent welds instead of a continuous weld. In thick plates, an X groove from both sides instead of a V groove from one side: less metal, and balanced shrinkage. Place welds symmetrically, and close to the neutral axis of the section, so they do not bend it.

And reduce the number of welds: a bent or drawn profile instead of several welded plates. In production there are several tools too. Presetting: the parts are tilted in advance at the opposite angle, so the shrinkage brings them back into place. Fixturing: jigs and clamps hold the parts during welding, but they leave stresses, and sometimes the part moves when it is released. Tack welds: short spots, at regular intervals and in the right order, so the gap does not close up.

And in large parts, extra length is left in advance, to compensate for the transverse shrinkage. A balanced welding sequence: weld alternately on both sides, and from the center outward. In backstep welding, each short segment is welded in the direction opposite to the overall progression. And in skip welding, scattered segments are welded, to spread the heat along the whole length. A lower heat process, such as pulsed welding or laser, reduces distortion.

And copper bars under or next to the weld absorb heat, and reduce the hot zone. If distortion still occurs, the part can be straightened. Mechanical straightening, by pressing or bending, suits small distortion and simple parts. In flame straightening, spots or triangles are heated in a precise location and allowed to shrink, which pulls the part back. It takes experience, and in heat treated steels the temperature must be limited, so as not to damage the properties.

And in precision parts, a machining allowance is left, and final machining is done only after welding. Residual stresses can reach the yield strength of the material. They are invisible, so they are measured by special methods, for example drilling a small hole and measuring the change around it. They add to the service stresses, and shorten the fatigue life, especially at the weld toe. They increase the risk of stress corrosion cracking, and of brittle fracture at low temperature.

And in a part machined after welding, removing material releases stresses, and the part moves after machining, sometimes right on the machine. So precision parts get a stress relief heat treatment before final machining. There is also vibratory stress relief, and hammering the weld toe, peening, which greatly improves fatigue resistance. Distortion is one of the most costly factors in welded fabrication. Straightening takes labor hours, sometimes more time than the welding itself.

A part that moves out of tolerance goes to scrap, or needs repair. Machining allowances, fixtures, and stress relief heat treatment add time and cost. So the cheapest way to deal with distortion is in the design: less weld metal, symmetry, and a planned welding sequence. To sum up: the shrinkage of the weld creates distortion and residual stress. They are controlled through design, sequence and heat during production, and corrected by straightening or heat treatment.

20. Welding Symbols on Drawings

On a drawing, a weld is not explained in words. A welding symbol is used: an arrow, a line, a small symbol, and numbers. Whoever can read the symbol knows exactly which weld to make, on which side, at what size and with which process. In this chapter we will learn the structure of the symbol according to ISO 2553, and compare it with AWS, where the sides are reversed. A welding symbol has several fixed parts. The arrow line points to the joint to be welded.

The reference line is a solid horizontal line, connected to the arrow. The symbol is placed on it. In ISO there is also a dashed identification line, parallel to the reference line, above or below it. The elementary symbol shows the weld type: fillet, V groove, and more. To the left of the symbol the weld size is written, and to the right the length. And at the end of the line there is a tail, with additional information such as the process number.

All of these are defined in the standard, so the symbol reads the same in every plant and every country. The most important question: which side is welded? Every joint has an arrow side, the side the arrow touches, and an other side. In ISO, a symbol on the solid line means welding on the arrow side. A symbol on the dashed line means welding on the other side, no matter whether the dashed line is above or below.

In AWS there is no dashed line: a symbol below the line means the arrow side, and a symbol above the line means the other side. A symbol on both sides of the line means welding on both sides, and then there is no dashed line. So before reading, check which standard the drawing follows. Confusion here leads to welding on the wrong side. The elementary symbols describe the shape of the weld. A triangle is a fillet weld.

Two parallel lines are a square butt weld, with no edge preparation. V is a V groove, and a half V is a bevel on one side only. In a half V, the vertical leg is always on the left, and the arrow points to the part that gets the bevel. There are also symbols for U and J grooves, which suit thick plates. A circle is a spot weld, and two lines through a circle are a continuous seam weld made with wheels.

And a rectangle marks a plug weld, which fills a hole or slot in one of the plates. And a small arc under the symbol marks a backing run from the other side. Symbols can be combined on both sides. Two Vs back to back are an X groove, and two half Vs are a K groove. And a triangle on both sides is a double fillet.

Supplementary symbols define the weld face shape: a straight line is a flat weld, an outward arc is a convex weld, and an inward arc is a concave weld. A rectangle with the letter M marks a permanent backing strip, and with MR a strip that is removed after welding. There is also a symbol for weld toes that blend smoothly into the base metal, which matters in parts subject to fatigue. A circle at the corner of the arrow line means welding all around the perimeter. And a small flag means a field weld, made at the erection site, not in the shop.

Now for dimensions. In an ISO fillet weld, the letter a gives the throat thickness, and the letter z gives the leg length. In an equal-leg weld, z is about 1.4 times a. So a5 and z5 are two welds of completely different size. In AWS only a number is written, and it is always the leg length. If there is no length to the right of the symbol, the whole length of the joint is welded.

An intermittent weld to ISO is written as the number of segments times the length, with the spacing in brackets. For example, three segments of 50 mm, spaced 100 apart. When the segments on the two sides are staggered relative to each other, a small Z sign is added. In AWS the length and pitch are written, for example 50 dash 150, and the pitch is measured from the center of one segment to the center of the next. Here too the difference between standards changes the result. And in a butt weld, the letter s before the symbol gives the required penetration depth. The tail at the end of the reference line carries information that does not fit in the symbol.

Usually the process number according to ISO 4063, such as 135 for MAG or 141 for TIG. The quality level to ISO 5817 can also be written there, the welding position, or a reference to the WPS. When there is no information, there is no tail, and then the general requirements in the drawing notes apply. And on drawings with many identical welds, the general requirement is written once in a note, and the symbols show only what differs. Let's read an example.

The arrow points to a corner between a horizontal plate and a vertical plate. On the solid line there is a triangle with a5 on the left: a fillet on the arrow side, with a 5 mm throat. On the dashed line there is another triangle with a4, and on the right 3 times 50, with 100 in brackets: on the other side, an intermittent fillet, three segments of 50, spaced 100 apart. The tail says 135: weld with MAG. And a flag at the corner: the weld is made in the field.

Notice that the dashed line here is below the solid line, and that changes nothing: what counts is which line the symbol sits on. In one line we got all the information: side, type, size, length, process and location. There are a few common reading mistakes. The first and most common: reading an AWS drawing as if it were ISO, or the other way around, and then the sides are reversed. The second: confusing a and z, which changes the weld size by almost 1.4 times.

The third: missing the symbol on the other side, and welding only one side. The fourth: misreading the direction of the half V, and beveling the wrong part. The fifth: ignoring the weld face symbol, and leaving a convex weld where a flat weld is required. And when something is unclear, don't guess: ask the designer before welding. To sum up: arrow, reference line, symbol, dimensions and tail.

In ISO the solid line is the arrow side, and in AWS it is the side below the line. a is the throat, z is the leg. In the next chapter: how to calculate the weld size, and the strength of a welded joint.

21. Weld Calculation and Strength

In the previous chapter we learned to read the weld size on a drawing. Now we will learn where that number comes from. A weld that is too small breaks under load. A weld that is too large costs more, distorts more, and is not always stronger. We will learn how to calculate the strength of fillet and butt welds, according to the principles of Eurocode 3 and AWS, and what changes when the load is cyclic. The strength of a fillet weld is set at the throat: the shortest distance from the weld root to the weld face. When such a weld fails, the fracture usually runs through the throat plane, because that is the smallest section.

In an equal-leg fillet, the throat is about 0.7 of the leg length: a 7 mm leg gives a throat of about 5 mm. So in ISO and Eurocode the calculation is based on a, the throat, while in AWS the leg is specified and the throat is calculated from it. The area that carries the load is the throat times the effective length of the weld. At the ends of the weld there is a start and a stop that are not full size, so according to Eurocode twice a is deducted from the length. And a weld that is too short does not count at all: the effective length must be at least 30 mm, and at least 6 times the throat.

And the throat itself is not less than 3 mm, even when the calculation gives less. A force passing through a fillet weld creates a stress in the throat: the force divided by the area. When the force is parallel to the weld, the weld works in shear along its length. When the force is perpendicular to the weld, the throat takes a combination of tension and shear. In practice, a transverse fillet is stronger than a longitudinal one, and AWS allows its strength to be increased by up to 1.5 times.

Eurocode offers two ways to calculate. The directional method resolves the throat stress into three components, and combines them in a single formula. The simplified method ignores the direction of the force, and only checks that the average throat stress does not exceed the shear strength of the weld. It is more conservative, and therefore convenient for a quick check. The weld strength is derived from the tensile strength of the weaker of the two base materials.

It is divided by the square root of 3 to get a shear strength. Then it is divided by a correlation factor and a safety factor. The correlation factor depends on the steel: 0.8 for S235, and 0.9 for S355. And the safety factor for joints is 1.25. For S235, with a tensile strength of 360 megapascals, the design strength in the throat comes out at about 208 megapascals. And for S355, with 490 megapascals, it comes out at about 251 megapascals.

Let's calculate an example. An S235 flat bar is pulled with a force of 150 kilonewtons, and is joined to a plate by two longitudinal fillet welds, each 100 mm long. Let's try a 4 mm throat. The effective length of each weld is 100 minus twice 4: that is, 92 mm. The total area: two welds, times 4, times 92, comes to 736 square millimeters.

Times 208 megapascals, that gives about 153 kilonewtons. 153 is more than 150, so a 4 mm throat is enough. With a 3 mm throat we would get only about 117 kilonewtons, and that is not enough. On an ISO drawing we would write a4. And in AWS we would specify a 6 mm leg, because 4 times 1.41 comes to about 5.7. In a full penetration butt weld, the calculation is simpler.

If the filler metal is at least as strong as the base material, the weld is as strong as the plate, and does not need to be calculated separately. This is called matching, and overmatching when the filler metal is stronger. In a partial penetration butt weld, it is calculated like a deep fillet: the throat is the guaranteed penetration depth, not the plate thickness. In AWS, for some angles and processes 3 mm is deducted from the preparation depth, because the root is not always fully penetrated. So partial penetration suits compression or light loads, not a joint that needs the full strength of the plate.

The force does not always pass through the center of the weld. When an arm is welded to a column and the load is far from it, a moment is created, and the weld takes both shear and bending. The welds are treated as lines, and section properties are calculated for them, as for a beam. The highest stress is at the point farthest from the center of the weld group. At that point the stress from shear is combined with the stress from bending, and checked against the weld strength.

So in bending, a longer weld helps much more than a thicker weld: the length acts squared. Everything we have calculated so far applies to static load. When the load changes again and again, as in machinery, bridges and cranes, the failure comes from fatigue. A fatigue crack almost always starts at the weld toe, where the geometry changes sharply. The standards classify every welded detail by a FAT class: the stress range it withstands for two million cycles.

A rolled plate with no weld gets FAT 160. The same plate with a transverse fillet drops to about 80, in other words to half. And another important point: in fatigue, a stronger steel hardly helps, because the fatigue strength of a welded detail hardly depends on the steel grade. What helps is geometry: grinding the weld toe, peening, or moving the weld to a zone of lower stress. There are also limits that do not depend on the load. On a thick plate, a small weld cools too quickly and may crack, so there is a minimum size according to the plate thickness.

According to AWS: a 3 mm leg for plates up to 6 mm, 5 mm up to 12, 6 mm up to 20, and 8 mm above 20. And along a plate edge 6 mm thick or more, the leg is 2 mm less than the plate thickness, so the corner does not melt and the weld can be measured. And now, what drives the cost. The cross-section of a fillet grows with the square of the throat: a throat of 6 instead of 4 needs 2.25 times the filler metal, and gives only 1.5 times the strength. A large weld needs more passes, more time, more heat, and more distortion that has to be corrected afterward.

Upsizing on the drawing just to be safe, or an extra pass the welder adds on his own, also add cost without adding safety. And a properly calculated weld, with enough length instead of a thick throat, saves material and time and reduces distortion. To sum up: the strength of a fillet weld is set by the throat, times the effective length. The weld strength is derived from the base material, with a correlation factor and a safety factor. A full penetration butt weld with matching filler is as strong as the plate. And under cyclic load you check fatigue, where geometry decides.

22. Weld Defects

There is no perfect weld. Every weld has small deviations from the ideal shape and structure. The question is not whether there are defects, but which ones, how large, and whether they are dangerous. In this chapter we will learn the main types of defects, how each one forms, and how to identify it. The standards distinguish between an imperfection and a defect. An imperfection is any deviation in the weld. It becomes a defect only when it exceeds the acceptance limit.

ISO 6520 divides imperfections into six groups: cracks, cavities, solid inclusions, lack of fusion and penetration, imperfect shape, and miscellaneous. ISO 5817 sets an acceptance limit for each type, at three quality levels: B, C and D. Level B is the strictest, and level D the most lenient. The designer chooses the level according to the load and the risk, and the highest level is not always needed. Cracks are the most dangerous defects.

A crack is sharp, and at its tip the stress is very high, so it can grow under load until fracture. Hydrogen cracks form after the weld has already cooled, usually in the HAZ, sometimes under the weld, and they cannot be seen from outside. They can appear even hours after welding, so in sensitive steels you wait up to two days before the final inspection. Hot cracks form during solidification, along the weld centerline, mainly when the weld is too narrow and deep. Crater cracks appear at the end of the weld, when the arc is stopped all at once and a depression is left.

They are prevented by filling the crater before finishing, or by ramping the current down gradually. And in thick plates lamellar tearing appears: a stepped crack inside the plate, under the weld, when the plate is pulled in its through-thickness direction. Porosity is cavities of gas trapped in the molten metal before it could escape. The common cause is poor gas shielding: wind, too low a flow, or too high a flow that creates turbulence and draws in air. A nozzle clogged with spatter, or a leaking gas hose, also harm the shielding.

A second cause is dirt: moisture, oil, paint, rust or zinc coating on the surface of the part. And a third cause is electrodes or flux that have absorbed moisture. The porosity can be isolated, scattered, clustered, or elongated like tubes. Surface porosity can be seen with the eye. Internal porosity is found only by testing, for example by radiography. Sometimes foreign material is trapped inside the weld.

The most common is slag that was not cleaned between passes, and was trapped under the next pass. It appears mainly in SMAW, FCAW and SAW, and near the groove walls, where cleaning is difficult. In TIG, tungsten inclusions are trapped, when the electrode touches the pool or the current is too high for it. On a radiograph, slag looks dark, and tungsten, which is very dense, looks bright. Lack of fusion is a condition where the weld metal did not fuse with the base material, or with the previous pass.

It can be at the groove wall, between passes, or at the root. Lack of penetration is a condition where the weld did not reach the root, and the groove stays open at the bottom. The causes: too low a current, too high a speed, a wrong angle, too narrow a groove, or too small a root gap. These defects are almost as dangerous as cracks, because they are flat and sharp. And usually they cannot be seen from outside: the weld looks good, but inside it is not bonded.

The next group is shape defects, most of which can be seen with the eye. Undercut is a groove along the weld toe, in the base material, caused by high current, a long arc or a wrong angle. Overlap is metal that has rolled over the base material at the weld toe without fusing with it. A weld that is too high or too convex wastes material, and creates a sharp change at the weld toe, which reduces fatigue strength. A weld that was not filled completely leaves a section smaller than required.

On the root side there is excess penetration, protruding below, or a concave root. And sometimes the arc burns a hole through the plate. Misalignment between the plates in a butt weld creates additional bending stresses in the joint. And a spot where the arc touched the part outside the weld, an arc strike, creates a small hard zone that can crack. How are they identified? First of all, check where the defect is. Shape defects, surface porosity, crater cracks and spatter are seen in visual inspection.

Internal cracks, lack of fusion, slag inclusions and internal porosity require non-destructive testing. And in judging severity, planar defects are distinguished from volumetric defects. Cracks and lack of fusion are planar and sharp, and therefore dangerous. Porosity and slag inclusions are volumetric and rounded, and therefore less dangerous. So the acceptance limits for cracks are usually zero, while a certain amount of porosity is allowed. And now, what drives the cost.

Repairing a defect costs much more than the original weld. The defect has to be found, gouged or ground out until it is gone, rewelded, and inspected again. In sensitive steels the preheat has to be applied again, and sometimes the post weld heat treatment repeated. Every repair reheats the HAZ, and many procedures limit the number of repairs in the same location. A quality requirement higher than needed also adds cost: level B instead of C means more inspection, more repairs and more highly qualified welders.

And the cheapest way is prevention: cleanliness, dry consumables, proper gas shielding and correct parameters. To sum up: an imperfection becomes a defect only when it exceeds the acceptance limit of the quality level. Cracks and lack of fusion are the most dangerous, and usually cannot be seen from outside. Porosity and slag inclusions come from gas, dirt and poor cleaning. In the next chapter: the inspection methods, from visual inspection to ultrasonics and radiography.

23. Weld Inspection

In the previous chapter we saw that most dangerous defects cannot be seen from outside. So welds are inspected with methods that reveal what the eye cannot see, without damaging the part. In this chapter we will meet the five main methods: visual inspection, liquid penetrant, magnetic particles, ultrasonics and radiography. Inspection does not start after welding. It starts before it. Before welding, the preparation is checked: the groove angle, the root gap, cleanliness and the right consumables.

During welding, the parameters, the preheat temperature, and cleaning between passes are checked. And after welding the weld itself is inspected. Non-destructive testing, NDT, leaves the part intact. Destructive tests cut a sample, and we will learn them in the next chapter. The first, and cheapest, inspection is visual inspection. It reveals most shape defects: undercut, overlap, an underfilled weld, misalignment, surface cracks and surface porosity.

Good lighting is needed, at least 350 lux, and the weld is viewed up close and at the right angle. The weld size is measured with weld gauges, which show the leg, the throat and the height. You also check that the weld is the right length and in the right place according to the drawing. Good visual inspection saves a lot of money, because it catches problems before the expensive tests. But it does not see what lies below the surface.

Liquid penetrant testing, PT, reveals cracks and porosity that are open to the surface, even when they are very fine. First the weld is cleaned thoroughly, because dirt closes the cracks. A colored liquid is sprayed, usually red or fluorescent, and you wait for it to seep into the cracks, usually between five and sixty minutes. The excess is wiped off the surface, and a thin white layer of developer is sprayed on. The developer draws the liquid out of the crack, and a red indication appears on the white background.

The method is simple and cheap, and suits almost any metal, including aluminum and stainless steel. But it reveals only defects that are open to the surface. Magnetic particle testing, MT, is suitable for ferritic steels only, because it needs a material that is attracted to a magnet. The area is magnetized, usually with a U-shaped yoke. A crack in the path of the magnetic field makes the field leak out, and fine iron particles collect there.

That way a clear line is seen along the crack, even when it is slightly below the surface. A crack parallel to the field does not show, so the test is done twice, in two perpendicular directions. The method is fast and sensitive, but it does not work on aluminum or austenitic stainless steel. Ultrasonic testing, UT, sends high frequency sound waves into the metal. For welds an angle probe is used, which sends the sound in at an angle and reaches the whole weld cross-section.

Gel is spread between the probe and the part, because sound does not travel through air. When the sound hits a defect, it returns to the probe, and from the time and the angle you know where the defect is and at what depth. UT is excellent for detecting cracks and lack of fusion, in other words the dangerous planar defects. It suits thick plates, but requires a skilled inspector, and on thin plates it is more difficult. In the advanced method, phased array, or PAUT, the probe has many elements, which steer the sound electronically.

That way you get an image of the cross-section, and the results can be stored and reviewed again. Radiographic testing, RT, passes radiation through the weld, X-rays or gamma rays. Behind the weld there is a film or a digital detector. Where there is less material, more radiation passes through. So porosity and slag inclusions look dark, and tungsten looks bright. RT is excellent for volumetric defects, and provides a permanent record of the weld.

But a crack or lack of fusion shows only if it is parallel to the direction of the radiation. And the radiation is dangerous: the area must be cleared, so exposures are often made at night or in a closed bay. It is also the most expensive and slowest of the five methods. How do you choose a method? By the type of material, the thickness, the type of defect expected, and accessibility. PT and MT cover the surface. UT and RT cover the volume.

And usually they are combined: visual inspection for all welds, and an additional method for some of them. The extent of inspection is set at the design stage: sometimes a percentage of the welds, and sometimes all of them. The result is assessed against the acceptance limits of the quality level, for example according to ISO 5817. And the inspectors themselves are certified according to ISO 9712, at three levels. Level 1 performs according to instructions, level 2 calibrates, tests and decides according to the standard, and level 3 writes the procedures.

And now, what drives the cost. Each method costs more than the one before: visual inspection is the cheapest, and RT the most expensive. The extent of inspection multiplies the price: testing all the welds costs much more than spot checks. RT stops work in the area, and requires coordination and radiation safety. A weld that fails needs repair and retesting, and sometimes the extent of inspection is increased to further welds.

And the big saving: careful visual inspection before, during and after welding, which prevents defects instead of finding them. To sum up: visual inspection comes first. PT and MT for the surface, UT and RT for inside the weld. UT is strong on planar defects, and RT is strong on volumetric defects. And the inspector must be certified. 24. Procedures and Qualification

Two welders weld the same joint, and get different results. How do you make sure every weld comes out as designed? The answer is written procedures, tested in advance, and welders qualified to carry them out. In this chapter we will learn three documents: the WPS, the PQR, and the welder qualification. A WPS is a welding procedure specification: the recipe the welder works to at the station. It defines the base material, the filler metal, the process, and the joint design.

It defines the welding position, the current, the voltage, the travel speed, and from them the heat input. And also the gas and its flow, the preheat temperature, the interpass temperature, and the post weld heat treatment. Each parameter has an allowed range, not a single value. The WPS has to be available at the welding station, not just in a file in the office. A good WPS is written so that the welder can work to it without guessing.

But how do you know the WPS really produces a good weld? You test it. You start with a draft, a pWPS, a proposed procedure that has not yet been tested. A test piece is welded to it, usually in the presence of a certified examiner. During welding the actual values are recorded: current, voltage, speed, temperatures. Then the test piece is checked by non-destructive testing, and then cut up for destructive tests.

If all the tests pass, this record is the PQR: the documentation that proves the procedure works. And on the basis of the PQR, the final WPS is written, which goes out to the production floor. These are the destructive tests we promised in the previous chapter. In a tensile test, a strip across the weld is pulled until it breaks, and the tensile strength must exceed the minimum of the base material. In a bend test, a strip is bent through one hundred eighty degrees around a mandrel, from the face side, the root side, or the side.

The weld must bend without cracking, and that is how lack of fusion and hidden defects are revealed. In a macro test the weld is cut, polished and etched with acid, and the penetration, fusion and HAZ are seen with the eye. In a hardness test, measurements are taken across the weld and the HAZ, to make sure there is no zone that is too hard and brittle. And when low-temperature toughness is required, an impact test, Charpy, is done in the weld and the HAZ. A PQR does not cover only exactly what was tested. It qualifies a range.

For example in thickness: a thick test piece usually qualifies thicknesses from half to twice its own thickness. The standard defines essential variables: base material, process, filler metal type, heat treatment, and sometimes also position and heat input. For example, a PQR on carbon steel does not qualify welding stainless steel, and a MAG procedure does not qualify TIG. A change in an essential variable outside the range requires a new PQR. A change in a non-essential variable is allowed, provided the WPS is updated.

So the test piece is planned wisely, so that it covers as much of the expected work as possible. A good procedure is not enough. You also need a welder who can carry it out. In welder qualification the person is tested, not the procedure. The welder welds a test piece to an approved WPS, and the test piece is visually inspected, and then radiographed, bend tested or fracture tested. The qualification is limited to the process, the material group, a thickness and diameter range, the joint type, and the positions.

A welder qualified in a difficult position, such as overhead welding, is usually also qualified for easier positions. And an operator of a welding machine or robot is qualified separately, as an operator, not as a manual welder. The qualification is not forever: under ISO the employer confirms every six months that the welder has kept welding, and it is renewed every few years. And under ASME, a welder who has not welded with a given process for six months loses the qualification for that process. There are several systems of standards, and they do not replace one another.

In Europe and Israel the usual basis is ISO: procedures to ISO 15614, and welders to ISO 9606. For pressure vessels and piping under the American code, the work follows ASME, Section IX. And for steel structures in the United States, the work follows AWS D1.1. AWS has an advantage: procedures that meet defined conditions are considered prequalified, and no PQR is needed. A qualification to one standard is not always accepted under another, so check in advance what the customer requires.

All of this has to be documented. For every important weld you know which WPS it was welded to, and which welder made it. A weld map on the drawing gives every weld a number, and next to it the procedure, the welder and the inspection results. The certificates of the materials and filler metals are also kept, so every weld can be traced even years later. And an outside auditor usually starts with the documents, and only then looks at the welds.

And now, what drives the cost. A PQR costs a lot: material, welding the test piece, laboratory tests, and the presence of a certified examiner. So it pays to choose the test piece so it covers a wide range, rather than doing a separate PQR for every job. Welder qualification also costs money and time, and every welder not qualified for the required position or material creates a bottleneck. A qualification that lapsed without anyone noticing can invalidate work that has already been done.

And work not done to the WPS, or without documentation, can be rejected even if the weld itself is good. To sum up: the WPS is the recipe, the PQR is the proof that the recipe works, and the welder qualification proves the welder can carry it out. Each of them qualifies a range, and a change outside the range requires a new test. In the next chapter, the last in the course: welding costs, and how to calculate what a weld really costs.

25. Welding Costs

We have reached the last chapter: what a weld costs. The price of welding is not set only by the filler metal. In most cases, the biggest part is time. In this chapter we will learn to calculate the cost per meter of weld, and see what really brings it down. The welding cost is made up of several components. Labor: the welder's wage, including overhead, for all the time spent at the station.

Materials: filler metal, gas, and flux or electrodes. Power and equipment: usually a small part of the cost. And before and after welding: cutting, edge preparation, fit-up, grinding, straightening, heat treatment and inspection. In manual welding, labor is usually between seventy and ninety percent of the cost of the welding itself. The calculation starts with the amount of weld metal.

You calculate the cross-sectional area of the weld, and multiply by the length. In an equal-leg fillet, the cross-sectional area equals the throat squared. For example, a 5 mm throat gives an area of 25 square millimeters. Add about ten percent for convexity, and you get about 27.5 square millimeters. Per meter of weld that is 27.5 cubic centimeters, and in steel, which weighs 7.85 grams per cubic centimeter, it comes to about 216 grams.

For grooves, the groove area is calculated from the angle, the thickness, the root gap and the root face. Not all the filler metal you buy ends up in the weld. With solid wire, about ninety-five percent goes into the weld, and the rest goes to spatter. With cored wire, about eighty-five percent. And with a covered electrode only about sixty percent, because of the discarded stub, the slag and the spatter. In our example, with solid wire, about 227 grams of wire are needed per meter.

Now the time, which is the most important component. Each process deposits metal at a different rate, in kilograms per arc hour. With a covered electrode, about one to two kilograms per hour. With MIG MAG, two to five. And with submerged arc welding, five to fifteen and more. The arc time equals the weight of weld metal, divided by the deposition rate. But the welder does not weld all the time: he changes electrodes, cleans, positions, moves the part and inspects.

The share of time in which the arc is on is called the operating factor. In manual electrode welding it is about twenty to thirty percent, in semi-automatic MIG MAG about thirty to fifty, and in a robotic cell sixty to eighty. Let's work the example through to the end. The prices here are example assumptions only. Weld metal: 216 grams per meter. Deposition rate in MAG: three kilograms per hour. Arc time: about 4.3 minutes per meter.

With an operating factor of thirty-five percent, the total time is about 12.3 minutes per meter. Assume a labor cost of 200 shekels per hour, including overhead: that comes to about 41 shekels per meter. Wire: 227 grams, at fifteen shekels per kilogram, about 3.4 shekels. Gas: fifteen liters per minute, over the arc time, about 65 liters, roughly two shekels. In total about 46 shekels per meter, of which about eighty-eight percent is labor.

In other words, saving ten percent of the time is worth far more than saving ten percent of the wire. What brings the cost down? First of all, the weld size. The fillet area grows with the square of the throat: a throat of 6 instead of 5 adds about 44 percent to the time and the material. So a correctly calculated weld, as we learned in chapter 21, is the first saving. Second, the groove design: in thick plates, an X groove instead of a V groove saves about half the volume.

And reducing the groove angle from sixty to forty-five degrees saves about a quarter of the volume, as long as there is still good access to the root. Third, the fit-up: a large gap between the parts increases the volume, and in grooves sometimes needs a whole extra pass. Accurate fit-up, with good fixtures, saves welding time. The choice of process also matters: a process with a higher deposition rate shortens the arc time. The position matters a lot: flat welding is much faster than vertical or overhead welding.

A positioner that brings the seam into the flat position pays for itself quickly. Mechanization and robots raise the operating factor, but need accurate fit-up and large quantities. Don't forget what happens before and after: grinding, spatter cleaning, straightening distortion and inspection. Sometimes they take more time than the welding itself. And repairing a defect costs several times more than the original weld, so good quality from the start is also a saving.

To sum up the method: calculate the cross-sectional area, and from it the weight of weld metal per meter. Divide by the deposition efficiency, and you get the amount of filler metal. Divide by the deposition rate and the operating factor, and you get the time. Multiply the time by the rate, the materials by their price, and add preparation, finishing and inspection. And that way you can compare alternatives: process, groove, position, or weld size.

In welding, time is money: in most cases labor is most of the cost. The right weld size, a smart groove, a convenient position and accurate fit-up save more than any discount on wire.
