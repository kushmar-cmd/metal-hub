0. What Is Heat Treatment

Two steel bars are lying on the workbench. Same diameter, same length, and exactly the same material: 4140 steel. The first one is easy to turn and drill. The second is so hard that an ordinary drill bit wears out on it without cutting in. The difference between them is not in the composition and not in the shape, but in the history: what each bar went through in the furnace. And that is exactly what this course is about: heat treatment.

Heat treatment is the controlled heating and cooling of a metal, while it stays solid the whole time. The goal is to change the properties of the material: hardness, strength, ductility and more. In most treatments the shape of the part stays almost the same, and the chemical composition does not change either. There are also treatments that add an element such as carbon or nitrogen to the surface, and we will get to them later in the course. What changes is the internal structure: how the atoms are arranged, and which structures they form. You cannot see this structure with the naked eye, but you feel it clearly: in the cutting tool, in the testing machine, and in the part working in the field.

Every heat treatment, simple or complex, rests on just three variables. The first is temperature: how hot we bring the part. The second is time: how long we hold it at that temperature, until the heat reaches the whole volume and the structure has time to change. The third is the cooling rate: slowly inside the furnace, in air, in oil or in water. It is convenient to draw all three on one graph: temperature over time. A rise to temperature, a flat section of holding, and then a drop, fast or slow. Every treatment we learn in this course is really a graph like this, with different numbers. And a small change in one of the variables can give a completely different part.

Let's go back to 4140 steel, which in Europe is called 42CrMo4. In the annealed condition it is relatively soft, around 200 Brinell, and very easy to machine. If we heat it to about 850 °C and cool it quickly in oil, it hardens to about 55 HRC. In this condition it is very hard, but it also breaks easily under a sharp blow. That is why hardening is almost always followed by tempering: reheating to a lower temperature. Tempering at 600 °C brings the hardness down to about 30 HRC, and in return the part becomes strong and tough, and stands up to impact. In this condition, quenched and tempered, the steel is also sold as prehard bar, which can be machined directly. Same composition, same shape, and three parts that behave completely differently.

The secret is inside the steel: at a high enough temperature, the iron atoms rearrange, and the carbon dissolves among them. On cooling they rearrange again, and the cooling rate decides which structure forms. Slow cooling gives a soft structure. Very fast cooling traps the carbon, and gives a very hard structure called martensite. We will go through all of this in chapters 1 and 2, with the iron-carbon diagram and the cooling diagrams. It is important to know that not every metal responds this way. In aluminum, for example, strength comes from a completely different process, called aging.

So what can heat treatment actually achieve? More than you might think. We can harden, so that the part resists wear, as in tools, gears and pins. We can raise the strength, so that a thinner shaft carries the same load. We can soften, so that the material is easy to machine, bend or form. We can relieve stresses left in the part by machining, welding or straightening, so that it does not distort later. And we can improve dimensional stability, and refine the grain size. But there is almost always a price: the harder the steel, the lower its ductility, and the less forgiving it is of impacts and notches. Good heat treatment is always a compromise: the right hardness for the job, not the maximum hardness.

Heat treatments can be divided into two large families. In the first, the whole volume of the part is treated: annealing, normalizing, stress relieving, hardening and tempering. In the second, only the surface layer is hardened, leaving a soft, tough core. That way a gear tooth resists wear on the outside, and does not break on the inside. Some surface methods only heat the outer layer, such as induction or flame hardening. And others also add an element to it: carburizing adds carbon, and nitriding adds nitrogen.

Heat treatment is not a step you add at the end, but part of the production plan. The raw material itself already arrives in a certain condition: annealed, normalized, or quenched and tempered. A typical part is machined in the soft condition, sent for hardening, and then ground to its precise dimensions. On sensitive parts, stress relieving is sometimes done between rough and finish machining, and also after welding. The order of operations decides whether the part ends up in tolerance, and we will devote a whole chapter to it later.

Heat treatment is a process whose result is hard to see. A part treated correctly and a part treated incorrectly look almost the same. The common problems are distortion, quench cracks, soft spots, and loss of carbon at the surface, which leaves a soft layer there. So you cannot rely on appearance: you need a clear specification on the drawing, control of the furnace, and a hardness test at the end.

And what sets the price? Mainly the furnace time, the type of furnace and its atmosphere, and how many parts go into each cycle. A controlled atmosphere or vacuum costs more than a furnace in plain air, but saves cleaning and machining after the treatment. Beyond that there is the subcontractor's minimum order charge, straightening of distorted parts, inspection and certificates. In the last chapter we will work through the full calculation, with examples.

The course is built from six groups. We start with the fundamentals: what happens in steel on heating and cooling, and what hardenability is. Then we go through the through-treatments, one by one, and the quenching media. From there we continue to surface hardening, and to treatments by material family: tool steels, stainless steels, aluminum, titanium and cast iron. And we finish with equipment and design, inspection, specifications and costs. To sum up: heat treatment changes the internal structure of the metal, using temperature, time and cooling rate. The same material can be soft, very hard, or strong and tough, depending on what it went through in the furnace. In the next chapter we go inside the steel, and see what happens in it when we heat it.

1. What Happens on Heating

In the previous chapter we saw that the same steel can be soft, very hard, or strong and tough. In this chapter we go inside the steel, and see what happens in it when we heat it. This is the basis of every heat treatment: without understanding heating, you cannot understand hardening, annealing or normalizing.

A solid metal is built of atoms arranged in a fixed order that repeats itself again and again. This is called a crystal lattice. In iron, at room temperature, the arrangement is a cube with an atom at each corner, and one atom in the center. This arrangement is called BCC, body-centered cubic. But iron has a special property: when you heat it, it changes its arrangement. At 912 °C, the atoms shift to a cube with an atom at each corner, and one in the center of each face. This arrangement is called FCC, face-centered cubic. And at 1394 °C it goes back to BCC, until the iron melts at 1538 °C.

In the change to FCC the atoms pack more tightly, so the iron actually shrinks a little, even though it is getting hotter. And even earlier, at around 770 °C, the steel loses its magnetism. Old blacksmiths made use of this: a magnet that no longer stuck to the part told them it was close to the hardening temperature. But in industry we do not rely on a magnet or on the glow color: we measure with thermocouples.

Now let's add carbon. The carbon atom is much smaller than the iron atom, so it squeezes into the gaps between the atoms. In the BCC lattice the gaps are small, so it can dissolve at most 0.022% carbon. In the FCC lattice the gaps are larger, and it can dissolve up to 2.14%, almost a hundred times more. BCC iron with this small amount of carbon is called ferrite. It is soft and ductile. FCC iron with the dissolved carbon is called austenite. In ordinary steel it exists only when hot. And the carbon that does not fit in combines with iron to form cementite: a compound with about 7% carbon, very hard but brittle.

All of this is summed up in one graph: the iron-carbon diagram. The horizontal axis is the carbon content, and the vertical axis is temperature. Every point on the graph tells you the structure at equilibrium. We will focus on the steel part, up to about 2% carbon. Beyond that we are already talking about cast iron. The first line is a horizontal line at 727 °C. It is called A1, and below it there is no austenite at all. The second line slopes down from the left, from 912 °C. It is called A3, and above it there is no ferrite left, only austenite. The third line rises to the right, and it is called Acm. Above it all the cementite has dissolved into the austenite. The three lines meet at one point: 727 °C, and 0.76% carbon. Above the lines there is one large field of austenite only. That is where, or close to where, we bring the steel before hardening.

And what happens below A1? Steel with 0.76% carbon, cooled slowly, turns entirely into pearlite. Pearlite is not a single phase, but thin alternating layers of ferrite and cementite, like a fingerprint. The name comes from its pearly luster, when you look at it under the microscope. Steel with less carbon has ferrite as well as pearlite. Steel with more carbon has pearlite, and a little cementite at the grain boundaries. The more carbon there is, the more pearlite there is, and the harder and stronger the steel is in its soft condition.

Let's heat 1045 steel, with 0.45% carbon, and follow it on the diagram. At room temperature its structure is close to 60% pearlite, and the rest is ferrite. Up to 727 °C the structure hardly changes, and the heat simply spreads through the part. At A1 the pearlite turns into austenite. Between A1 and A3, the remaining ferrite gradually dissolves into the austenite. And at around 775 °C, on the A3 line, the whole part is austenite. In practice we heat a little above that, as we will see in chapter 7.

The diagram describes equilibrium, that is, very slow heating and cooling. In real heating the transformations happen a little higher, so they are marked Ac1 and Ac3. On cooling they happen lower, and they are marked Ar1 and Ar3. The alloying elements also move the lines: manganese and nickel lower them, and silicon and molybdenum raise them. That is why the exact temperatures are taken from the steel's data sheet. For example, one producer gives C45 steel, the European equivalent, an Ac1 of 725 °C and an Ac3 of 760. That is a little lower than what we read off the diagram, because of the manganese in it. And it matters: heating too low leaves soft ferrite, and that is a source of soft spots after hardening.

Reaching the temperature is not enough. Time is needed too. The austenite forms first at the boundaries between the grains, and the carbon needs time to spread out evenly. At first the austenite grains are small and fine, and that is excellent. But if we heat too hot, or hold too long, the grains grow and swallow one another. A coarse grain stays coarse after cooling too, and the hardened part comes out more brittle. So there is a right temperature, and a right time.

What can go wrong in heating? Heating a thick or complex part too fast creates temperature differences between the surface and the core, and these can distort it and even crack it. So with parts like these, and with tool steels, we heat in stages, with intermediate holds. Heating in air forms oxide scale, and draws carbon out of the surface, leaving a soft layer there. Heating too hot coarsens the grain, as we saw. And in the extreme case, close to the melting point, the grain boundaries start to melt. This is called burning, and the part goes to scrap.

And what makes the heating stage expensive? Every degree and every hour in the furnace cost energy and furnace time, and thick parts need a longer holding time. Preventing scale and decarburization takes a controlled atmosphere, vacuum or a salt bath, and each of them adds cost. And heating in stages, which complex parts and tool steels require, lengthens the whole cycle.

To sum up: when steel is heated, the iron changes from BCC to FCC, and the carbon dissolves in it and forms austenite. The iron-carbon diagram shows when this happens, and the real temperatures for each steel are on its data sheet. And to get good austenite, you need the right temperature, the right time, and uniform heating. In the next chapter we will see the other side: what happens on cooling, and how the cooling rate decides the structure.

2. What Happens on Cooling

In the previous chapter we heated the steel until all of it turned into austenite. Now comes the moment that decides everything: cooling. From exactly the same austenite you can get soft steel, strong steel, or very hard steel, depending only on the cooling rate.

Let's start with very slow cooling, inside the furnace. Then the steel follows the iron-carbon diagram exactly, only in the opposite direction. In 1045 steel, below A3 ferrite starts to appear, first at the austenite grain boundaries. Ferrite holds almost no carbon, so the carbon is pushed into the remaining austenite, which keeps getting richer. At A1 the remaining austenite, with about 0.76% carbon, turns entirely into pearlite. The result is ferrite and coarse pearlite, and the steel is soft and easy to machine.

To form pearlite, the carbon atoms have to migrate and gather into layers of cementite. This migration is called diffusion. Diffusion takes time, and it is fast when hot and slow when cold. With faster cooling, in air for example, there is less time, and the layers come out thinner: fine pearlite, stronger and harder. And what if we cool so fast that the carbon has no time to move at all? Then something completely different forms.

To understand this we build a special diagram: the TTT diagram, time, temperature, transformation. We take small samples of austenite, cool each one quickly to a different temperature, hold it there, and measure when the structure starts to change and when it finishes. The vertical axis is temperature, and the horizontal axis is time, on a logarithmic scale: one second, ten seconds, a hundred seconds. We get two curves shaped like the letter C: one for the start of the transformation, and one for its end. The protruding part of the curve is called the nose. In steel with 0.76% carbon it is at around 540 °C. Near the nose the transformation is fastest: half of the austenite transforms within a few seconds. Above the nose pearlite forms: coarse close to A1, and finer as we go down toward the nose. Below the nose, down to about 220 °C, a different structure forms, called bainite.

Bainite also contains ferrite and carbides, but not in orderly layers; instead, as thin needles with tiny particles. Bainite is strong and tough, and sometimes it is exactly what we want. We will talk about that in chapter 10, on austempering.

And now the extreme case: we cool very fast, so that we miss the nose and let nothing happen on the way. The austenite arrives almost intact at a low temperature, and there, suddenly, it changes its structure all at once, without diffusion. The atoms move together, as in shear, and the carbon stays trapped inside the lattice. This is martensite. The trapped carbon strains the lattice, and turns it into a distorted, elongated cube. That is why martensite is so hard, and also brittle. Martensite starts to form at a temperature called Ms, and finishes at a temperature called Mf. How much martensite forms depends on the temperature reached, not on the holding time. The more carbon there is, the lower Ms gets. In steel with 0.76% carbon it is around 220 °C, and in C45 steel about 305. In high-carbon steels Mf lies below room temperature, so some of the austenite remains. This is called retained austenite, and we will come back to it in chapter 10.

How hard is martensite? That depends almost only on the amount of carbon. With 0.4% carbon it reaches about 56 HRC, and with 0.5%, about 61. Above about 0.7% the hardness hardly rises any more, and levels off at around 65. The alloying elements do not raise the hardness of martensite. They help us get to it, and that is the subject of the next chapter.

In practice, parts are not cooled to a constant temperature and held there. They cool continuously, from the heat down to room temperature. So we use a CCT diagram, continuous cooling transformation. It resembles the TTT, but the curves shift a little to the right and down. On the diagram we draw cooling curves, and see which region each one crosses. Furnace cooling crosses the pearlite region early, and gives coarse, soft pearlite. That is annealing. Air cooling gives fine pearlite, which is stronger. That is normalizing. Cooling in oil or water, fast enough to miss the nose, gives martensite. That is hardening. The slowest cooling rate that still misses the nose is called the critical cooling rate.

But the part does not all cool at the same rate. The surface cools fastest, and the core slowest. In a thin part the core also misses the nose, and the whole section turns into martensite. In a thick part the core may hit the nose, and come out softer than the surface. In plain carbon steel the nose is very close to the temperature axis, and very fast cooling is needed. In alloy steels, such as 4340, the nose moves to the right, and much slower cooling is enough. This is exactly the difference between hardness and hardenability, and that is the next chapter.

What can go wrong in cooling? Cooling too slowly, or a quenching medium that is too weak, leaves pearlite or bainite instead of martensite, and leaves soft spots. Cooling that is too fast and uneven creates large stresses: the surface has already hardened, while the core is still shrinking and transforming. And in the change to martensite the steel also expands slightly. This combination distorts parts, and in severe cases cracks them, especially at sharp corners and changes in thickness. One more thing: a hardened part left without tempering can crack even hours later. So we temper as soon as possible after hardening.

And what makes the cooling stage expensive? A good quenching medium needs agitation, temperature control, and maintenance of the oil or the solution. High-pressure gas quenching, inside a vacuum furnace, gives clean parts and little distortion, but the equipment is far more expensive. And every part that cracks or distorts in the quench costs straightening, extra machining, or scrap.

To sum up: from the same austenite we get different structures, depending on the cooling rate. Slow cooling gives ferrite and pearlite, moderate cooling gives fine pearlite or bainite, and very fast cooling gives martensite. TTT and CCT diagrams show, for each steel, how fast it has to be cooled, and the hardness of martensite depends on the carbon content. In the next chapter we will see why a thick part does not always harden through to the core: hardness versus hardenability.

3. Hardness Versus Hardenability

In the previous chapter we saw that the hardness of martensite depends almost only on the amount of carbon. But on the shop floor people ask two different questions: how hard will the part be, and how deep will it harden. The first question is about hardness, and the second about hardenability. This chapter is about the difference between them.

Hardness is a property of a point: how much the material resists indentation, at the place where you measure. The maximum hardness a steel can reach in hardening is set by carbon: more carbon, harder martensite. Hardenability is a property of the steel as a whole: how easily it turns into martensite, even when the cooling is not especially fast. A steel with high hardenability hardens to a great depth, and with milder cooling too, in oil or even in gas. And on the CCT diagram from the previous chapter, high hardenability means the nose is far to the right, and the cooling misses it easily.

When a hot bar is quenched, the heat leaves only through the surface. The surface cools fastest, and the further in you go the slower the cooling, because the heat has to pass through all the metal around it. The thicker the bar, the more slowly its core cools. And the quenching medium changes the whole picture: water removes heat faster than oil, and oil faster than air. So the same steel can harden right through in a thin bar, and stay soft in the core of a thick bar.

To compare steels we need one fixed test, and that is the job of the Jominy test, also called the end-quench test. We take a bar 25 mm in diameter and 100 mm long, and heat it to austenite. Within less than 5 seconds it is moved to the fixture, and a jet of water is sprayed on the bottom end only, for at least 10 minutes. That end cools very fast, and every millimeter above it cools a little more slowly. So one bar contains all the cooling rates. After cooling, two flats are ground along the bar, a few tenths of a millimeter deep, to remove the decarburized layer. And Rockwell C hardness is measured along the flat: the first point 1.5 mm from the end, followed by more points at fixed intervals. The result is the Jominy curve: hardness as a function of distance from the quenched end.

Let's compare three steels with similar carbon, between 0.4% and 0.5%: the carbon steel 1045, and the alloy steels 4140 and 4340. At the quenched end all three reach a similar hardness, around 55 to 60 HRC, because the carbon is similar. But in 1045 the curve drops fast: after only about 6 mm the hardness falls below 40. In 4140, with chromium and molybdenum, the curve falls slowly: at 25 mm it is still around 45. And in 4340, with nickel, chromium and molybdenum, the curve is almost flat, and stays above 50 even at 25 mm. The height of the start of the curve shows the hardness, and its slope shows the hardenability.

Why is this useful? Because every point on a Jominy bar represents a particular cooling rate. And there are charts that match points on the bar to locations in a round part: the surface, mid-radius and the center, by diameter and by quenching medium. That way you can predict in advance, without cutting a part, what hardness there will be inside it. For example, a 1045 bar 2 inches in diameter, about 50 mm, quenched in oil, reaches only about 25 HRC at mid-radius. And that is exactly the same steel that reached more than 55 at the end of the Jominy bar.

What raises hardenability? Mainly alloying elements dissolved in the austenite. Manganese, chromium, molybdenum and nickel delay the breakdown of austenite into ferrite and pearlite, and push the nose to the right. Molybdenum is especially powerful for its amount, and boron, in tiny amounts of thousandths of a percent, raises hardenability a great deal. A coarse austenite grain raises it too, but it harms impact toughness, so the grain is not coarsened on purpose. And it is important to remember: these elements do not raise the maximum hardness. They only let you reach it at greater depth.

Even within one steel grade there is scatter: every heat arrives with a slightly different composition, within the range of the standard. So each steel has a defined hardenability band, the H-band: an upper curve and a lower curve, and every heat has to fall between them. In 4140, at 19 mm from the end, the band runs from about 39 to 55 HRC. When depth is critical, you order an H-steel, for example 4140H, where the producer guarantees a Jominy curve inside the band, and there are also steels with a narrower band.

In design it is worth asking first: where does the part really need to be strong? In a shaft loaded in bending or torsion, the highest stresses are at the surface, and the core does less work. So through-hardening is not always needed. But in a bolt or a rod loaded in tension, the whole section carries the load, and you need a steel that hardens to the center. In the European standard you see this right in the table: for quenched and tempered 42CrMo4, the tensile strength up to 16 mm diameter is 1,100 to 1,300 MPa. And at 100 to 160 mm diameter, the same steel reaches only 800 to 950. The thicker the part, the higher the hardenability you need to get the same properties.

What goes wrong? The common mistake is to check hardness only on the surface, and assume that is the hardness throughout the part. The data sheet usually shows the properties of a small sample. A thick part of the same steel comes out weaker in the core. And high hardenability is not always an advantage: next to a weld, the heated zone hardens by itself as it cools in air, and may crack. And another mistake: water-quenching a steel with low hardenability, to squeeze more depth out of it. That increases distortion and the risk of cracking, and it is better to choose a suitable steel and quench in oil.

And what drives cost? Alloy steel costs more than carbon steel, and every added element, especially nickel, raises the price. An H-steel with a guaranteed band costs more again, and sometimes comes with a minimum order quantity. Really checking the core means cutting up a sample part, or adding a test coupon to the batch, and that costs money too. But a steel with too little hardenability costs the most: parts rejected at inspection, or worse, failing in service.

To sum up: hardness is how hard the part can be, and it is set mainly by carbon. Hardenability is how deep it will harden, and it is set mainly by the alloying elements, and in the part itself also by the diameter and the quenching medium. The Jominy test measures it, and its curve lets you predict the hardness inside a real part. In the next chapter we start on the through-treatments, with the first of them: annealing.

4. Annealing

So far we have seen what happens in steel on heating and cooling. From this chapter on we begin the treatments themselves, and the first is annealing. Annealing is a treatment whose purpose is to make the steel soft, ductile and easy to work. We anneal before machining, before cold forming, and after cold work that has hardened the material.

There are several kinds of annealing, and the main difference between them is the temperature. In full annealing we heat above the upper line, until all the steel turns into austenite. In spheroidizing we hold for a long time around the lower line, A1. And in subcritical annealing and process annealing we stay below A1, so the steel does not turn into austenite at all.

Let's start with full annealing of a steel with less than 0.8% carbon, like most engineering steels. We heat it to about 50 degrees above Ac3, the line above which all the ferrite has turned into austenite. In 4140 steel, for example, the annealing temperature is 815 to 870 °C. Then we hold at temperature, so that the core of the part also gets there, and the austenite becomes uniform. A common rule of thumb is about one hour for every 25 mm of thickness, but the steel producer's instructions are what count.

The secret of full annealing is the cooling: we cool very slowly, inside the furnace. Usually at a rate of tens of degrees per hour, until we are through the transformation range, below A1. From there the parts can be taken out and cooled in air. The slow cooling gives ferrite and coarse pearlite, with wide spacing between the cementite plates, and this is the softest of the lamellar structures. After full annealing, 4140 steel has a hardness of up to about 197 HB, far less than after hardening.

In steel with more than 0.8% carbon the rule is different: we heat only slightly above Ac1, and not above Acm. If we heat it above Acm and cool slowly, the excess cementite collects on the grain boundaries. There it forms a continuous network of cementite, which makes the steel brittle. When we stay below Acm, some of the cementite does not dissolve, and it remains as small particles and not as a network.

The next kind is spheroidizing, also called spheroidize annealing. In ordinary pearlite the cementite is built of thin layers, like pages. In spheroidizing these layers break up and turn into small spheres. The reason is that a sphere is the shape with the smallest surface area, and the material tends toward it when given enough time and heat. There are three main ways: a long hold just below A1, at around 700 °C. Cycles of slight heating and cooling, above and below A1, or heating slightly above A1 and cooling very slowly. It is a long process, which can take tens of hours, but it gives the softest condition you can get in steel.

Who is it for? First of all, high-carbon steels, such as 52100 bearing steel and tool steels. In these steels the spheroidized structure gives the best machinability. After annealing, 52100 steel is at about 183 to 212 HB. It is also a good starting structure for hardening tool steels, because the carbides are evenly distributed. Another important use is cold forming, such as cold heading of bolt heads, because spheroidized steel stretches without cracking. But in steel with less than 0.4% carbon, spheroidizing is bad for machining: the material is too soft, it smears, and sticks to the tool.

Now to annealing below A1. In cold working, such as drawing, rolling or bending, the grains are stretched and fill up with defects. The material work-hardens, and if you keep working it, it cracks. In process annealing we heat to about 550 to 700 °C, below A1. At this temperature new grains form, round and free of defects, in place of the distorted grains. This is called recrystallization. The steel becomes soft again, and you can go on working it. In deep drawing of sheet or in wire drawing this is repeated between stages. The advantage: there is no passage through austenite, so the cycle is shorter, and there is less scale and less distortion.

What does this mean in the workshop? Often you do not need to anneal at all, because you can buy the material already annealed. In the European standards, +A means annealed, and +AC means spheroidized, that is, annealed to spheroidal carbides. On the drawing or the order it is worth stating a maximum hardness as well, for example: annealed, 197 HB max. And when the part will be hardened later, we anneal before machining, not after.

What goes wrong? Full annealing is a long cycle at high temperature, and in an air furnace the surface loses carbon and gets covered with scale. A decarburized layer will not harden properly later, so you have to leave a machining allowance, or anneal in a protective atmosphere. Heating too hot or holding too long coarsens the grain, and that hurts the properties even after further treatments. Cooling too fast, for example when the parts are taken out of the furnace early, gives a harder steel than we expected. And two mistakes we have already mentioned: heating above Acm in high-carbon steel, and spheroidizing a low-carbon steel that needs machining.

And what drives cost? Time. Full annealing ties up the furnace for many hours, because the slow cooling is done inside it. Spheroidizing is longer still, and sometimes takes more than a full day. A protective atmosphere or vacuum, which prevent decarburization, cost more than an air furnace. And without them you need extra stock and grinding. So, when possible, it is cheapest to buy the material already annealed from the mill, +A or +AC, rather than anneal it yourself.

To sum up: full annealing is heating above Ac3 and cooling slowly in the furnace, and it gives ferrite and coarse pearlite. Spheroidizing turns the cementite into spheres, and gives the softest condition, mainly for high-carbon steels and for cold forming. Process annealing, below A1, restores ductility after cold work. In the next chapter we will meet a treatment that looks similar, but gives a different result: normalizing.

5. Normalizing

In the previous chapter we looked at annealing. Normalizing looks almost the same, but the small difference between them gives a completely different result. Normalizing gets its name because it returns the steel to a normal, uniform structure, after an earlier process has spoiled it. We will see how it is done, how it differs from annealing, and when it is used: after forging, after casting and after welding.

In normalizing we heat the steel to about 30 to 80 degrees above the upper line, Ac3, until all of it turns into uniform austenite. In 4140 steel, for example, the nominal normalizing temperature is 870 °C. We hold at temperature until the core gets there too, exactly as in annealing. And now comes the difference: we take the parts out of the furnace, and cool them in still air. In air the part cools much faster than inside a switched-off furnace, but much more slowly than when quenched in water or oil.

What does the faster cooling do? The austenite has less time, so it transforms at a lower temperature. Fine pearlite forms, with narrow spacing between the cementite plates, and the grains are smaller and more uniform. Fine pearlite and small grains mean a stronger steel than after annealing. In 4140 steel, in a 25 mm diameter bar, normalizing gives about 302 HB and a tensile strength of about 1,020 MPa. After full annealing, the same steel reaches only about 197 HB. And one more important difference: in normalizing the furnace is free again right away, so it is faster and cheaper than full annealing.

In steel with more than 0.8% carbon, here the rule is the opposite of annealing: we normalize above Acm. The aim is to dissolve all the cementite, including the network that formed on the grain boundaries in an earlier process. In air cooling there is not enough time for the network to form again, and the cementite stays dispersed within the pearlite. So normalizing is a good way to break up a cementite network, before spheroidizing or before hardening.

Normalizing has one drawback: the result depends on the thickness of the part. A thin part cools quickly in air, and comes out stronger. A thick part cools slowly, and its core is closer to the annealed condition. So the 4140 values we saw are valid for a 25 mm diameter bar, not for every part. And in steels with high hardenability, air cooling is already enough to form bainite, and sometimes even martensite. Then the steel comes out harder and more brittle than we wanted, so with these steels we normalize and then temper. And stainless steels are usually not normalized at all.

The most common use of normalizing is after forging. In forging the steel is heated to a very high temperature, sometimes above 1,100 °C, and at such a temperature the grains grow a great deal. Forging is also not uniform: some regions cool fast and some slowly, and in rolled steel bands of ferrite and pearlite form. Normalizing wipes all this out. The steel passes through austenite again, and is rebuilt with a fine, uniform grain. And a uniform structure before hardening means the part will respond to hardening uniformly, with less distortion.

In steel castings too the structure is coarse and non-uniform, because the steel cooled slowly from the liquid state, and normalizing improves it greatly. And in welding as well: the weld itself and the zone next to it, which got very hot, end up with a coarse grain. In critical parts, such as piping and pressure vessels, the whole part is sometimes normalized after welding, to refine the grain in all the zones. This is a heavy treatment, because the whole part goes into the furnace, and it may distort. In most cases stress relieving is enough, and we will meet it in the next chapter.

A point that surprises many people: in low-carbon steels, normalizing gives better machinability than annealing. After annealing such a steel is too soft, it smears, and a built-up edge forms on the tool. After normalizing it is a little stronger, and the chip breaks cleanly. In steels with more carbon the opposite is true, and there annealing gives easier machining.

In the European standards, normalizing is marked +N, for example in the structural steel S355J2+N. Plate like this can be normalized in a furnace, or normalized-rolled, where rolling finishes at a temperature that gives the same structure. On the drawing you write normalized, and if the properties matter, you also add a hardness or strength range. And when the part is a forging, it is worth requiring normalizing before machining, so the structure is uniform from the start.

What goes wrong? When parts are stacked tightly against each other, the ones in the middle cool slowly, and come out different from the ones on the outside. Long, thin parts may sag while they are hot. So they are hung vertically, or laid on many supports. A strong draft or a cold floor cools one side faster, and that creates distortion and stresses. Here too, heating in air forms scale, and the surface loses carbon. And with alloy steels, people sometimes forget that they have hardened in air, and end up with a brittle part, or one that is very hard to machine.

And what drives cost? Normalizing is cheaper than full annealing, because the furnace is free again right away. But the scale has to be cleaned off, usually by sand or shot blasting, and sometimes bent parts have to be straightened. Alloy steels need an extra tempering cycle, and large weldments need a furnace big enough for the whole structure. So, as with annealing, it is often cheapest to buy material that is already normalized.

To sum up: normalizing is heating above the upper line and cooling in still air. It gives fine pearlite and a small, uniform grain, and a stronger steel than after annealing. It is used after forging, casting and welding, before hardening, and to break up a cementite network. And the result depends on the thickness and the type of steel. In the next chapter we will meet a treatment that does not change the structure at all, but only relieves the stresses: stress relieving.

6. Stress Relieving

You know how it goes: you machine a part, it comes out accurate, and once it is released from the vise, or a day later, it is already bent. The culprits are residual stresses, stresses that stayed locked inside the material. Stress relieving is a heat treatment with one purpose: to lower these stresses, without changing the structure of the steel.

A residual stress is a stress that exists in the part even when no external force is acting on it. These stresses are balanced: one region is in tension, another is in compression, and the part stays in equilibrium. It is like a loaded spring trapped inside the material. As long as nothing is touched, everything is stable. But when material is removed, in machining for example, the balance is broken, and the part moves to find a new balance. And a tensile residual stress also adds to the service load, which shortens fatigue life and promotes stress corrosion cracking.

Where do they come from? The first source is machining itself. In heavy cutting, the tool deforms the surface layer, and that layer is left with stresses. The raw material arrives with stresses too: bars that were cold drawn or straightened at the mill, and plates that were cut. And when a lot of material is removed from one side, for example a deep pocket on one side of a plate, the part bows the other way.

The second source, and the strongest, is welding. The weld solidifies at a high temperature, and as it cools it shrinks. But the cold metal around it prevents it from shrinking. The result: along the weld there are tensile stresses that sometimes reach the yield strength of the material, and further away there is compression. These stresses distort the structure, and in thick parts they raise the risk of brittle fracture and of stress corrosion cracking. That is why, in piping and pressure vessels, the codes sometimes require stress relieving after welding.

The third source is straightening and cold forming. When a bent shaft is straightened in a press, opposite stresses are put into it. It is straight, but only as long as those stresses are not released. If it is later heated, or material is removed from it, it may go back to its original bend. And hardening itself also creates very large stresses, which we will talk about in the coming chapters.

How does heat relieve stresses? When steel is heated, its yield strength drops. Any stress higher than the yield strength at that temperature causes a small amount of local flow, and the material relaxes. And during the hold at high temperature the material also creeps slowly, and the stresses keep dropping. Below about 260 °C almost nothing happens. At 540 °C, about 90% of the stresses are already relieved. And after the hold, we cool slowly and uniformly, so that the cooling itself does not create new stresses.

In carbon steels and low-alloy steels, stress relieving is usually done at 550 to 650 °C. That is below A1, so the steel does not turn into austenite, and its structure does not change. The usual holding time is about one hour for every 25 mm of thickness. Then we cool in the furnace or in still air, and with large parts, slowly and at a controlled rate. In annealed or normalized steel, the hardness hardly changes. The properties stay, and only the stresses drop.

And what about a quenched and tempered part? Here there is an important rule. Stress relieving must be done at a temperature about 30 to 50 degrees below the last tempering temperature. Otherwise it is really an additional tempering, and the hardness of the part drops. For example, a part tempered at 600 °C can be stress relieved at about 550. But a part tempered at 200 °C cannot be stress relieved at a high temperature without losing hardness.

In welding of piping and pressure vessels, the treatment is called PWHT, post-weld heat treatment, and the codes define it precisely. According to ASME, plain carbon steel is held at 595 to 650 °C, one hour for every 25 mm. In piping to B31.3, it is mandatory in carbon steel when the wall is thicker than 19 mm. The code also limits the heating and cooling rates above 425 °C: the thicker the wall, the more slowly you have to heat and cool. And when the structure cannot be put into a furnace, only the weld zone is heated, with electric heating blankets and thermocouples that record the cycle.

The right order in machining: first rough machining, leaving stock, then stress relieving, and only at the end finish machining. That way the stresses from the raw material and from rough machining are relieved, and the part moves before it is at its final size. The same goes for a weldment that needs precise machining: weld, stress relieve, and only then machine. And on the drawing it is worth writing this explicitly, for example: stress relieve before finish machining, with the temperature and the time.

What goes wrong? Heating or cooling too fast creates new stresses, and in complex parts distortion as well. At high temperature steel is weak, and a long part that is not well supported in the furnace can sag under its own weight. Stress relieving a hardened part at too high a temperature lowers its hardness. In an air furnace scale forms, so finished surfaces need protection or a protective atmosphere. And in austenitic stainless steels it is a completely different story, with dangers of its own, and we will talk about them in a separate chapter.

And what drives cost? Furnace time, including slow heating and cooling of thick parts, which can take longer than the hold itself. A large weldment needs a large furnace, or local heating with equipment, thermocouples and records. And the machining allowance and the extra machining step after the treatment cost money too. But it is still far cheaper than a precision part that warped after finish machining, or a weldment that cracked in service.

To sum up: residual stresses come from machining, welding, straightening and hardening, and they cause distortion and failures. Stress relieving is heating below A1, usually 550 to 650 °C, holding and cooling slowly, with no change in structure. In a hardened part, we stay below the tempering temperature, and in machining, we stress relieve between roughing and finishing. In the next chapter we come to the treatment that changes steel more than any other: hardening.

7. Hardening: Heating to Austenite

Hardening is the treatment that changes steel more than any other. It can turn a soft steel into a material almost three times as hard. Hardening is made up of three stages: heating to austenite, fast quenching, and tempering. In this chapter we focus on the first stage: what temperature to heat to, and how long to hold. We will deal with quenching in the next chapter, and with tempering in the one after.

Why does it have to be austenite? Because martensite forms only from austenite. Any ferrite left in the steel at the moment of quenching stays soft ferrite after the quench too. And in addition, the carbon has to dissolve in the austenite. Only the dissolved carbon enters the martensite and gives it its hardness. So the goal of heating is uniform austenite, with the right amount of carbon, throughout the part.

In steel with less than 0.8% carbon, we heat to about 30 to 50 degrees above Ac3. That way all the ferrite turns into austenite, but we go no higher than necessary. In 4140 steel, for example, the hardening temperature is about 830 to 870 °C, followed by an oil quench. And what happens if the temperature is too low, between Ac1 and Ac3? Some of the ferrite does not turn into austenite. After quenching you get martensite with islands of soft ferrite, and the hardness is lower than expected.

In steel with more than 0.8% carbon, the rule is different: we heat only to about 30 to 50 degrees above Ac1, and not above Acm. At this temperature some of the cementite stays undissolved, as small particles within the austenite. That is exactly what we want: these hard particles improve the wear resistance. And the austenite itself holds about the carbon needed for hard martensite, with no excess. If we heat above Acm, all the carbon dissolves, the grain grows, and after quenching a lot of austenite is left that did not turn into martensite. The result is less hardness and a higher risk of cracking.

In alloyed tool steels, the carbides of chromium, molybdenum and vanadium dissolve only at a much higher temperature. Steels like D2 and H13 are hardened at around 1,000 °C, and high-speed steels even close to 1,200. Here we always work to the steel producer's data sheet, and we will expand on this in the chapter on tool steels.

And what happens when we heat too high? The austenite grain grows. A coarse grain gives coarse, brittle martensite, with less impact toughness. The temperature differences in the quench get larger, and so do the distortion and the risk of cracking. And at a higher temperature, the surface also loses more carbon and is covered with more scale.

Now for time. The time in the furnace has two parts: the time until the whole part reaches temperature, and the holding time itself. Heat enters from the surface inward, so in a thick part, the core reaches temperature long after the surface. The furnace matters a lot: in a salt bath heat passes into the part very quickly, and in an air furnace or in vacuum much more slowly. The load matters too: a furnace full of tightly packed parts heats more slowly than a single part. That is why the temperature is measured in the part itself, or in a sample part with a thermocouple, and not only the furnace temperature.

How long do we hold? One rule of thumb for an air furnace is about 1.2 minutes per millimeter of thickness, and in a salt bath about half of that. A more conservative rule, which appears in many specifications for alloy steels, is one hour for every 25 mm of thickness. Too short a time leaves undissolved carbides, and regions that did not turn into austenite, and the hardness is uneven. Too long a time coarsens the grain and loses carbon from the surface. So the rules of thumb are only a starting point, and the producer's or the customer's specification is what counts.

With complex parts and with tool steels, the cold part is not put straight into the hardening temperature. We first heat to an intermediate stage, about 650 °C, and wait for the temperature to even out throughout the part. In high-speed steels a second stage is sometimes added, at around 850 °C, before the high temperature. Preheating reduces the temperature differences between thin and thick sections, and with them the distortion and the risk of cracking.

At the hardening temperature, in ordinary air, the surface loses carbon quickly. A decarburized layer does not harden, and stays soft even after a perfect quench. So we harden in a protective atmosphere, in vacuum or in a salt bath, and tool steels are sometimes wrapped in stainless steel foil. And if there is no choice, we leave extra stock, and grind this layer off after the treatment.

What goes wrong? Soft spots: too low a temperature, too short a time, or parts that touched in the furnace and shielded one another. Heating too high or too long: coarse grain, brittleness, and more quench cracks. A soft skin: decarburization of the surface, discovered only in a hardness test, or when the part wears quickly in service. Uneven heating of a part with thick and thin sections, which leads to distortion. And a material mix-up: if the part is made of a different steel from the one on the drawing, the temperature will not suit it, and the result will not be right.

And what drives cost? The type of furnace: vacuum and protective atmosphere cost more than an air furnace. Thick parts need a long cycle, and preheating adds more stages. Arranging the parts in the furnace, fixtures for hanging and supporting them, and hardness tests are all part of the price. And when the treatment is sent out to a subcontractor, there is usually a minimum charge per load, so it pays to batch parts together.

To sum up: in hardening we first heat to austenite, so that the whole volume turns into martensite in the quench. In steel with less than 0.8% carbon we heat above Ac3, and in steel with more, slightly above Ac1. In tool steels, to the producer's data. The time depends on the thickness and the type of furnace: enough for everything to turn into austenite, and no more than that. In the next chapter we come to the critical moment of hardening: the quench, and the quenching media.

8. Quenching Media

In the previous chapter we heated the part to austenite. Now it has to be cooled fast enough for the austenite to turn into martensite. But cooling too fast distorts the part, and sometimes cracks it. So the choice of quenching medium is always a balance: fast enough to harden, and no faster than that.

When a hot part is plunged into a liquid, the cooling is not uniform over time. It goes through three stages. In the first stage a layer of vapor forms around the part. The vapor insulates, and the cooling is relatively slow. When the surface has cooled enough, the vapor layer collapses, and the liquid touches the part directly and boils on it. This is the second stage, the boiling stage, and it is the fastest. When the surface drops below the boiling point of the liquid, the third stage begins: convection, and the cooling is slow again. A good quenching medium gives a short vapor stage, fast boiling at the high temperatures, and slow convection in the range where martensite forms, to reduce the risk of cracking.

To compare quenching media, we use a number called the quench severity, or the Grossmann H value. Still oil is at around 0.25 to 0.3. Still water, around 1. Brine reaches 2 and more, and with strong agitation even 5. The higher the number, the faster the cooling, and the deeper the part hardens. But the distortion, the stresses and the risk of cracking go up too.

Water is the fastest and cheapest of the common quenching media. It is used mainly for carbon steels, and for parts of simple shape. But water is very sensitive to temperature. In hot water, the vapor layer lasts much longer, and the cooling becomes much weaker. For example, still water at 32 °C gives about 1.1, and in water at 55 °C this drops to about 0.2, less than oil. Brine breaks the vapor layer faster, so its cooling is faster and more uniform. But it promotes corrosion, and parts have to be rinsed well afterwards.

Oil cools more slowly than water, mainly in the boiling stage and the convection stage. The slow convection in the martensite range greatly reduces the risk of cracking, which is why oil is the usual choice for alloy steels. There are quench oils of different speeds: normal, medium and fast, with additives that shorten the vapor stage. But hot oil can catch fire. You need a bath of sufficient volume, control of the oil temperature, and a cover for putting out a fire. And after an oil quench the parts have to be washed, before tempering.

Polymer quenchant is a solution of water and a special polymer, and gives cooling between water and oil. The polymer dissolves in cold water, but near the hot part it comes out of solution and forms a thin film on it. This film slows the cooling, and prevents the sharp jumps of plain water. The cooling rate is set by the concentration, the temperature and the agitation. A higher concentration cools more slowly. Polymer does not catch fire like oil, but its concentration has to be checked regularly, usually with a refractometer.

A molten salt bath works at a temperature of hundreds of degrees, and is used for special methods that we will meet in chapter 10. And in vacuum furnaces the parts are cooled in gas, usually nitrogen, at a pressure of a few bar and up to about 20 bar. Helium cools faster than nitrogen, but costs more. Gas quenching is clean, with no oil and no washing, and the distortion is very small. But it is relatively slow, so it suits mainly steels with high hardenability, such as tool steels.

Agitation of the quenching medium is almost as important as the choice of medium. Agitation breaks the vapor layer, sweeps away bubbles, and brings cold liquid to the surface. Without agitation, pockets of vapor remain, for example in blind holes and recesses, and soft spots form there. Direction matters too: a long part is immersed vertically, along its axis, and not on its side. That way all sides cool at the same time, and the part distorts less.

How do we choose? The rule: the mildest quenching medium that still hardens the part to the required depth. Carbon steel usually needs water, brine or polymer. Alloy steel makes do with oil or polymer. Alloyed tool steels harden in gas or even in air, according to the data sheet. And in a part with a complex shape, holes and corners, a milder medium is better, with a steel of higher hardenability.

What goes wrong? Cracks: a quenching medium too severe for the steel, or sharp corners and uneven sections. Soft spots: vapor pockets, weak agitation, or water that has warmed up. Distortion: a part that enters the bath at an angle, or with one side cooling before the other. Fire: oil that has heated beyond its limit, or parts too hot in a bath too small. And a contaminated quenchant: water in oil causes sudden boiling and cracks, and old oil loses some of its cooling power.

And what drives cost? Gas quenching in a vacuum furnace is the most expensive, but it saves washing and straightening. With oil there are the costs of washing, fire safety, and replacing and disposing of the oil. With polymer, the concentration has to be monitored constantly. And the hidden cost is distortion: straightening and grinding after too severe a quench sometimes cost more than the whole difference in price between quenching media.

To sum up: quenching has three stages: vapor, boiling and convection, and boiling is the fastest. Quench severity rises from oil, through polymer and water, up to brine, and agitation raises it further. We choose the mildest quenching medium that still hardens the part, and look after temperature, cleanliness and agitation. In the next chapter we will meet the stage without which fresh martensite is simply too brittle: tempering.

9. Tempering

In the previous chapter we quenched the part and got martensite. It is very hard, but also brittle, and full of internal stresses. A part like this can crack on the shelf, before it even goes into service. Tempering is reheating to a temperature below Ac1, holding and cooling. It gives up a little hardness, and gives back a lot of toughness.

In fresh martensite, the carbon is trapped inside the iron lattice and distorts it. This distortion is the source of the hardness, and also the source of the brittleness. On heating, the carbon starts to leave the lattice and forms tiny carbide particles. Up to about 200 °C the carbides are very small. The hardness hardly drops, but the stresses are relieved. Between 200 and 300 °C, the retained austenite breaks down. Above 300 °C cementite forms, the particles grow, and the hardness drops gradually. The higher the temperature, the softer and more ductile the matrix becomes. And that is exactly what we tune.

The tool for this tuning is the tempering curve: hardness as a function of tempering temperature, at a fixed holding time. For example, oil-quenched 4140 reaches about 55 HRC after tempering at 200 °C, about 45 at 425 °C, and about 30 at 650 °C. Toughness goes the opposite way: the hotter we temper, the more energy the part absorbs before it breaks. The curve is different for every steel, so we take it from the producer's data sheet, and not from memory.

In practice there are three main tempering ranges. Low tempering, between 150 and 250 °C, keeps a hardness of 58 to 62 HRC. This is how cutting tools and bearings are tempered. Medium tempering, between 350 and 500 °C, gives about 40 to 50, with good elasticity. This is how springs are tempered. High tempering, between 500 and 650 °C, gives 25 to 40 HRC with high toughness. The combination of hardening and high tempering is called quench and temper. Shafts, gears and high-strength bolts are quenched and tempered. And 4140 bought prehard, at about 28 to 32 HRC, has already been through the process at the supplier.

In tempering, temperature matters far more than time. According to the Hollomon-Jaffe parameter, in high tempering, making the time 10 times longer is roughly equal to raising the temperature by about 40 degrees. In other words, an extra hour in the furnace will not rescue tempering at the wrong temperature. A rule of thumb for the holding time: one hour for every 25 mm of thickness, and no less than two hours, from the moment the core has reached temperature. After the hold, we usually cool in air.

When do we temper? Right after the quench. We wait until the part has cooled to about 50 to 70 °C, when you can almost touch it, so that the martensite finishes forming. And then it goes into the tempering furnace. A hardened part is not left on the floor overnight, because the stresses can crack it. Even a few hours without tempering are dangerous, in high-carbon steels and in parts with sharp corners.

There are two ranges in which tempering actually lowers the toughness. The first is between about 250 and 400 °C, and is called tempered martensite embrittlement. It forms quickly, within an hour. So for parts that need toughness, we simply do not temper in this range. The second is temper embrittlement, between 375 and 575 °C. It develops slowly, in a long hold or in slow cooling through the range, for example in thick parts. The cause is impurities such as phosphorus, tin and antimony, which collect at the grain boundaries. Chromium-nickel steels are especially sensitive, and a small addition of molybdenum reduces the problem. This kind can be reversed: we temper again above 600 °C, and cool quickly through the range.

In alloyed tool steels, a lot of retained austenite is left after the quench. In the first temper this austenite breaks down, and in the cooling that follows it turns into new martensite, fresh and brittle. This new martensite has to be tempered again. That is why steels like H13 and D2 get at least two tempers. High-speed steels like M2 are tempered three times, two hours each time, at about 550 °C. And between tempers we cool all the way to room temperature. Without that the austenite does not turn into martensite, and the next temper does not do its job. In these steels the curve also looks different: around 500 to 550 °C the hardness rises again, because of carbides of molybdenum, vanadium and tungsten. This is called secondary hardening.

After tempering, we measure hardness. If the part is too hard, we temper again at a slightly higher temperature. That is a cheap and simple fix. If it is too soft, tempering will not help, because it only lowers hardness. The part has to be hardened again, and tempered again. And the temper colors on the surface, straw yellow or blue, are only a thin oxide film. They are no substitute for a thermocouple in the furnace and a hardness test.

What makes tempering expensive? The number of tempers: every temper is another furnace cycle, with heating, holding and cooling. A triple temper can take up a whole working day. Thickness and weight: a thick part needs a longer hold, and ties up the furnace for longer. A narrow hardness range: a requirement of 2 HRC instead of 4 or 5 calls for samples, measurements, and sometimes an extra temper. Atmosphere: tempering in vacuum or in a protective gas keeps the surface clean, but costs more than tempering in air. And the most expensive of all is a part that came out too soft, because then you pay for the whole hardening again.

To sum up: tempering gives toughness back to martensite, at the price of a little hardness. The temperature sets the result, according to the tempering curve of the steel, and time only fine-tunes it. We temper right after the quench, we keep away from the embrittlement ranges, and tool steels are tempered two or three times. 10. Martempering, Austempering and Sub-Zero Treatment

In a conventional quench, the surface cools long before the core. The outside is already turning into martensite and expanding, while the core is still hot. This difference is the main source of distortion and cracks. In this chapter we will meet three ways to control it: martempering, austempering and sub-zero treatment.

Let's go back to the TTT diagram. The pearlite nose sets how much time we have, and the Ms line marks where martensite begins. In a conventional quench the part plunges through Ms, and the outside and the core cross it at different times. The first two methods stop the cooling in a hot bath, above Ms. And from there they go their separate ways.

Martempering is also called marquenching. The part is quenched into a bath of salt or hot oil, at a temperature slightly above Ms. It is held there only until the temperature evens out across the whole section, and not long enough for bainite to form. Then it is taken out to cool in air. The outside and the core cross Ms almost together, and martensite forms throughout the part at the same time. The result: the same martensite, with far less stress, less distortion and fewer cracks. After martempering the part still needs ordinary tempering, as in the previous chapter. A common version is quenching in hot oil, at about 100 to 190 °C, for precision parts such as gears and bearings.

The method suits steels with good hardenability, such as 4140, 4340 and 52100. In plain carbon steel the hot bath is too slow, and the part will hit the pearlite nose before it gets to Ms. And a part that is too thick will not cool to the bath temperature in time either. So martempering is chosen for precise and complex parts, where every bit of distortion costs grinding.

In austempering the part is quenched into a hot salt bath, at about 260 to 370 °C, again above Ms. But this time it is not taken out: the part is held in the bath until all the austenite turns into bainite. That takes from a few minutes up to an hour and more, depending on the steel and the temperature. There is no martensite here, so no tempering is needed. The cooler the bath, the finer and harder the bainite. The typical hardness is 35 to 55 HRC.

The advantage of bainite: at the same hardness, it is tougher and more ductile than tempered martensite, and stands up better to shock. The distortion is very small, because the transformation takes place at a uniform temperature throughout the part. And there are no quench cracks here, and no temper embrittlement either. That is why austempering is common for flat springs, clips, spring washers and blades.

The big limitation is thickness. The part has to reach the bath temperature before it hits the pearlite nose, and the hot bath cools slowly. In carbon steels this means very thin parts. And above about 13 mm you need a steel with very high hardenability. And in ductile cast iron, austempering produces ADI, which we will get to in chapter 19.

Now to the third problem: retained austenite. In steels with a lot of carbon and alloy, and in carburized cases, Mf drops below room temperature. So some of the austenite does not get to transform, and stays in the part even after it has cooled. It is soft, and lowers the hardness. And worse: over time, in service or in the cold, it turns into martensite and expands. A dimension that was inspected and passed can go out of tolerance after a few months.

The solution is to keep cooling, below zero. In sub-zero treatment the part is cooled to minus 70 to minus 80 °C in dry ice, or to minus 196 in liquid nitrogen. There the retained austenite goes on turning into martensite. This is done right after the quench, before tempering. If we wait, the austenite stabilizes and is harder to transform. And after the cooling the part must be tempered, because fresh, brittle martensite has formed. In complex parts that might crack, a short, low temper is sometimes done before the cooling.

When is sub-zero treatment needed? When dimensional stability is critical: gauges, precision bearings and dies. In carburized gears, specifications such as AMS 2759/7 limit the amount of retained austenite. In tool steels, deep cooling in liquid nitrogen is also considered to improve wear resistance. We will expand on this in chapter 15. And the amount of retained austenite is measured by X-ray diffraction.

Conventional quenching and tempering suits almost any thickness, but distorts the most. Martempering gives martensite with far less distortion, and still needs tempering. Austempering gives tough bainite with no tempering, but only in thin parts.

What drives cost? Salt baths: you need a dedicated installation, washing after the treatment and waste handling, and not every plant has them. Time in the bath: in austempering the part ties up the bath until the transformation is finished. On the other hand you save the tempering, and sometimes it works out cheaper than quench and temper. Sub-zero treatment: another step, another temper, and checking retained austenite by X-ray costs money. But the big saving is in straightening and grinding: less distortion, less work after the treatment.

To sum up: martempering evens out the temperature before the martensite forms, and reduces distortion and cracks. Austempering turns everything into bainite in a hot bath, with no tempering, in thin parts. And sub-zero treatment gets rid of the retained austenite, when dimensional stability is critical. 11. Induction and Flame Hardening

So far we have hardened the whole part. But sometimes all you need is a surface that is hard against wear, and a tough core that absorbs impact. A shaft with bearings, the teeth of a gear, a machine slideway: only the place that does the work needs to be hard. In this chapter we will meet two methods that heat only the surface: induction and flame.

In induction hardening, a copper coil surrounds the part, and a high-frequency alternating current flows through it. The magnetic field of the coil induces circular currents in the steel, eddy currents, and they heat it from within. These currents concentrate near the surface. This is the skin effect. The layer heats to austenite within a few seconds, and the core hardly has time to heat up. Right afterwards a spray of water or polymer quenches the layer, and it turns into martensite. And the coil itself? A hollow copper tube, with cold water flowing through it.

What sets the depth? Mainly the frequency. The higher the frequency, the closer the current stays to the surface, and the thinner the layer. Medium frequency, 1 to 10 kHz, suits deep cases. High frequency, 100 to 500 kHz, suits thin, precise cases. Typical case depths are between half a millimeter and five millimeters. The power and the heating time have an effect too: more time, more heat flows inward, and the case is deeper.

There are two main ways of working. In single-shot heating, the coil covers the whole zone, the part rotates inside it, and then everything is quenched together. In scanning, a short coil moves along the shaft, with the quench spray right behind it. This is how long shafts are hardened. With gears, the whole circumference can be hardened at once, or tooth by tooth.

Which steels are suitable? Medium-carbon steels, about 0.35 to 0.55% carbon, such as 1045, 4140 and 4340. The carbon sets the surface hardness. In 1045 you get about 50 to 58 HRC. The starting structure matters too: quenched and tempered steel, with a fine, uniform structure, responds best. The heating is very short, and the carbides do not have much time to dissolve. So we heat to a higher temperature than in a furnace. And when everything is done right, you get a hard surface over a tough, quenched and tempered core.

The big advantage is compressive stress. The layer that turned into martensite expands, and the core holds it back. So compressive stresses are left in the surface. Compressive stresses hold back fatigue cracks. That is why induction-hardened shafts and crankshafts last much longer. But below the case, in the transition zone, there are tensile stresses that balance them. So a case is not ended exactly where the load is concentrated.

The second method, and the older one, is flame hardening. A torch burning oxygen and acetylene, or another gas, heats the surface to austenite, followed right away by a spray of water or polymer. You can heat a single spot, scan along a surface, or spin the part in front of the torch. Flame-hardened cases are usually deeper than induction-hardened ones. The advantage: cheap, portable equipment, which suits very large parts, such as huge gears, slideways and dies. The drawback: less control. The depth and the hardness depend on the speed, the distance of the torch, and the operator.

What can go wrong? Holes, keyways and sharp corners disturb the heating currents. The corners overheat, and that is where cracks form. So corners are rounded, and a distance is left between the edge of the case and holes and shoulders. At the end of a scan path, or where two cases meet, a soft band forms that has been tempered back. And we temper right after hardening, at a low temperature, in a furnace or in the coil itself. A delay in tempering is one of the causes of cracks.

How is it specified on the drawing? The hardened zone is marked, and the surface hardness and the effective case depth are stated. The effective depth is the depth at which the hardness drops to 80% of the minimum surface hardness, according to ISO 18203. And it is checked on a sample part: it is cut, polished, and microhardness is measured from the surface inward.

Induction: fast, precise and repeatable, but you need a coil for every part. Flame: flexible and cheap to start with, for large parts and small quantities, but less uniform. And both distort far less than hardening the whole part in a furnace, because only a thin layer goes through the change.

What drives cost? The coil: every part shape needs its own coil, which has to be designed and built. In small quantities, the coil is a large part of the cost. Destructive testing: to approve the depth a real part is cut up, and for an expensive part that is a significant cost. Complex geometry: holes, slots and shoulders call for setup and trials. And with flame the equipment is cheap, but the labor hours and the control are expensive.

To sum up: induction and flame harden only the surface, and leave a tough core. In induction the frequency sets the depth, and the compressive stresses greatly improve fatigue resistance. Flame suits large parts and small quantities. In the next chapter: carburizing, where carbon is added to the surface, so that even a low-carbon steel can be hardened.

12. Carburizing and Carbonitriding

In the previous chapter we hardened medium-carbon steels. But what do you do with a low-carbon steel, which hardly hardens at all? You add carbon to it, at the surface only. This is called carburizing. The result: a carbon-rich case that hardens, over a low-carbon core that is tough and strong.

The parts are heated to austenite, usually at 925 °C, in a carbon-rich atmosphere. In gas carburizing, the atmosphere comes from gases such as methane or propane, and its carbon potential is controlled. The carbon moves in from the surface by diffusion, and forms a gradient: a lot of carbon on the outside, and less and less toward the core. At the surface we aim for about 0.7 to 0.8% carbon. More than that adds no hardness, and can form carbides and retained austenite. Then the parts are quenched, usually in oil, and tempered at a low temperature, up to about 180 °C. The surface reaches 57 to 63 HRC, and the core stays tough.

How long does it take? The depth grows with the square root of time. In other words, to double the depth you need four times the time. At 925 °C, a case of one millimeter takes about two hours, and a case of 4 mm can reach 36 hours. A higher temperature shortens the time, and vacuum furnaces work up to 1,050 °C. But the grains grow, and the distortion increases. That is why case depth is the biggest factor in the price.

How is the depth defined? According to ISO 2639, the effective case depth is the distance from the surface to the point where the hardness drops to 550 HV. In American standards it is measured down to 50 HRC, which is about 513 HV. So the same part will get a slightly larger depth there. The total depth, down to where the carbon returns to the core level, is larger still, about one and a half times. And importantly: the depth is defined after final machining. If you grind after the treatment, you add the grinding stock to the depth you order.

Which steels are suitable? Low-carbon steels, with carbon between 0.15 and 0.25%. In the United States the common one is 8620. In Europe 16MnCr5 and 20MnCr5, and for heavy parts 18CrNiMo7-6. The alloy hardly changes the surface hardness, but it sets the core strength and the hardening in thick sections. So the alloy is chosen according to the size of the part and the load on the core. And the classic use: gears in gearboxes.

Carbonitriding is gas carburizing with ammonia added to the atmosphere. The ammonia breaks down on the surface, and nitrogen enters the steel together with the carbon. It is done at a lower temperature, about 790 to 845 °C, and for a shorter time. So the case is shallower: usually between 0.075 and 0.75 mm. The nitrogen raises the hardenability of the case. That way even a plain carbon steel can be oil quenched, with less distortion. It is common for small parts in large series: bolts, pins and mechanism parts. And it raises the compressive stresses in the case, which improves resistance to contact fatigue.

Sometimes part of the surface must not take up carbon: a thread that has to stay tough, a place for welding, or an area that still has to be machined. The familiar method is copper plating. The copper blocks the carbon, and the plated area is not carburized. A cheaper method is stop-off paint, which is brushed onto these areas before the treatment. And there is a third way: leave extra stock in the area, carburize, machine the case off, and only then harden. On the drawing you mark clearly which areas are carburized and which are not. Masking is manual work, and on complex parts it adds a lot to the cost.

What can go wrong? Too much carbon at the surface leaves retained austenite, and lowers the hardness. In gas carburizing, oxidation forms at the grain boundaries and weakens the surface. In vacuum carburizing this problem hardly exists. The part goes through a long heating and a quench, so it distorts. Grinding stock is left. And grinding that is too aggressive can burn the surface, or remove too much of the case.

What drives cost? Case depth: twice the depth is four times the furnace time. Masking: copper or paint, and manual work to remove it. Grinding after the treatment: on a hard part, and with care. And testing: a test coupon that is cut and measured in every load.

To sum up: carburizing adds carbon to the surface of a low-carbon steel, and then we quench and temper. The effective depth is measured down to 550 HV, and the time grows with the square of the depth. Carbonitriding adds nitrogen too, at a lower temperature and for a thinner case. In the next chapter: nitriding and nitrocarburizing, hardening with no quench and almost no distortion.

13. Nitriding and Nitrocarburizing

In carburizing and in induction hardening, the hardness comes from martensite: we heat to austenite and quench. And quenching means distortion. Nitriding works differently: nitrogen is put into the surface at a low temperature, without going to austenite and without quenching. The result is a very hard surface, with almost no distortion. In this chapter we will meet nitriding, and also nitrocarburizing, its shorter version.

In gas nitriding the parts are held between 495 and 565 °C, in an atmosphere of ammonia. The ammonia breaks down on the surface, and the nitrogen enters the steel by diffusion. There it combines with chromium, molybdenum, vanadium and aluminum, and forms tiny, very hard nitrides. The temperature is low, and the steel does not turn into austenite. There is no phase change and no quench, so there is almost no distortion. The part grows by only a few microns, and predictably. So it can be machined to final size before the treatment. And the temperature control is tight: about five degrees either side of the set value. The drawback: it is slow. A cycle lasts between 10 and 130 hours, and the case is usually no deeper than half a millimeter.

In a section under the microscope you see two zones. On top, the compound layer. It is called the white layer, because after etching with nital it stays white. It is made of iron nitrides, epsilon and gamma prime, and is usually 5 to 30 microns thick. Below it is the diffusion zone: dissolved nitrogen and tiny nitrides, which give most of the depth of the case. The nitrogen expands the lattice in the case, and compressive stresses form in it. That is why nitriding greatly improves fatigue resistance. And the case keeps its hardness when hot too, up to the nitriding temperature. That is why it is used on gears, crankshafts, injection molding screws and dies. The white layer is hard and resists wear and corrosion, but it is brittle. Too thick a layer can spall.

So the thickness of the white layer is controlled. In two-stage nitriding, after a short first stage the nitrogen potential is lowered, and the white layer stays thin. On parts that work under heavy contact loads, it is sometimes removed by grinding or lapping. And on the drawing you can specify a maximum thickness for the white layer, or require that there be none at all. And the nitriding depth is defined by hardness: down to the point where the hardness is 50 HV above the core hardness.

Which steels are suitable? Steels with elements that form nitrides. Steels with aluminum, such as Nitralloy, reach the highest hardness. Chromium-molybdenum steels like 4140 and 4340 give a case that is less hard, but less brittle. Tool steels such as H11 and D2 also respond well to nitriding. And the specification AMS 2759/6 gives ready-made parameters for Nitralloy, 4140, 4340 and H11. Plain carbon steel is not suitable for gas nitriding: the case comes out brittle and spalls. Before nitriding the part is hardened and tempered, at a temperature at least 30 degrees above the nitriding temperature. Otherwise the core will soften in the furnace. In chromium-molybdenum steels, the case hardness depends on the core hardness: a softer core gives a softer case. And one more thing: after rough machining we stress relieve, and only then finish machine. That way the part will not move in the furnace.

The second method is plasma nitriding, also called ion nitriding. The parts sit in a vacuum chamber with a mixture of nitrogen and hydrogen, and an electric voltage creates a purple glow around them. The plasma gives precise control: you can get a white layer, or do without it completely. It also breaks down the passive layer of stainless steel, so it is suitable for nitriding stainless. With stainless we work at a low temperature, up to about 450 °C, so as not to form chromium nitrides that damage the corrosion resistance. Masking is simple: the area is covered with a metal cover, which can be used again. And in gas too there are two ways: mask the area, or nitride the whole part and grind the case off the area that does not need it. The drawback: the equipment is more expensive.

Nitrocarburizing adds nitrogen to the surface, and a little carbon as well. It is done at around 565 to 580 °C, and in a short time: in a salt bath, usually one to two hours. The main aim is a good white layer, and not a deep diffusion zone. This layer improves resistance to wear, to galling and to corrosion, and to fatigue as well. And the big advantage: it also suits plain carbon steels and cast iron, with almost no distortion. There is also gas and plasma nitrocarburizing. In plasma you can work at a lower temperature, and then the distortion is even smaller.

The familiar salt bath version is called Tenifer, or Tufftride. In the QPQ version, two more steps are added to it. After nitrocarburizing the parts are cooled in an oxidizing bath at about 400 °C, which forms a black layer of magnetite on the surface. Then they are polished, and dipped again in the oxidizing bath. The oxide layer reaches three to four microns. The result: a black finish, and corrosion resistance that is often better than electroplated coatings. That is why it is widely used on hydraulic piston rods.

What can go wrong? A white layer that is too thick spalls under load. A core that was tempered at too low a temperature softens in the furnace, and the case loses its support. And oil or dirt on the surface leaves patches that were not nitrided. So cleaning before the treatment is a must.

What drives cost? Time: gas nitriding can tie up the furnace for several days. Masking: in gas you need paint or plating, and in plasma mechanical covers. The white layer: a requirement to remove it adds grinding or lapping. And QPQ: three steps, polishing in the middle, and salt baths that have to be managed. On the other hand, it saves a separate coating.

To sum up: nitriding puts in nitrogen at a low temperature, with no quench, and so with almost no distortion. The white layer is hard and durable, but its thickness has to be controlled. Before the treatment we harden and temper, at a temperature above the nitriding temperature. Nitrocarburizing and QPQ are shorter, also suit carbon steel, and add corrosion resistance. 14. Laser, Electron Beam and Boriding

In the previous chapters we met the common hardening methods. In this chapter we will meet three more. Two of them heat only the surface, quickly and precisely: the laser beam and the electron beam. And the third, boriding, diffuses boron into the surface, and forms a layer harder than any method we have seen so far.

Let's start with laser hardening. A powerful laser beam moves along the surface, and heats a thin layer above the hardening temperature. The heating lasts a fraction of a second, so only the top layer turns into austenite, and the core stays cold. As soon as the beam moves on, the cold metal underneath draws the heat away very quickly. This is called self-quenching: no water, no oil and no polymer. The part quenches itself, and martensite forms. Today mainly high-power diode lasers are used, which convert electricity to light with high efficiency. With some lasers a dark coating is applied, so that the surface absorbs more light and does not reflect it.

The hardening depth with a laser is usually between 0.1 and 1.5 mm, and in some steels 2.5 mm can be reached. The depth depends on the power, the width of the beam and the travel speed: a lower speed, deeper heat. The big advantage is distortion: the heat that goes into the part is very small, so the part stays almost at its original dimensions. So you can harden after final grinding, and sometimes save the machining after heat treatment. And you can harden only the area that wears, for example the edge of a bending tool, or the lobes of a camshaft. But there is a limitation: the beam is only a few millimeters wide, and to cover a wide area you run adjacent tracks. When a new track passes next to a track that has already been hardened, it reheats it and softens a narrow strip, and this has to be planned in advance.

The second method is electron beam hardening. The principle is similar: an energy beam heats a thin layer, and the part quenches itself. The difference is that the process takes place in vacuum, because air molecules scatter the electrons. Magnetic coils deflect the beam at enormous speed, and it can scan a whole shape, or several spots almost at the same time. No coating is needed, and the vacuum keeps the surface clean, with no oxidation. The limitation is the vacuum chamber: the part has to fit into the chamber, and every cycle includes pumping down.

How does this compare with induction? In induction you need a coil matched to the shape of the part, and usually a spray quench as well. Laser and electron beam need no coil and no water, and suit complex shapes and small areas. Induction is faster on large areas, and goes deeper. So laser suits a shallow, precise case with minimum distortion, and induction a deep case on simple parts.

Now to the third method, which works in a completely different way: boriding. As in carburizing and nitriding, an element is diffused into the surface. This time the element is boron. The boron combines with the iron, and forms a very hard compound: iron boride. The common method is pack boriding: the parts are packed in a box with a powder that contains boron, and the box is closed. It can also be done with a paste that is applied only to the required area, in a salt bath or in gas. The process needs a high temperature, usually between 800 and 1,050 °C, and a long time: from three hours to more than half a day.

Two layers form on the surface. On the outside, where there is the most boron, a layer of FeB forms. And below it a layer of Fe2B forms. Under the microscope the layer looks like saw teeth driven into the metal. This shape helps it grip the base. The hardness of the boride layer is about 1,400 to 2,000 HV. For comparison, a carburized case reaches about 700. The layer is thin: usually tens of microns, and in carbon steels about 250 can be reached. The layer grows with the square root of time, as in carburizing: to double the thickness, you need four times the time. We want a single layer only, of Fe2B. The FeB layer is more brittle, and cracks tend to form at the boundary between the two layers. To prevent FeB, the amount of boron in the powder and the process time are controlled.

Boriding can be done on carbon steels, alloy steels, tool steels, stainless steels and cast iron. Boriding is not recommended for steels with aluminum or silicon, or for free-machining steels with sulfur or lead. The process takes place at the austenite temperature, so afterwards the core is hardened: a quench in vacuum or in salt, and then tempering. Air-hardening steels are especially convenient, because they harden in the cooling after boriding. The uses: extrusion and pressing dies, pump parts, valves, and parts in agriculture and in oil and gas, which suffer abrasive wear.

The first problem with boriding is dimensional change: the layer stands proud of the original dimension, by about 20 to 25% of the layer thickness. And it cannot simply be ground, because it is thin and very hard. So the dimensions are planned in advance, and only lapping is left. The layer is also sensitive to impact and to thermal shock, so it does not suit parts that take heavy blows. With laser, the common problem is a dirty surface: grease, rust or dirt cause an uneven depth. And with adjacent tracks, the softened zones between the tracks can become the starting point of a fatigue crack.

What drives cost? With laser and electron beam the machine is very expensive, so you pay mainly for machine time and programming. Every new part needs a path, a fixture and calibration trials. In a small quantity, the setup is most of the price. On the other hand, sometimes you save the straightening and the grinding after heat treatment, and that can cover the difference. In boriding, the powder is cheap, but the process is long and takes a lot of manual work: packing, unpacking and cleaning. And if the core has to be hardened after boriding, that is another furnace cycle, and another cost.

To sum up: laser and electron beam heat a thin layer, and the part quenches itself, with almost no distortion. Boriding diffuses in boron and forms a thin, very hard layer, which suits severe wear. But the dimensions have to be planned in advance. In the next chapter we move on to tool steels: how they are hardened, and why they need several tempers.

15. Tool Steels

Tool steels are the steels used to make punches, dies, molds, knives and cutting tools. They contain a lot of carbon and alloying elements, so their heat treatment is different from that of ordinary steel. In this chapter we will see how four common tool steels are hardened: A2, D2, H13 and M2.

Tool steels come in families, and each family is marked with a letter. The letter A marks an air-hardening steel, and the letter O an oil-hardening steel. The letter D marks a steel with a lot of carbon and chromium, for cold work. The letter H marks a hot-work steel, as in die-casting dies and forging dies. And the letter M marks a high-speed steel with molybdenum, for cutting tools. The number after the letter is just a serial number within the family.

Tool steel is heated in stages, because a highly alloyed steel conducts heat slowly. First it is heated to a preheat temperature, held until the whole part equalizes, and only then taken up to the hardening temperature. That way the difference between the surface and the core is small, and there is less distortion and less cracking. The hardening temperatures are high: roughly between 950 and 1,230 °C, depending on the steel. At this heat some of the carbides dissolve, and the carbon and the alloying elements go into the austenite. But the surface loses carbon easily, so it is protected: in vacuum, in a salt bath, or in a wrap of stainless steel foil.

Let's start with the cold-work steels. A2 is a general-purpose, air-hardening steel. It is hardened at around 950 to 980 °C, and cooled in air. After tempering it reaches 60 to 62 HRC, and its distortion is very small. D2 has much more carbon and chromium, and contains large chromium carbides, which give very high wear resistance. It is hardened at around 1,010 °C, and it too hardens in air. Here there is a choice: a low temper, at around 200 °C, leaves about 61 HRC. A high temper, at around 510 °C, makes use of secondary hardening and breaks down the retained austenite. It is a must if the part will later be coated or nitrided at a high temperature.

H13 is a hot-work steel, with about five percent chromium, plus molybdenum and vanadium. It is used for aluminum die-casting dies, extrusion dies and forging dies. It is heated with two preheat stages, and then hardened at around 1,010 to 1,030 °C. In large dies, cooling that is too slow hurts the impact toughness, so the quench has to be fast enough. It is tempered at least twice, between 540 and 650 °C, and reaches 44 to 52 HRC. The hardness is lower than in cold-work steels, because a hot die mainly has to resist thermal fatigue cracks.

M2 is the most common high-speed steel. It is used for drills, milling cutters and punches. It contains tungsten, molybdenum, chromium and vanadium, which form very hard carbides. To dissolve enough carbides, it is hardened close to melting: at around 1,190 to 1,230 °C. Too little heat, and the tool will not keep its hardness when it heats up in cutting. Too much, and the grain boundaries start to melt. So it is heated in stages: two preheats, and then a fast rise and a short time at the hardening temperature. After the quench it is tempered three times, at around 550 °C, and reaches 63 to 65 HRC.

Today most tool steels are hardened in vacuum furnaces. In vacuum there is no oxygen, so the parts come out clean and bright, with no scale and no decarburization. The quench is done with nitrogen gas under pressure, usually between 2 and 10 bar. A powerful fan circulates the gas, and the cooling rate is set by the pressure and the fan speed. Gas cools more slowly than oil, so the distortion is small. But it only works in steels that harden easily, like tool steels. And in especially thick parts the core cools slowly, and you have to make sure the pressure is high enough.

After the quench, tool steel contains hard, brittle martensite, and sometimes a lot of retained austenite as well. So it is tempered immediately, as soon as the part cools to about 50 °C. If you wait, the part may crack by itself, because of the stresses left in it. In the high temper, some of the retained austenite turns into new martensite during cooling. The new martensite has to be tempered again, so two or three tempers are given, with cooling to room temperature between them. Each temper lasts at least two hours, or about one hour for every 25 mm of thickness.

Another way to get rid of retained austenite is sub-zero treatment in liquid nitrogen, down to minus 196 °C. At this cold most of the retained austenite turns into martensite, and the dimensional stability improves. Studies also show an improvement in wear resistance, mainly in steels like D2. The cooling and the warming are slow, so as not to cause thermal shock. And after the sub-zero treatment tempering is a must, because the new martensite is brittle.

Decarburization of the surface leaves a soft layer, which wears quickly. Heating too fast, or without preheating, causes distortion and cracks. Heating too high causes coarse grains and a lot of retained austenite, and the hardness actually drops. And sharp corners and holes close to an edge are where quench cracks start.

What drives cost? A vacuum furnace is expensive, and the hardening cycle is long, with preheats and several tempers. Each temper is a few more hours in the furnace, and three tempers can double the process time. In high-speed steel the temperature is very high, and the control has to be precise, and that costs more. Sub-zero treatment is one more step, and one more temper after it. But a tool that was hardened correctly lasts much longer, and that usually covers the difference.

To sum up: tool steels are heated in stages, and the surface is protected from decarburization. Each steel has its own temperature: A2 and D2 for cold work, H13 for hot work, and M2 for cutting. Tempering follows the quench immediately, two or three times, and sub-zero treatment can be added. In the next chapter we move on to stainless steels: austenitic, martensitic, and precipitation-hardening steels.

16. Stainless Steels

Stainless steels contain at least 10.5% chromium. The chromium forms a thin layer on the surface that protects against rust. But not all stainless steels respond to heat treatment in the same way. Some can be hardened, and some cannot. In this chapter we will meet three families: austenitic, martensitic, and precipitation-hardening steels.

The austenitic steels, such as 304 and 316, contain chromium and nickel, and their structure stays austenite even at room temperature. They have no phase change on cooling, so they cannot be hardened by quenching. They are strengthened only by cold work. The ferritic steels, such as 430, do not harden by quenching either. The martensitic steels, such as 420 and 440C, contain more carbon and less nickel, and harden like ordinary steel. And the precipitation-hardening steels, such as 17-4, get their strength from tiny particles that form during moderate heating.

Let's start with the austenitic steels. Their main treatment is called solution annealing. They are heated to about 1,050 to 1,150 °C, a temperature at which all the carbon dissolves in the austenite. And then they are cooled quickly, in water or in a strong air blast, so that the carbon stays dissolved. Solution annealing softens the steel after cold work, and gives it back its ductility and its corrosion resistance. Cold work strengthens the steel, and in some grades also makes it slightly magnetic. Solution annealing cancels that. Sometimes a stress relief is also done at a lower temperature, but you have to beware of the range we will see now.

Between about 450 and 850 °C, something dangerous happens in austenitic steel. The carbon combines with the chromium, and forms chromium carbides along the grain boundaries. The zone next to the boundary loses chromium, and there the protective layer no longer forms. The result is corrosion along the grain boundaries, even when the surface looks sound. It happens when cooling is too slow, in a long hold in this range, and also next to welds. The solution: fast cooling, or low-carbon steels such as 304L and 316L. And if it has already happened, a new solution anneal repairs it.

Now to the martensitic steels. 420 has about 0.3% carbon, and is used for knives, surgical instruments and plastic molds. 440C has about one percent carbon, and is the hardest of the common stainless steels. It reaches 58 to 60 HRC, and is used for bearings, valves and cutting tools. It is hardened at around 1,010 to 1,065 °C, and quenched in oil, or in gas in a vacuum furnace. After the quench a lot of retained austenite is left in it, so sub-zero treatment is often used before tempering. Too long a time at the hardening temperature causes coarse grains, so that is watched as well.

The martensitic steels are tempered immediately after the quench, usually at a low temperature, around 150 to 370 °C. There is a range in which tempering is forbidden: roughly between 425 and 565 °C. In this range the impact toughness drops, and the corrosion resistance drops too, because chromium carbides form. Anyone who needs a lower hardness tempers above this range, at around 600 to 675 °C.

17-4 steel has about 17% chromium, 4% nickel, and copper as well. It comes from the mill in a condition called Condition A: solution treated at around 1,040 °C, and cooled. In this condition the structure is already martensitic, but not yet at full strength, and it can be machined. A part is not used in Condition A, because it may fail by brittle fracture. After machining comes aging: moderate heating, between 480 and 620 °C, for one to four hours. During aging, tiny copper-rich particles form, which block movement in the lattice and strengthen the steel.

The name of the condition comes from the aging temperature in degrees Fahrenheit. H900 is aging for one hour at 482 °C. It gives the highest strength, about 44 HRC, but the lowest impact toughness. H1025 is four hours at 552 °C, and gives about 38 HRC, with better impact toughness. H1150 is four hours at 621 °C. The hardness drops to about 33, but the steel is at its toughest. In aging the part shrinks slightly: about 0.05% in H900, and 0.1% in H1150. The change is small and predictable, so you can machine almost to final size before aging.

In all heat treatments of stainless steels, the surface must be clean of oil and dirt before it goes into the furnace. Heating in air forms scale that hurts the corrosion resistance, so a vacuum furnace or a protective atmosphere is preferred. And after heat treatment, passivation is often done: a dip in acid that cleans the surface and strengthens the protective layer.

Slow cooling after solution annealing, or welding without planning, cause corrosion along the grain boundaries. Tempering a martensitic steel in the forbidden range causes brittleness and rust. In 440C, retained austenite that is left can turn into martensite later, and change the dimensions of precision bearings. And a 17-4 part that was left in Condition A, or was aged incorrectly, may fail by brittle fracture.

What drives cost? Stainless steels almost always need a vacuum furnace or a protective atmosphere, to avoid scale. Solution annealing needs fast cooling from a high temperature, and in large parts that is complicated. In 440C there is hardening, sub-zero treatment and tempering: three separate steps. And passivation and corrosion tests are more steps, and more cost. In 17-4 the saving is large: it is machined in a relatively soft condition, and then gets one aging, at a low temperature, with almost no distortion.

To sum up: austenitic steels do not harden by quenching. They are solution annealed, and cooled quickly. Martensitic steels are hardened and tempered like ordinary steel, but not tempered between 425 and 565 °C. And 17-4 is machined in Condition A, and the aging condition is chosen by the strength and toughness required. In the next chapter we move on to aluminum: solution treatment, quenching and aging.

17. Aluminum

Aluminum does not harden like steel. It has no change to austenite and martensite, so quenching alone does not harden it. Its strength comes in a different way, called age hardening. In this chapter we will see the three steps: solution treatment, quenching and aging. We will also meet the tempers T4, T6, T73 and T651.

Not all aluminum alloys respond to heat treatment. The 2000, 6000 and 7000 series harden by aging. They contain copper, magnesium and silicon, or zinc. The common ones are 6061 for general parts, and 7075 for high-strength parts, as in aerospace. The 1000, 3000 and 5000 series do not harden by aging. They are strengthened only by cold work, and their temper is marked with the letter H. In the age-hardening alloys, the temper is marked with the letter T, and the digit after it describes the sequence of steps.

The principle is simple. At a high temperature, the alloying elements dissolve completely in the aluminum. If it is cooled quickly, they stay trapped inside it, far more than it can hold at room temperature. This is an unstable state, called a supersaturated solid solution. With time, the elements cluster into very tiny particles, scattered all through the metal. These particles block movement in the lattice, and that is how the aluminum gets stronger. The whole process takes place in the solid state. The part does not melt, and does not change its shape.

The first step is solution treatment. 6061 is heated to about 530 °C, and 7075 to about 480 °C. The holding time depends on the thickness, from a few minutes for thin sheet to several hours for a thick part. The parts are arranged in baskets with gaps between them, so that the heat reaches every surface evenly. The temperature window is very narrow. A little below it, not all the elements dissolve, and the final strength is low. A little above it, the grain boundaries start to melt. That is damage that cannot be repaired, and the part is scrapped. So in furnaces for aluminum the temperature has to be very uniform, about plus or minus 5.5 °C.

The second step is quenching, usually in water. The water tank has to be large, with agitation, so that the water does not heat up too much when the basket goes in. The quench has to be very fast, so the time from opening the furnace to entering the water is limited to a few seconds. In thin parts that means only five to ten seconds, and in thick parts about fifteen seconds. If the quench is too slow, some of the elements come out of solution too early, and the strength and the corrosion resistance drop. The fast quench also distorts the parts, and leaves residual stresses in them. To reduce the distortion, hot water or a polymer solution is sometimes used, which cool a little more slowly.

Right after the quench, the aluminum is still relatively soft. This condition is marked with the letter W, and it is not stable. At room temperature the aging starts by itself, and this is called natural aging. After a few days the strength stabilizes, and you get the T4 temper. That is why parts are straightened and formed right after the quench, while they are still soft. And rivets made of 2024 are kept in a freezer, to delay the aging until they are installed. In 7075 the natural aging does not stop, so it is always given artificial aging.

In artificial aging the part is heated to a moderate temperature, and the formation of the particles is speeded up. 6061 is aged for about 18 hours at 160 °C, and 7075 for about 24 hours at 120 °C. This is the T6 temper, which gives the highest strength. In extruded profiles, such as 6063, sometimes only artificial aging is done, straight after extrusion. This is the T5 temper. During aging the strength rises, reaches a peak, and then drops if you carry on. Aging that is too short is called under-aging, and aging that is too long is called over-aging. In over-aging the particles grow and move apart from each other, and the strength drops.

But sometimes over-aging is done on purpose. This is the T73 temper. In 7075 it is done in two stages: a few hours at around 105 °C, and then a day or more at around 165 °C. The strength drops by about ten to fifteen percent compared with T6. But the resistance to stress corrosion cracking rises a great deal, so T73 is common in thick aerospace parts.

And what is T651? The digits 51 mean that after the quench, and before the aging, the plate is stretched. It is stretched by between one and a half and three percent, and the stretching relieves the stresses that the quench left. That is why T651 plate hardly distorts when parts are machined from it. And in 2024 the T3 temper is common, meaning solution treatment, light cold work and natural aging. And there is also the O temper, which is full annealing. It is heated to about 415 °C, and cooled slowly. In the O temper the aluminum is at its softest, and easy to form and bend. Afterwards it can be given solution treatment and aging.

Melting at the grain boundaries because of overheating is the worst damage, because it cannot be repaired. A long delay before the quench leaves low strength, and hurts the corrosion resistance. Distortion after the quench calls for straightening, and the straightening adds stresses. Aging at too high a temperature gives unintended over-aging, and low strength. And welded parts lose strength next to the weld, and sometimes have to be heat treated again. To check that the temper is right, hardness and electrical conductivity are measured. Each temper has its own conductivity range.

What drives cost? First of all the furnace. It needs a very uniform temperature and control to a standard, and that is expensive to build and to maintain. The quench tank has to be right next to the furnace, and sometimes a drop-bottom furnace is used, which drops the parts straight into the water. Artificial aging takes up to a whole day, and T73 even more. Straightening and stretching after the quench are more work too. And hardness and conductivity tests for every batch add more cost.

To sum up, aluminum hardens by aging, not by quenching. Solution treatment in a narrow window, a very fast quench, and then natural aging to T4, or artificial aging to T6. T73 gives up a little strength for corrosion resistance, and T651 is stretched to relieve stresses. In the next chapter we move on to titanium, beryllium copper and superalloys.

18. Titanium, Beryllium Copper and Superalloys

In this chapter we will meet three groups of special materials: titanium, beryllium copper and superalloys. All of them get their strength from tiny particles, like aluminum, but each one has its own requirements. And in all of them, a small mistake in heat treatment costs a lot of money.

Let's start with titanium. Titanium has two structures: alpha at room temperature, and beta at high temperature. The most common alloy is Ti-6Al-4V, also called Grade 5. It contains six percent aluminum and four percent vanadium. On its own it accounts for about half of all the titanium used in industry. The aluminum stabilizes alpha, and the vanadium stabilizes beta, so at room temperature it contains both structures together. Above about 995 °C, the whole alloy turns into beta. This temperature is called the beta transus. Almost all heat treatments of the alloy are done below this temperature, to keep a fine structure and good ductility. It is used in aerospace, in space, and in medical implants, because it is strong, light and corrosion resistant.

Titanium has three main treatments. The first is stress relieving, between 480 and 650 °C, after heavy machining or welding. The second is annealing, at around 700 to 790 °C. In most cases the material comes from the supplier already in this condition. The third is solution treatment and aging, which raises the strength. It is heated to about 40 °C below the beta transus, around 950 °C, and quenched in water. Then it is aged between 480 and 600 °C, for a few hours. The strength rises by about fifteen percent, but only in relatively thin parts. In a thick part, the core cools too slowly and does not strengthen. When higher resistance to crack growth is needed, a duplex anneal is sometimes done, or an anneal above the beta transus.

The big problem with titanium is that when hot it absorbs oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen from its surroundings. The oxygen penetrates the surface, and forms a hard, brittle layer called alpha case. Tiny cracks form in this layer, and they greatly shorten the fatigue life of the part. So titanium is heated only in high vacuum or in argon, and never in air or in an atmosphere with hydrogen. Hydrogen causes hydrogen embrittlement, so it too is limited in the specification. And if alpha case has formed anyway, it is removed by machining or by chemical milling. Cleaning before the furnace matters too. Fingerprints and coolant residue can contaminate the surface.

Now to beryllium copper. The common alloy is C17200, with about two percent beryllium. It is the strongest of the copper alloys, and still conducts electricity and heat well. It is used for springs, electrical connectors, plastic injection molds, and non-sparking tools. The material usually comes from the mill after solution treatment, at around 790 °C, followed by a fast quench. In this condition it is soft, and easy to machine and form. After machining it is aged for two to three hours, at around 315 °C. Beryllium copper does not age at room temperature, so it can be stored in the soft condition for a long time. In aging, the hardness rises to about 38 to 41 HRC, and the conductivity improves too. During aging the surface oxidizes, even in vacuum, so the parts are cleaned after the treatment. It is important to know that beryllium dust is toxic. Grinding and polishing call for extraction and protective equipment, according to the safety instructions.

The third group is superalloys, based on nickel. The most common is Inconel 718. It contains about fifty percent nickel, nineteen percent chromium, and five percent niobium. It keeps high strength up to about 650 °C, so it is used in jet engines, in turbines and in high-temperature bolts. Its strength comes from tiny particles of nickel and niobium, called gamma prime and gamma double prime. First comes solution treatment, usually at around 980 °C, and fast cooling. Then a double aging is done: eight hours at 720 °C, furnace cooling to 620 °C, and holding there up to eighteen hours in total. The particles in 718 form slowly, and that is an advantage. The part can be welded before aging without cracking, and only then strengthened. Here too the work is done in a vacuum furnace, to keep the surface clean.

In the table you can see the differences between the three materials. In titanium, the atmosphere is the main risk. In beryllium copper, the aging time and safety. And in 718, the temperature accuracy and the long cycle time.

Alpha case that was not removed from titanium is a common cause of fatigue failure. Heating titanium above the beta transus by mistake coarsens the grains and hurts the ductility. In beryllium copper, aging that is too long or too hot causes over-aging, and the hardness drops. And in 718, a wrong solution temperature changes the structure, and hurts the strength and the creep resistance. In all three materials, dirt on the part before the furnace contaminates the surface, so they are cleaned thoroughly before every treatment.

What drives cost? Titanium and superalloys need advanced vacuum furnaces, and tight temperature control. Aging of 718 takes eighteen hours, plus heating and cooling time. Removing alpha case by chemical milling is an extra step, and needs extra stock in the design. In beryllium copper, the safety measures and the cleaning add to the cost. And all these materials need test coupons and hardness tests, and sometimes tensile tests for every lot as well.

To sum up: titanium is heated only in vacuum or in argon, with care for the beta transus and for alpha case. Beryllium copper gets a short aging at a low temperature, and demands attention to safety. And Inconel 718 gets solution treatment and a long double aging, which give it high strength when hot. In the next chapter we move on to cast iron: annealing, stress relieving and ADI.

19. Cast Iron

In this chapter we move on to cast iron: an iron alloy with a lot of carbon, usually between 2.5 and 4%. Most castings get no heat treatment at all. But when it is needed, the treatment mainly changes the matrix, and the shape of the graphite stays as it formed in casting. We will meet the types of cast iron, stress relieving, annealing and hardening, and finally ADI.

In gray cast iron, the carbon is in the form of graphite flakes. It is cheap, easy to machine, and damps vibration. So it is used for machine frames, bases and engine blocks. But the flakes behave like small cracks, so it is brittle and relatively weak in tension. In ductile cast iron, magnesium is added to the melt, and the graphite forms as small spheres, called nodules. The nodules do not concentrate stress like the flakes, so it is much stronger and more ductile, and close to steel in its properties. In white cast iron there is no graphite: the carbon is tied up in carbides. It is very hard and brittle, and is used for wear-resistant parts. Around the graphite there is a matrix, ferritic, pearlitic or a mix of the two, and that is what heat treatment changes.

A casting cools unevenly: the thin walls cool quickly, and the thick sections slowly. That leaves residual stresses in it. When material is removed in machining, the stresses are released, and the part moves and distorts. So in precision castings, such as machine frames and bases, stress relieving is done before final machining. In gray cast iron the temperature is usually between 510 and 650 °C, depending on the composition. In an unalloyed casting, around 550. It is heated slowly, at about 100 °C per hour, and held for about one hour for every 25 mm of thickness. The cooling is just as important: it is cooled slowly in the furnace, at least down to about 300 °C. Fast cooling creates new stresses. In the past, castings were left for months to stabilize by themselves, but studies have shown that this relieves almost no stress.

When the casting is too hard to machine, it is annealed. The aim: to break down the pearlite, so that a matrix of soft ferrite is left. In a subcritical anneal it is heated to about 700 to 760 °C, held for one to four hours, and cooled slowly in the furnace. In a full anneal it is heated above the transformation temperature, around 870 to 900 °C, and cooled very slowly, about 55 °C per hour. If there are carbides in the casting, usually in thin walls that cooled quickly, a graphitizing anneal is done at about 900 to 950 °C. The heat breaks the carbides down into graphite and iron. But chromium, even a quarter of a percent, forms stable carbides that hardly break down. The result of annealing: lower hardness, easier machining, and in ductile iron higher ductility as well.

In normalizing it is heated to about 870 to 900 °C and cooled in air. You get a pearlitic matrix, with higher strength and hardness. Cast iron can also be hardened like steel: heating to about 840 to 900 °C, an oil quench, and then tempering. The risk is cracking. So it is heated slowly up to about 650 °C, and only from there faster. Complex castings are quenched in hot oil, at 80 to 100 °C. After hardening and tempering, the wear resistance can be five times higher than that of a pearlitic matrix. In practice, it is more common to harden only the surface, by induction or flame, for example on machine tool ways and cylinder liners. This method works well only in a pearlitic matrix. In a ferritic matrix there is not enough dissolved carbon, so the hardness hardly rises.

And now we will meet ADI: austempered ductile iron. It is heated to austenite, at around 850 to 950 °C, and quenched quickly into a hot salt bath, between 230 and 400 °C. It is held in the bath for one to several hours, until the matrix turns into ausferrite: acicular ferrite and carbon-rich austenite. Because of the high silicon in the iron, carbides do not form as in the bainite of steel. That is what gives ADI its special properties. The structure forms in the bath itself, so no tempering is needed afterwards, and the distortion is small. The bath temperature sets the grade: a hot bath gives ductility and toughness, and a cool bath gives strength and hardness.

The grades in the ISO 17804 standard range from 800 to 1,400 MPa tensile strength, and in the American standard up to 1,600. The strength is double that of ordinary ductile iron, and the fatigue resistance is about fifty percent higher. It is also about ten percent lighter than steel, so it replaces forged steel in gears, suspension arms and agricultural machinery. After the treatment it is hard to machine. So almost everything is machined before austempering, and afterwards only grinding or finish machining is done. The part also grows slightly in the treatment. The change is predictable and repeatable, so it is taken into account already in the design.

Cooling too fast after stress relieving creates new stresses, and the part distorts in machining. Stress relieving at too high a temperature breaks down the pearlite, and the strength and hardness drop. Quenching without gradual heating cracks castings, especially at transitions between thin and thick walls. ADI is sensitive to the time in the bath: too short a time leaves unstable austenite that turns into martensite. Too long a time breaks it down into carbides. In both cases the ductility drops. And even a perfect treatment does not fix a bad casting: porosity, too few graphite nodules or an uneven composition are still there after the furnace.

What drives cost? Stress relieving is relatively cheap, but large castings take up a whole furnace, and need many hours of slow cooling. A full anneal and a graphitizing anneal are longer, and at a higher temperature. ADI needs a salt bath, close control of time and temperature, and high-quality base iron from the foundry. Machining after the treatment is expensive, so it is important to plan the sequence of operations in advance. On the other hand, ADI often replaces forged steel, and overall the part comes out cheaper.

To sum up: cast iron is heat treated only when needed, and the treatment mainly changes the matrix, not the shape of the graphite. Stress relieving stabilizes dimensions, annealing improves machining, and surface hardening adds wear resistance. And ADI turns ductile iron into a strong, tough material that competes with steel. In the next chapter we move on to furnaces and atmospheres: air, controlled atmosphere, vacuum and salt, as well as decarburization and loading the parts in the furnace.

20. Furnaces and Atmospheres

In this chapter we will talk about the place where heat treatment happens: the furnace, and the atmosphere around the part. At high heat, the surface of the steel reacts with the gas around it. The atmosphere decides whether the part comes out clean, oxidized, or with no carbon in the outer layer. We will meet the types of furnaces, heating in air, controlled atmosphere, vacuum and salt bath, and finally how the parts are loaded in the furnace.

The most common furnace is the box furnace: a batch of parts is loaded, heated, and taken out. In an integral quench furnace, the oil bath is inside the furnace, and the parts move to the quench without meeting air. In a pit furnace, the parts hang vertically from above. This is how long shafts are treated, without bending under their own weight. In series production a continuous furnace is used: the parts move on a conveyor through heating, holding and quenching zones. At high heat, the heat is transferred mainly by radiation. In tempering, at a low temperature, a fan is needed to circulate the air, otherwise the heating is not uniform.

The simplest way is to heat in air. But at high heat, the oxygen in the air forms scale on the steel. And something else happens: the carbon in the outer layer reacts with the atmosphere and leaves as gas, and carbon from inside the part migrates outwards to replace it. This is decarburization. It starts at about 700 °C, and grows with temperature and with time in the furnace. A layer with no carbon does not harden in the quench. You get a soft skin over a hard core, and low resistance to wear and fatigue. It is checked under the microscope, on a section of the part, or by measuring hardness from the surface inwards. When heating in air, extra stock is left and the layer is machined off after the treatment. That is why heat treatment is usually done before final machining. In tool steels, which are heated to a high temperature, the part is sometimes wrapped in stainless steel foil. The foil ties up the oxygen left inside.

The classic solution is a controlled atmosphere. The most common is the endothermic atmosphere. It is produced in a generator, from natural gas and air, which pass over a nickel catalyst, at about 1,040 °C. The approximate composition: 40% hydrogen, 40% nitrogen and 20% carbon monoxide. The important concept is carbon potential: the amount of carbon the atmosphere tends to leave at the surface. When the carbon potential equals the carbon in the steel, the atmosphere neither adds nor removes. If it is higher, it adds carbon to the surface. It is measured with an oxygen probe inside the furnace, and also checked by the dew point of the gas. An alternative to the generator is nitrogen and methanol. The methanol breaks down in the furnace, and an atmosphere of almost the same composition forms. And remember safety: carbon monoxide is toxic, and hydrogen is flammable. So there is a flame at the furnace openings that burns off the gas, and the furnace is purged with nitrogen before opening.

In a vacuum furnace the air is pumped out to a very low pressure, usually around a hundredth to a tenth of a millibar. There is almost no oxygen, so there is no scale and no decarburization. The parts come out bright and clean. In vacuum, heat is transferred only by radiation, which is slow at low temperatures. So at the start of heating nitrogen is let in, and circulated by a fan. The quench is done with gas: nitrogen at a pressure of 2 to 20 bar, which a powerful fan drives through the parts. A gas quench is gentler than an oil quench, so the distortion is small. But it is also slower, and suits mainly steels with high hardenability. In too high a vacuum, elements such as chromium can evaporate from the surface. So the work is sometimes done at a partial pressure of argon or nitrogen. Vacuum furnaces are more expensive, but they are the leading choice for tool steels, stainless steel, titanium and aerospace parts.

In the oldest method, the parts are dipped in molten salt. In neutral salts, based on chlorides, parts are heated before hardening. The salt conducts heat directly to the part, so the heating is fast and very uniform. The part also partly floats, so it distorts less under its own weight. But the salt oxidizes with time and starts to take carbon out of the steel. So the bath is treated regularly and checked. Safety here is critical: a wet part that goes into molten salt causes a dangerous spatter. Only dry, warm parts go in. After the treatment the salt is washed out of every hole. Disposal of used salt is expensive too, so the use of salt baths keeps declining.

Even the best furnace will not help, if the parts are loaded the wrong way. Gaps are left between the parts, so that the heat and the gas reach all sides. Parts that are too crowded heat slowly and unevenly. Long parts are hung vertically or supported at several points. When hot, steel is weak, and a part supported only at its ends will sag. Very thin and very thick parts are not mixed in the same load. They reach temperature at very different times. The holding time is counted from the moment the part itself has reached temperature, not from the moment the furnace has. So a thermocouple is attached to a part inside the load. Too large a load is a common mistake: the parts in the middle do not manage to reach temperature, and the quench oil heats up too much.

Heating in air without extra stock leaves a layer with no carbon, and the part fails the hardness test. An oxygen probe that has not been calibrated makes the atmosphere add or remove carbon, without anyone noticing. A small leak in a vacuum furnace lets air in, and the surface gets an oxidation color. Salt residue in holes and threads causes corrosion, sometimes weeks after the treatment. And crowded parts or too large a load give a different hardness between the parts in the middle and the parts at the edges.

What drives cost? A vacuum furnace costs more than an atmosphere furnace, especially with high-pressure gas quenching. A controlled atmosphere needs a generator or gases, probes, calibration and constant control. Large or long parts take up a whole furnace, or need a pit furnace and hanging fixtures. On the other hand, a vacuum furnace often saves cleaning, grinding and scrapped parts, and overall can come out cheaper.

To sum up: the atmosphere in the furnace is as important as the temperature. Air forms scale and takes carbon out of the surface. A controlled atmosphere keeps the carbon with the help of a precise carbon potential, and vacuum gives clean parts and a gentle gas quench. A salt bath heats quickly and uniformly, and correct loading of the parts decides whether they all get the same treatment. In the next chapter we will talk about distortion and cracking, and about how to design a part that will come through heat treatment safely.

21. Distortion, Cracking and Design

A part goes into the furnace straight and accurate, and comes out bent, or worse, cracked. In this chapter we will understand why that happens. Distortion and cracking are the most common reason for scrapping parts after heat treatment, and a large part of it is decided back at the design table. We will meet the sources of stress, the forms of distortion, quench cracks, and how to design a part and a process that reduce the risk.

There are two sources of stress in quenching. The first is thermal stress: the surface cools and shrinks, while the core is still hot. The second is transformation stress: martensite takes up more volume than austenite, and it forms at the surface and in the core at different times. When the sum of the stresses exceeds the yield strength of the hot material, the part distorts. When it exceeds the strength of the martensite, a crack forms. The history of the part matters too. Stresses left from machining, straightening or welding are released by the heat, and the part moves. And there is gravity as well: when hot, steel is weak, and a long part that is not supported properly simply sags. It is important to distinguish between two kinds of change: a change in size, where the whole part grows or shrinks slightly, and a change in shape, where it bends.

A long, thin shaft tends to bend like a banana, especially if the quench is not uniform from all sides. A ring or a sleeve can come out oval, and the inner diameter changes. A thin, flat plate dishes like a saucer, because its two sides do not cool at the same rate. An asymmetric part, for example with a keyway along one side, bends toward the side that has less material. And a part with thin walls next to thick sections distorts at the transition between them, because each zone cools at a different rate. Even a clean, symmetric part changes its dimensions slightly in hardening. The change is small, but in precision parts it has to be taken into account.

A quench crack usually starts where the stresses concentrate: a sharp corner, a hole close to an edge, a groove or a sharp change in thickness. A sharp corner cools faster than its surroundings, so it both concentrates stress and turns into martensite first. The risk grows with high carbon, with coarse grains from overheating, and with a quenchant that is too severe, for example water for a steel meant for oil. A crack can also appear hours after the quench, if tempering is delayed. So the part goes into tempering as soon as it has cooled enough. How do you recognize a quench crack? It runs in an almost straight line from the surface toward the center, and there is no decarburization next to it. If there is decarburization inside the crack, it was already there before hardening, for example from forging. If there is scale in it, it opened before tempering. And there are grinding cracks too: aggressive grinding of a hardened part heats the surface, and creates a network of fine cracks. They are prevented with a sharp wheel, good cooling, and removing little material in each pass. They are found by acid etching or by magnetic particle inspection.

The first rule of design: generous radii instead of sharp corners, wherever possible. The second rule: wall thickness as uniform as possible, and a gradual transition between thin and thick sections. A symmetric part distorts less. If there is a groove on one side, it can sometimes be balanced with a groove or a hole on the other side. Holes are kept away from the edge and from other holes. A through hole is better than a blind hole, where stresses and quench oil are trapped at the bottom. For parts with thin walls, a steel with high hardenability is chosen, which hardens even with a gentle quench, in oil or in gas. And on the drawing, a grinding allowance is marked on the precision surfaces, and areas that must not be hardened, such as threads, are specified. And it is worth talking to the heat treater already at the design stage. They know how the part will behave in the furnace and during the quench.

A typical sequence of operations for a precision part: rough machining, stress relieving, semi-finish machining, hardening and tempering, and finally grinding. Stress relieving after rough machining releases the stresses from the raw material and from the machining, before the part reaches its final size. When possible, only the area that needs it is hardened, by induction or by laser. That way the heat is local, and the distortion is much smaller. In nitriding there is no quench at all, so the distortion is very small, and often no grinding is needed afterwards. During the quench, a long part goes into the oil vertically, and a flat part goes in on its narrow edge, so that it cools evenly. Holes that do not need hardening are sometimes plugged with plugs or with insulating material, so that the quench around them is gentler. Rings and gears are sometimes quenched in a press with a die, which holds the shape during the quench. A part that has bent is straightened after tempering, preferably while it is still warm. After straightening it is checked for cracks.

A design with no grinding allowance leaves a hardened part that cannot be brought to size. A part that was machined to the end before hardening, with no stress relieving, moves and goes out of tolerance. The combination of a sharp corner and a severe quench is the classic recipe for a quench crack. A part that waited a whole night between the quench and tempering can crack on the shelf. And straightening a hard, cold part by force sometimes ends in a fracture.

What drives cost? A grinding allowance means grinding time, and a hardened part is ground slowly and carefully. Straightening, quench presses and dies also add manual work and special equipment. Parts scrapped because of distortion or a crack cost everything that was invested in them up to that stage. On the other hand, a larger radius or a uniform wall costs almost nothing in design, and saves a lot of money in production.

To sum up: distortion and cracks come from thermal stresses and transformation stresses, and concentrate at corners, holes and changes in thickness. Generous radii, uniform thickness and symmetry are the cheapest way to reduce them. A correct sequence of operations, a grinding allowance and tempering right after the quench complete the picture. In the next chapter we will talk about testing and control: how to make sure the treatment really succeeded.

22. Testing and Control

After heat treatment the part looks exactly as it did before. You cannot see by eye whether it is hard, whether the case is deep enough, or whether the furnace was at the right temperature. So we test: the part itself, the test coupons that went through the process with it, and the furnace. We will meet hardness testing, case depth measurement, metallography, test coupons, and furnace control according to AMS 2750.

The hardness test is the most common test. An indenter is pressed into the material with a known force, and the indentation is measured. In the Rockwell method, a diamond cone is pressed in two stages, and the machine measures the depth of penetration. It is a fast test, so it is the most common on the shop floor. The Rockwell C scale suits hardened steels. For softer materials a ball and the B scale are used. In the Brinell method, a large ball leaves a wide indentation, and its diameter is measured. The large indentation averages the structure, so the test suits cast iron and annealed parts. In the Vickers method, a diamond pyramid leaves a square indentation, and its diagonals are measured under a microscope. There is one scale for the whole hardness range. For the result to be correct, the scale and the decarburized layer are removed first, and the test is made on a flat, clean surface. The thickness of the part has to be at least ten times the depth of the indentation, otherwise the anvil underneath affects the measurement. And on a curved surface a correction is added from a table. The machine is checked regularly on a test block. And conversion tables between the scales are only an approximation.

In a thin hardened case, an ordinary test goes through the case and measures the core as well. So microhardness is used. The part is cut, the section is polished, and a row of small indentations is made with a force of grams, from the surface inwards. Each indentation gives a value, and a profile of hardness against depth is plotted. According to ISO 18203, in carburizing the effective depth is measured down to 550 HV. In induction or flame hardening, down to the point where the hardness drops to 80% of the minimum required at the surface. And in nitriding, down to the point where there is still 50 HV above the core hardness. This is a destructive test: the part that is tested is cut up. So it is usually done on test coupons, and we will talk about those in a moment.

Metallography is looking at the structure itself. The sample is cut, mounted in resin, ground and polished to a mirror finish. Then the surface is etched with a mild acid, for example nital, and the boundaries between the phases are revealed. Under the microscope you see whether martensite has formed, how much retained austenite is left, how the carbides are distributed and what the grain size is. You also see defects: a decarburized layer, a white layer that is too thick in nitriding, or oxidation along the grain boundaries in carburizing. What you see is compared with reference pictures in the standard or the specification, and documented with a photograph. Metallography answers a question that hardness does not answer: why. Two parts with the same hardness can have a completely different structure.

A test coupon is a small piece, of the same material and from the same production lot as the parts, which goes through the whole process with them. It is placed inside the load, usually in the most difficult spot, for example in the middle of the basket, where heating and quenching are slower. After the treatment it is cut and tested: a hardness profile, metallography, and sometimes tensile and impact tests as well. It is important that its thickness is similar to the thickness of the part. A thin coupon cools faster, and will give a better result than the part itself. Every coupon is marked, so that it can be linked to the load, the furnace and the date. This is part of the traceability of the process.

The last part is furnace control. In the aerospace industry it is done according to the specification AMS 2750, which deals with temperature measurement. Every thermocouple and measuring instrument is calibrated, and thermocouples have a limit on the number of uses or on the time in use. In the system accuracy test, SAT, what the furnace displays is compared with the reading of a reference thermocouple, at the same point. The temperature uniformity survey, TUS, checks whether the whole working volume of the furnace really is at the same temperature. Many thermocouples are spread through it, and readings are taken over time. According to the survey results, the furnace gets a class: from class 1, with a deviation of up to 3 °C either way, to class 6, with up to 28 °C. The specification of the part or of the process sets which class is required. The type of instrumentation in the furnace is also defined, from A to E: how many thermocouples there are, and what is recorded. The frequency of the tests is set by the class and by the instrumentation type. And all the records are kept: the temperature chart of every load, the results of the calibrations and the surveys. An auditor can ask for them even years later.

A hardness test on scale or on a decarburized layer gives a low value, and a good part is rejected. A Rockwell test on a part that is too thin gives a wrong value, because of the anvil underneath. A test coupon that is too thin passes the test, while the thick part itself did not harden enough. A thermocouple that is past its time in use drifts, and the furnace runs at a different temperature from the one displayed. And without a record of the load, there is no way to prove that the part went through the correct process.

What drives cost? Destructive tests need coupons, cutting and preparation in the laboratory. A microhardness profile and metallography take hours of skilled work. Compliance with AMS 2750 needs frequent calibrations and surveys, new thermocouples, instrumentation and documentation. On the other hand, a cheap test now saves a rejected part that is discovered only at the customer.

To sum up: hardness is tested by the Rockwell, Brinell or Vickers method, on a clean surface and with enough thickness. Case depth is measured with a microhardness profile, and the structure is seen by metallography, usually on test coupons. And the furnace itself is controlled with calibrations, accuracy tests and uniformity surveys. 23. Specifications and the Drawing

Everything we have learned in the course has to end up on one sheet: the drawing. From it the shop, the supplier and the test laboratory know exactly what is required. A drawing with a note like "harden" says almost nothing: to what hardness, to what depth, where is it measured, and to which specification? In this chapter we will see how to write a heat treatment requirement on a drawing, which specifications are referenced, what certificate you receive, and what Nadcap is.

The international standard on the subject is ISO 15787. It defines how the final condition of a steel part after heat treatment is shown on a drawing. Note: the drawing states the result, not the process. Not temperatures and times, but what the part has to be in the end. First the heat treatment condition is written in words, in the order in which it is done. For example: quenched and tempered, or carburized, hardened and tempered. Then the hardness, the case depth if there is one, and the test points. All of these are grouped next to the title block. And if the part is still ground after the treatment, the requirement is stated both before grinding and after it, because grinding removes part of the case.

According to the standard, every hardness value must have a tolerance. You write, for example, 60 plus 4 HRC, meaning between 60 and 64. The tolerance is set as wide as the function of the part allows. Too narrow a tolerance makes things hard for the supplier, and raises the price. Hardness can also be required by the Vickers or Brinell method, and then the test force is stated as well. Case depth has an abbreviation for each process: for carburizing CHD, for induction or flame hardening SHD, and for nitriding NHD. The depth gets a tolerance too: for example, CHD 0.8 plus 0.4, meaning between 0.8 and 1.2 mm. In nitriding the thickness of the white layer is sometimes added as well, in microns, for example 10 to 20. And the measuring point is marked on the drawing with a special symbol, with a number, so that everyone who tests measures at the same place.

Very often only some of the surfaces need to be hard: a bearing race, gear teeth, or a sealing area. The area is marked with a thick chain line, and dimensioned. A thick dashed line means: hardening is allowed here, but not required. And a thick dotted line means: not allowed. This is very important in carburizing and nitriding. Threads, for example, sometimes have to stay soft. Then they are covered with copper plating or with a special paste, so that the carbon or the nitrogen does not get in. Without this marking, the supplier will harden the whole part, or guess.

In the aerospace and defense industry, the drawing usually refers to a specification, and does not spell out the process itself. For steels, the basic specification is AMS 2759. It sets general requirements, and has slash sheets for each family and each process. For example, for carbon and low-alloy steels AMS 2759/1, for carburizing AMS 2759/7, and for gas nitriding AMS 2759/6. Aluminum has a separate specification, AMS 2770, and titanium has AMS 2801. And the furnace itself comes under AMS 2750, which we met in the previous chapter. The specification sets the temperatures, the times, the tests and the documentation. And large customers add their own specifications on top of it, which take precedence. It is important to check the revision too. The letter at the end of the specification number marks the revision, and the requirements change between revisions.

At the end of the process the supplier sends a certificate. It is the only link between the part you received and what happened in the furnace. A good certificate includes the part number and the quantity, the lot number, the specification and its revision, the test results, and a signature. For raw material there is a European standard for types of certificates, EN 10204. In a 2.2 certificate the manufacturer attaches general results, which were not necessarily tested on your shipment. In a 3.1 certificate the results were measured on the lot that was supplied, and an inspection representative of the manufacturer, who is independent of production, signs them. And in a 3.2 certificate an external inspector, or a representative of the customer, signs as well. It is the most reliable, and also the most expensive. In receiving inspection the certificate is compared with the drawing: the same specification, the same revision, and results within tolerance.

Heat treatment is a special process: the result cannot be fully checked on the finished part without destroying it. So in aerospace a test at the end is not enough. The supplier itself is audited: the furnaces, the procedures, the training and the records. Nadcap is an accreditation program shared by the large aerospace and defense companies. It is run by an organization called PRI. An expert auditor comes to the supplier for several days, and goes through detailed checklists for heat treatment and for temperature measurement. Every nonconformance calls for a correction and a root cause analysis, and only then is the accreditation granted. And it is renewed in repeat audits. Many customers in aerospace and defense require a heat treatment supplier with Nadcap accreditation. Without it, the supplier is simply not on the list.

And now, the common mistakes on a drawing. Hardness with no tolerance, or a tolerance of only two Rockwell points, which is very hard to meet consistently. A case depth without stating whether it is before grinding or after it. A hardness requirement on a surface the machine cannot reach, or on a wall that is too thin to measure. And a reference to a specification with no revision, or to a specification that does not suit the material.

What drives cost? Tight tolerances on hardness and on depth call for close control, and sometimes cause rejections. Extra test points, test coupons and destructive tests add time and work. A 3.2 certificate needs an external inspector, and a supplier with Nadcap accreditation charges more, because it carries the costs of the whole system. On the other hand, a clear drawing saves questions, mistakes and rejections. It is the cheapest investment in the project.

To sum up: the drawing states the final result, according to ISO 15787: the heat treatment condition, hardness with a tolerance, case depth and measuring points. Local treatment is marked with lines, and in aerospace the drawing refers to AMS specifications with a revision. The certificate links the part to the process, and Nadcap accreditation ensures that the supplier itself meets the requirements. 24. What Drives the Cost of Heat Treatment

We have reached the last chapter of the course. And we will finish with the question everyone who orders heat treatment asks: why does it cost what it costs? The price of heat treatment is not set by weight alone. It is set by furnace time, manual work, testing and documentation. We will see how suppliers price, what drives cost, how to save in design and in purchasing, and at the end we will go over the whole course together.

Suppliers price in three ways: per kilogram, per part, or per whole furnace cycle, when the furnace runs only for you. There is almost always a minimum order charge as well. That is why one small part can cost as much as ten. The furnace costs money for every hour it runs, full or empty. So the supplier prefers to fill it, and combines parts from several customers that go through the same process. A part that needs a special cycle, at a temperature or in an atmosphere nobody else needs, pays for the whole cycle alone. For example, a ten-kilogram part that goes into a shared load with other parts is much cheaper than the same part in a cycle of its own. And note: large, complicated parts take up space in the furnace, even when their weight is small.

The first factor is furnace time. Normalizing and a simple temper are relatively short cycles. A full anneal is longer, because the cooling inside the furnace is slow. A vacuum furnace is expensive equipment, so a cycle in it usually costs more too. On the other hand, it saves cleaning and gives a clean surface. In carburizing, the depth grows with the square root of time. In other words, to double the case depth you need about four times the time. A higher temperature uses more energy and wears out the furnace. That is why hardening high-speed steel, at around 1,200 °C, costs more. Gas nitriding lasts tens of hours, so it is expensive too, even though the temperature is low. Every extra step is an extra cycle: a double or triple temper, sub-zero treatment, or stress relieving before final machining. And a thick part needs more time, until the core reaches temperature as well.

A large part of the cost is not in the furnace at all, but in the work around it. Before the treatment, oil and dirt are washed off. And after it, the scale is cleaned off, by blasting or with chemicals. The parts are loaded on fixtures, and sometimes hung one by one to prevent distortion. That is manual work. Areas that have to stay soft are covered with copper plating or with paste, and the cover is removed afterwards. A part that has distorted needs straightening as well, and sometimes another temper after the straightening. And after hardening, all machining is slower and more expensive: grinding instead of milling, and the tools wear quickly.

The testing and the documentation are part of the price too: hardness tests, test coupons, metallography and certificates. A supplier that works to AMS 2750 and holds Nadcap accreditation pays for calibrations, surveys and audits, and that goes into the price. So it is not worth requiring a 3.2 certificate and destructive tests for a part that does not need them. But when the part is critical, these are exactly the tests you must not save on.

The cheapest way to save is at the design stage. If a medium hardness is needed, you can sometimes buy pre-hardened steel, such as 4140 at about 28 to 32 HRC, and skip the heat treatment altogether. If only one surface has to be hard, induction hardening of that area is cheaper than carburizing the whole part. A steel with suitable hardenability allows quenching in oil or in gas instead of in water, with less distortion and fewer rejects. Set the hardness tolerance as wide as possible, and the case depth only as deep as the function requires. Every tenth of a millimeter of depth costs furnace time. And choose a process that the local suppliers know well. A rare process costs more, and it is also harder to find a supplier for it. And design the part for heat treatment, as we saw in chapter 21: uniform thickness, radii, and machining allowance in the right places.

You can save in purchasing too. Combine small orders into one lot, so as not to pay the minimum charge again and again. Talk to the supplier before the drawing is frozen. Sometimes a small change lets them use a standard cycle that is already running at their shop. Compare quotations on the same basis: the same specification, the same tests, the same certificates, and the same lead time. And ask for a quotation that includes everything: cleaning, straightening, tests and certificates, so that no extras turn up on the invoice. And remember transport and time as well: a part that waits a week for heat treatment holds up the whole project.

And what is really expensive? A reject at the end of the process. When a part is rejected after heat treatment, we have already paid for the material, for the machining and for the treatment. And sometimes it cannot be repaired. And a part that fails at the customer, because of a shallow case or a crack, costs far more than any test we saved on. And time costs money too: a rejected part goes through the whole chain again, and the project waits.

And now, let's go over the whole course. We started with the basics: the structure of iron, the iron-carbon diagram, and the time and cooling diagrams that explain when martensite forms. We saw how to anneal, normalize and relieve stresses, and how to harden: heating to austenite, quenching, and tempering. We learned to harden only the surface: by induction and flame, by carburizing, by nitriding, by laser and by boriding. We went through the families of materials: tool steels, stainless steels, aluminum, titanium, beryllium copper, superalloys and cast iron. And we saw that every material needs its own treatment, and that there is no single recipe that suits all metals. And in the practical part we talked about furnaces and atmospheres, about distortion and cracking, about testing, and about how to write the requirement on the drawing. The main message of the course: heat treatment changes the internal structure, and that structure cannot be seen by eye. So you plan it in advance, write it clearly, and check that it really happened.

Thank you for being with us throughout the course. Good luck in the shop, in design and at the drawing board.
